# Chapter 22: The Turn

I did not get down. I want that on the record, because Nell has said since that it was the single stupidest thing I did all week, and she's counting the week I let a man put a kettle on in front of me for five years.

I turned. That's all. I turned toward the headlights, the way you turn toward your name.

The rifle made a flat, ordinary crack, smaller than I'd have thought, and Hollis Dunn hit me in the chest with his whole body.

He was sixty-eight and he had a bad hip and he came off that stone bench like a man leaving a burning building. We went down together on the wet rock. The thermos went over. Something went past the place where my head had been with a sound like a zipper opening in the air, and behind us a chunk of the breakwater burst into white dust.

"Stay *down,*" said Hollis, into my ear, almost cross. "You never listen. I've said it for twenty years."

Then he made a small surprised noise, the noise of a man who has been tapped on the shoulder, and a warm wet weight spread across my collar that wasn't rain.

A second shot. A third, from a different place, louder, and I knew that one: Nell's service pistol, firing from the dark on the landward side, and then Kessler's, a half beat behind. Somebody shouted my name. Somebody shouted *police,* which seemed a foolish thing to bother with.

I got my head up. The figure at the water's edge had gone down on one knee. Beyond him, the dark rental sedan had pulled onto the shoulder at an angle, and the flood that had cut me off was sheeting around its front wheels, foam piling on the bumper. He'd come in off the old quarry track behind Nell's Ford, between her and me, and the tide had taken the road out behind him the way it had taken it out behind me. Two people, one rifle, and the whole North Atlantic closing the door.

"Reyes," I said.

"Yes." Hollis's voice had gone small. "Desmond does hate a loose end. He gets that from me."

The man on his knee stood up. Even at a hundred yards I knew the head, the smooth shine of it under the headlights, the rimless glasses. Desmond Reyes, vice president, claims, who had called me at 9:12 on a Friday night and said *it's an easy one, Theo,* in the voice of a man handing you a coat.

He worked the bolt. He was a careful man. He had always been careful. He lifted the rifle again.

Nell's pistol cracked twice. The windshield of the sedan went white. Reyes flinched like a man who'd been splashed, and then, for the first time, he looked afraid, and I understood that he had never in his life stood anywhere he couldn't leave. He ran for the car. The water was past the hubcaps. He got the door open and the sea came in with him.

I'd have liked to watch that. I didn't. Hollis had rolled off me, and he was sliding.

---

The breakwater at the end of the Neck road is granite blocks the size of kitchen tables, laid in a long shallow curve, and on a storm tide the top row is the last dry thing for a mile. The sea had been getting up onto it in sheets. When Hollis rolled, his coat caught the wash, and the wash did what it does, which is take hold quietly and then not let go.

He went in between two blocks. He didn't shout. I'd have heard that. He went down into the gap with his white hair spread out and one arm reaching up, the good arm, as if for a banister.

I had the coat in my hand before I knew I'd moved. That's the truth, and I haven't improved it for the telling. Fingers closed on the borrowed wool at the collar, the stolen coat from a fourth-floor hospital closet, and the sea pulled and I was lying flat on granite with my arm straight down a crack in the world, holding a man by the neck of a coat.

He came up with his face. It was gray. Water ran out of his mouth.

"Let go," he said.

"No."

"Theo. Be sensible." He coughed. "You've the confession. Nell has it on a wire. You don't need me *breathing.* Nobody would say—" The next wave came through the gap and took the rest of the sentence. When it drew back he was lower. The coat was filling. It weighed what a man weighs, and then what a man and a sea weigh, and I felt my shoulder start to go.

I want to tell you what I thought. I'd like to say I thought of Mara's face, or of Lydia, or the cannery watchman whose name I'd learned from a pencil ledger, or Tarbell in his skiff with his hand trailing in the water he'd been afraid of all his life. I thought of all of them. It was a very fast, very clear list, like a man reading a claims schedule.

And under the list I thought something I'm less proud of, because it was so simple. *It would be so easy. The sea would do it. Nobody would ever know I'd let it.*

He saw it. He had always been able to see what I was thinking, that was the thing he had; he'd been reading my face since I was twenty-six. His own face, half-drowned and white under the swollen eye, did something I hadn't seen it do. It stopped arranging itself. It was old and frightened and waiting.

"Nobody would know," he said, almost wonderingly. "Dear boy. Nobody's here."

"I'm here," I said.

That's all I had. I've turned it over a hundred times and it's still all I had. A man had told me once that every loss was a story somebody was telling you, and I'd spent five years believing it, and Hollis had been telling mine the whole time, start to finish, in a warm voice. If I let go, that was the end of the story, the one *he'd* write: Hollis Dunn, lost to the sea, the last word his, a clean page. He'd have the ending he'd always arranged for everyone else.

I wasn't going to give him an accident.

"Kessler!" I screamed. "*Kessler!*"

I got my other arm round his chest. I got a knee up. The next wave came in over both of us, green and heavy and so cold it felt like being hit, and I held on to a murderer in the dark with my teeth shut and thought, quite calmly, *this is what it was like for her.* Then a hand took me by the belt, and another took the coat, and Trooper Kessler, with his bruised rib and his bad language, was hauling on a rope he'd run round a bollard, and the two of us came up out of the crack like a cork out of a bottle.

We lay on the granite. Hollis was a long way from dead. He lay on his side and vomited seawater and breathed in great ragged whoops, and the blood from his shoulder ran out pink across the stone and into the rain.

Nell came up out of the dark at a run, soaked to the thighs, her gun still in her hand. She looked at me, and at him, and at Kessler, and I saw her do the count.

"Mr. Dunn," she said, once she had her breath, "I'm placing you under arrest for the murder of Alan Farrow, and I'll do the rest at the hospital, because you're bleeding on my evidence."

"Detective," said Hollis, faintly. "I'm so sorry about the mess."

She knelt and turned him, and put the cuffs on him as gently as I've ever seen a person cuff a person, one thin wrist and then the other, and when she'd done it she kept her hand a moment on his sleeve.

"You pulled him out," she said to me, not turning her head.

"Yes."

"I'd have let him go." She said it flatly. "I'm not saying that to be hard. I'm saying I'd have let him go, and I'd have told myself it was the sea. And then I'd have had to be that person." She stood. "You did the dumb thing. Don't ever do it again."

"I won't."

"Good," said Nell, and her voice cracked straight across the middle, and she turned and walked a few steps into the dark and stood there with her back to us, in the rain, for as long as it took.

---

Reyes was on the roof of the sedan.

They'd got to him by the time the water was at the doors, a team out of Rockland in a high-wheeled truck with a winch, and a state police boat that had no business out on that sea and went anyway. He sat with his knees drawn up on the roof, the rifle gone, one lens of his glasses cracked, looking out at all that water with the expression of a man consulting a spreadsheet that has stopped making sense. He did not resist. He did not say anything to anyone until they had him in a blanket in the back of the truck.

Then he said to Obert, who'd come out with the second vehicle and who was, apparently, the sort of man who turned up wherever there was paper: "I'd like to speak to counsel. And I want it noted I never touched that man. I was invited."

"By whom?" said Obert.

Reyes turned his head. Hollis was being lifted into the ambulance a hundred feet off, strapped flat, the white hair stuck to his skull. And Hollis, who could not possibly have heard him, who was half drowned and shot and in handcuffs, rolled his head on the stretcher and found Reyes across the dark and gave him a small, courteous, final nod, like a man at a dinner party leaving early.

It went through Reyes like current. I watched it. I think that was the moment he understood that he was not the one telling this story either.

---

We did the rest in order. Nell insisted, and so did Sayer, who arrived at the Rockland barracks at ten in a charcoal suit with the red-threaded bag over her arm and looked at me once, wrapped in a gray blanket and holding a paper cup, and said, "I did say be careful."

"I was careful."

"You were wet," said Ellen Sayer. "It isn't the same." And she sat down beside me and put her hand, briefly, flat on the back of mine.

Chain of custody was a thing I learned that morning to love. The coat came off Hollis in the ambulance with a paramedic and a trooper both watching, and went into a bag, and the bag was sealed and signed and photographed. At the barracks Obert laid it on a table under the camera with Nell and Sayer on either side and me, by agreement, at the end, not touching anything, and he cut the seams one at a time, calling out each thing as it came.

"Inner left pocket. One white envelope, sealed with a wax-paper wrapper, damp at the corners. Addressed in blue felt-tip: *Theo.* Handwriting consistent with exemplars on file, subject to examination. A small wave drawn under the name."

I made a sound. Sayer's hand tightened.

"Inner right pocket. One cloth-bound book, green, four by six, rubber band, wet. Ledger form."

He did not open either thing then. That was Sayer's condition, and it was a good one. He photographed the envelope front and back, and the green book from every side, and bagged them separately, and wrote the time on the bags: 11:47 a.m. A forensic document examiner would take the book to Augusta that afternoon. The envelope, Sayer said, in the voice she used for the final point, would be opened by a person with a pair of gloves and a camera, in a room, with Mr. Brandt present or not present as he chose, and then copied, and then, the original having been examined and the examiner's report in hand, it would be his.

"It's hers," I said.

"It's evidence first," said Nell, gently. "It'll be hers again. I promise you that, Theo. I'll bring it to you myself."

I thought of the five years it had spent in a coat. I said: "Open it now."

They looked at each other.

"Not for me," I said. "For the record. I'd like to be told what it says by somebody who'll read it right. And then I'll read it later, when I'm somewhere I can fall over."

Nell took the bag and went and got a pair of gloves.

---

I won't give you all of it. Some of it belongs to the two people it was written between, and one of them is dead and the other is still, I think, working out what he's been left.

She'd written it on the fourteenth of October, in the morning, at our kitchen table. Obert noted a ring in the lower right corner, a mug ring, coffee. I'd been wrong about the other letter's stain from the first night: the crystals on that one were salt, but not the sea's. They were a woman's, from a kitchen, from crying over a page she hadn't yet decided to send.

The letter is short. She wrote it fast, in the blue felt-tip, the one she used for grocery lists. She said she was meeting Hollis at the breakwater at four. She said she'd thought about it all night and decided that of everybody in the world, he was the one person she could ask to read the files with her, because he'd taught us both what a lie looks like, and he'd know. She said she wasn't afraid. She said she was only afraid of one thing, which was that Theo would find out that she'd gone to someone and been wrong about them.

*If you're reading this, I got something wrong,* Nell read, and stopped, and began again. *And if I did, then I need you to know what I was wrong about. I wasn't wrong to tell you. I told you because you're my husband and I'd rather be in the dark with you than in the light on my own. And you were right to tell someone. I know you did. You were never able to carry a thing like that by yourself, and that isn't a flaw, my love. It's the best thing about you. It's why I married you. You always, always go and find somebody to hold the other end.*

She stopped there, for a moment.

*If it was him,* she'd written, *please don't spend the rest of your life on that. He was the person you trusted most in the world because I trusted him too, and he was very, very good at it. I was in the room when he was good at it. It wasn't your fault. It was a gift, and it was a lie, and it fooled both of us.*

*The tide's slack at four. I'll tell you the rest tonight.*

*Mara.*

And under it, the little wave.

Nell put the letter down on the table. Nobody said anything. Outside the barracks windows it was getting light, a thin, scoured, washed gray over the lot, and a gull was walking up and down the roof of a cruiser with its hands behind its back, if gulls have hands, and in the corner the coffee machine clicked and dispensed a brown liquid nobody could account for.

"I'll take that coffee now," I said, and the sound I made then was half a laugh, and half the other thing, and Sayer put an arm round me as if I'd been her own, and Nell stood at the window with her fist against her mouth.

---

The green book was read on Tuesday, under the camera, with Sayer there and a document examiner from Augusta with a magnifying visor and a patient manner.

It was nothing fancy. A claims man's ledger, kept the way he'd kept everything, in a small clear schoolmaster's hand. A column of initials. A column of dates. A column of sums, and beside each sum, a fraction in pencil, and the fraction was always thirty. It ran back nine years. P.D. R.A. L.F. M.B. And in a different ink, still blue-black, A.F., for last Thursday.

Five lines. The examiner counted them aloud, twice, as if there might be a mistake in the counting.

"There's a sixth," said Nell.

It was on the last ruled line, and I knew what it said before the examiner turned the book to the light, the way you know the shape of a thing in a room you've lived in. He'd dated it Monday, the fifth of October, five years gone: the very day he told me he'd sat up with me till ten and walked me to the car.

*T.B.*

There was no sum beside it. Where the sum should have been he'd written a single word and struck it through, and written it again, and struck that through too, and finally, in smaller letters, tucked up against the margin as if he hoped nobody would ever read the margin, put the word *hold.*

Below it, in the very same blue-black, in pencil, years later and much fainter:

*D.R. — approves. Not yet. Ask me when he's ready.*

I'd like to say it surprised me. It didn't, quite. I think some small cold part of me had been reading that column for five years, in every Tuesday, in every pot of tea. *There were always six of us,* he'd said once to the sea. I'd thought he meant a ring. I'd thought it meant six conspirators. It meant six names. Five dead, and one he'd kept for last, and every Tuesday, sitting across from me with his sleeves rolled, he'd been deciding whether he could stand to write the sum.

He couldn't. That's the only mercy in the whole thing, and I'll thank you not to call it one.

Desmond Reyes, of course, could. That's how you find your sixth member. The one who wants the line filled in.

---

It was a long autumn, and I won't linger.

Voss walked out of Rockland General in the first week of November with a cane he claimed was for effect, and testified in a suit and tie. Hal Rouse, in his navy cardigan, gave the grand jury a whisper that forced the court reporter to lean forward in her chair and then, forty minutes in, to take off her shoes and sit on the floor. He told them everything. He had looked away for eleven years, and he said so in plain words, and when he stepped down he found me in the corridor and said, "I should have stopped you coming back for Mara's file," and I said, "You told me to be careful who I told," and he said, "That's the sentence I'll hear on my deathbed," and I said, "No. You'll hear her."

Cora Farrow got her father's money in the end, after a judge in Augusta decided what the law said about a man who'd bought his daughter a policy with a murderer's blessing. She gave most of it away. She came to Portland in December and stood on the stairs of my office over the chandlery with a bucket in her hand, because she'd heard the roof leaked, and said, "I don't know what I'm doing here," and I said, "Nobody does," and gave her tea. Not Yorkshire Gold. Something worse, from a tin Gus left. She drank it anyway.

Ellen Sayer sent a bill, a small one. Nell Castellano sent a photograph, in a plain envelope, with nothing written on the back: two women at a folding table in a church hall, one in a blue cardigan with her hand up against a camera, laughing, a plate of lemon bars between them. Six years old. Mara, at thirty-six, with her hair coming down.

I framed it. It stands on the radiator in the office, in the leak's blast radius, which Mara would have found funny.

Hollis Dunn, in a courtroom in Rockland in March, pleaded guilty to five counts of murder and wouldn't look at me once.

He asked for tea in the holding cell, and was told no sugar was available, and said it was no matter, he took none.

---

After Hollis pleaded guilty in March, I went to the cemetery with the letter in my pocket in its plastic sleeve, the copy, and the sky the color of a wet slate and nothing left in it to drop.

It's a plain stone. I chose it in a fog. There's her name, and the two dates, and under them the only words I could manage in that state, which were *Mara Brandt, who went to find out.* It's been five years and I still can't decide if that was a good epitaph, or a confession of what I'd done.

I stood there a while. Then I knelt, which I hadn't expected to do, and took a thing from my coat pocket.

It's small. It's bronze, the size of a half-dollar, stamped with a triangle in a circle. They hand them out in a church basement, the same basement where a warm hand had rested on my coat while I told the room I was grateful. I've carried that one eighteen hundred and thirty days. A man who is now in a cell once slipped it into my palm.

I'd meant to leave the coin. I wasn't sure why. To give her something back, perhaps, or to give *him* back to nobody. But I turned it over in my fingers, with the triangle going dull and warm against my thumb, and it was still mine. Nobody had written that. Nobody had arranged it. A man had held my coat and told me what to do, and I'd stood up every morning for eighteen hundred and thirty days and done it, and it had been me doing it, not him, not once, not at any point.

I put it back in my pocket.

"Every loss is a story somebody is telling you," I said to her. It was very quiet. "He said that. I used to think it was a rule. It's only a warning. It means check who's telling it."

The wind moved in the grass.

"So here's mine," I said. "You went to find out. I told someone, and it was the wrong someone, and you were right to love me anyway. And the water didn't take you. Somebody put you in it. And the tide goes out, Mara, and then it comes in, and it doesn't care, and it isn't telling anyone anything. It's only the sea."

I stayed until the light changed. When I got up, my knees hurt, which I took as an excellent sign. Then I went home.
