# Chapter 19: Where It Doesn't Fit the Water

I didn't sleep. I sat beside his bed until a nurse came at midnight and said, kindly, that Mr. Dunn needed rest and so, frankly, did I. Then I walked back to the Anchor in the dark, the causeway lights strung across the water like a necklace somebody had dropped.

Room six. The chain on. The radiator ticking.

I did what he taught me. I took out the notebook, a clean page, and drew a line down the middle.

*Every loss is a story somebody is telling you. Find where it doesn't fit the water.*

On the left I wrote what I knew. On the right, what I'd been told. For twenty years I'd done this for strangers, and it had never felt like holding a knife.

**The latch.** Left: Nell told me about the hook-and-eye inside the boathouse door, on the Saturday, in the cold light of that yard. She told nobody else. Right: I told Hollis the door was *locked from inside*. That's all I said. I'd written his answer in the margin that night, because I write down what people say to me: *That latch sticks in damp, always did.*

Not *the door*. The latch. A man who thought the door was bolted, or barred, or keyed, would have said *the door sticks.*

**9:12.** Left: Nell told me the minute Reyes called, after dinner, when Hollis was at his sink. Right: Hollis said "nine-twelve" on the Sunday, in his study, and when I looked at him he said Reyes had rung him Friday. Reyes had rung him Friday. I had checked. Reyes could have mentioned it. Reyes could have mentioned anything.

**The coat.** Tarbell told me a man took his coat off and put it back on in the harbor office, a talker, a kind one. Gray waxed cotton hangs on a hook in the hall at Quarry Lane. It has hung there my entire adult life. Every man in Maine over sixty owns one.

**The tea.** A used bag in a dead man's bin, with a string and a paper tag. Alan Farrow, by his housekeeper's account in every statement I'd seen, drank whisky and coffee. I put down my pen and thought about a red box in a kitchen in Port Carrow, and a paper sleeve in a coat pocket, and a man who said *hotel tea is a crime* and carried his own.

**The lamp.** Mara's ledger photograph, a green glass shade out of focus at the top edge. *You'll know the chair.* I knew the chair. I'd sat in it. I'd been looking for the lamp in the wrong office.

**The kettle.** A note, rising. Wet wool. I'd felt it twice, in my own kitchen on the fourteenth and in his, and each time I had told myself that it was the smell of being looked after.

I looked at the page.

There is a thing that happens when you stack enough small coincidences. Each one is nothing. A man gets lucky, a man gets unlucky, people say things. Then you stop adding and start *seeing*. The whole sheet rearranges itself into a shape that was there from the beginning, only now it has a face.

I turned the page over, face down.

Then I turned it back and added one more line, at the bottom, in letters so small I could hardly read them.

*There you are.*

A man in a doorway could have said it. A man on a stranger's voicemail could have said it. I had said it myself, to a thousand people, when they were late.

And Hollis was in a hospital bed. That was the thing I kept going back to. At 5:51 Friday afternoon, when Barrett Voss was shot on a camp road in Friendship, Hollis Dunn had been under a blanket with a policeman outside his door and eleven stitches in his scalp. He could not have stood in those trees. Whatever else this was, a man who is bleeding in a hall at noon doesn't fire a gun at six.

*Then there's more than one of them,* the part of me that was still an adjuster said. *You knew that. The ledger says six.*

The other part said: *Or there is only one, and he is in a hospital, and the one who shot Voss is a man who wanted what Voss had. And you are reading a pattern into a kind old man because you are tired, and drunk on being afraid, and you have wanted for five years to find somebody to hate.*

I looked at that for a long time. It was a good argument. It was exactly the argument a man would make who needed to go on being loved.

At ten past one I picked up the phone and called Nell.

---

"Are you drunk?" she said.

"No."

"Then it's a bad hour." I heard a lamp click. "Go ahead."

"When you told me about the latch on the boathouse door. Did you tell anyone else?"

A short silence.

"No."

"Not Ouellette. Not Hollis, not in passing. Not in the hospital."

"Theo. I told you at the scene because you were the only one in the county who might know how a man could do that to himself with his own door. I didn't tell the lieutenant until Monday. Why?"

"I'm checking my notes."

"Don't do that to me."

"I'm checking my notes," I said, "and I want to ask about the tea."

She was quiet again, and when she spoke her voice had changed.

"Aldous," she said. "I got to her yesterday. Seventy-one, tough, a long day. The Colonel, she calls him. He didn't keep tea in the house. Never. 'A man who drinks tea,' she says, 'has something to hide.' He hated it. She emptied that boathouse bin herself on Wednesday, the seventh, because it stank. The bag came after that." A pause. "The tag says Yorkshire Gold. Red box. Half of Maine drinks it."

"And the receipt."

"You're building something."

"I'm trying to knock it down."

"Hanley's. Ten-forty on the sixth. Fifty yards of forty-pound braided line, one brass eye hook. Cash." I heard her breathe. "The clerk is nineteen and remembers nothing. Everybody who owns a boat buys line there. Everybody who owns a door buys a hook."

"Fifty yards."

"Don't say it."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said it so loudly I can hear it from here," Nell said. "Go to sleep. The diary opens at nine at the barracks, with Sayer, in a room with a camera. I'd like you to come rested. I'm going to watch your face, and I want what I see to mean something."

I said I would try.

I lay down in my clothes. The harbor lights went in and out through a gap in the curtain, and the chain on the door shone, and I thought, *at least I know what I'll see when I close my eyes.*

I was wrong. I slept for four hours without a dream, like a man who has been shut in a dark room and told to wait.

---

They opened it on a steel table under a ceiling camera, at the state police barracks in Rockland, at nine-oh-six on Saturday morning.

It was a black Letts desk diary with a cracked spine, one page a day. Five years ago in tired gold. Dill had carried it from the cottage in a bag with a numbered seal, and Nell cut the seal while a trooper filmed. Ellen Sayer sat beside me in a charcoal suit with her red-stitched bag between her feet. She had told me on the stairs, in a low voice, to look at nothing but the page. "If you remember something, don't say it. Write it down. Tell me. Then we'll talk."

Nell wore gloves. She turned the pages slowly, by the corners, with a flat steel spatula.

Hollis's handwriting. I'd have known it blind. The long tails, the way he crossed his sevens like a European. *Dentist. Sailing club AGM. Rotary lunch. Call B. about the roof.* A year of a retired man's life, written in a black ballpoint that slowly ran out and was replaced by another.

Nell stopped at the first week of October.

"Fourth," she said. "Sunday."

*Church. Bread. Quiet day. Nobody rang.*

I read it twice.

"Fifth. Monday."

It was a small neat entry. It sat in the middle of the page in a different ink, and it took me a moment to know why my chest had gone tight, until I saw it was bluer, blue-black, the sort that dries to a shine.

*T. at 7. Troubled, would not say why. Tea. Sat with me until 10. Sober, thank God. Walked him to the car.*

Under it, in the same blue-black, cramped to fit:

*Whatever it is, it will keep till he is ready.*

Nobody said anything. The camera hummed.

"That's the night Pryor says you were at his house," said Nell, very flat.

"Yes."

"If it's true, you were here. In Hollis's kitchen. From seven to ten." She looked at me. "Pryor's handwritten statement says he saw you at eight."

"Then one of them is wrong."

"Or both of them are telling a story," said Sayer. She had not moved. "A diary is not an alibi, Detective. It's a man's account of his own evening in his own hand. I would like it very much to be true. I'd like to know when that ink was put on that page."

"It's blue-black," Nell said. "The rest of the week is ballpoint."

"Then I'd like to know when it was written."

Nell turned the pages with the spatula, three, four, five days, and I could see it. On the sixth, *Nothing. Fog.* On the seventh, *Wrote to Alan.* In black. Then the eighth, the ninth. Then the fourteenth.

She didn't read it aloud.

I read it from where I sat.

*Breakwater. Rain. Mara.*

Nothing else. No time. Nothing about the day, or how he had known. I looked at it and I saw a man sitting in a quiet kitchen with a pen. I saw it so clearly it was like being struck.

"It's blue-black," I said.

"Yes."

"The fifth. The fourteenth. The rest is ballpoint."

"Yes."

"The signature on page seven. My signature. In the Lydia file." I could hear my own voice, very careful. "The ink on that, Mara wrote in the margin of her copy. *Blue-black, not his.* I never use it. I use a black gel pen. I've used one for twenty years."

"I know," Nell said. "I have the copy."

Nobody breathed.

"That is a lab matter. And it could be any fountain pen in Maine. A man has been through something and writes in a diary with whatever is to hand." Sayer paused. "Mr. Brandt, I'm going to say this once so you hear it from me. There is a version of this morning where a decent old man was kind to you in the worst week of your life, wrote it down, and was attacked in his own hall by someone who wanted what he knew. I would like you to be able to defend that version in front of a jury. If you cannot, they will not let you defend any other."

"I know."

"Do you?"

I looked at the line again. *Whatever it is, it will keep till he is ready.*

"He says," I said slowly, "that I wouldn't say why."

"Yes."

"I'd have told him. If I'd come to that kitchen that night, I'd have told him everything. He was the first person I ever told things."

"Perhaps you didn't," said Sayer. "Perhaps you'd lost your nerve."

"I didn't lose it with Rouse."

I stopped. They were both looking at me.

"That's all," I said. "I didn't lose it with Rouse."

---

The cutter lay at her mooring in the cove below Quarry Lane, white hull, tan mainsail furled along the boom, the name in blue script on the transom. *Second Opinion.* A tide-line of green weed on her waterline and a gull on the pulpit. I'd slept on her four summers ago. I had been sick over her rail once, the first year, and he had held the back of my collar and said, "Better out than in, dear boy," and rinsed the deck himself afterward.

Sayer had asked him that morning, at the hospital, whether Nell might look aboard. He had said, through the dressing on his face and a plastic cup of apple juice, "Take whatever you need. The key's with Theo. I've nothing to hide on a boat," and Sayer had written down the sentence word for word.

Nell went down the companionway first. Dill took photographs from the cockpit. I stood in the hatch with my hands in my pockets.

It was neat. It was the neatest boat I'd ever been on. Brass polished, charts rolled, a row of paperbacks held in by a bungee. The cabin smelled of diesel and wet wool.

I stood in the smell and I did not fall down.

"Logbooks," said Nell.

They were on a shelf above the chart table, bound, a spine for each year, in a row of dark green cloth. She ran a gloved finger along them and stopped.

"There's a gap."

Five years ago. Between the book before it and the book after, an empty slot with a faint paler line in the varnish where the spine of a book had kept the sun off.

"He'll say he lost it," said Dill, from above.

"He'll say it's at the cottage," said Nell. "Which it may be." She took down this year's volume with two fingers and let it fall open on her palm. She was quiet for some time.

"Come and look."

October. Six pages of a ruled log. Each one, a neat boat-owner's record of a day: *Wind NW 12. Beat out of the cove. Nothing in the pots.* Then the sixth, the seventh, the eighth, the ninth. Four pages. Where each of them had been there was a clean straight edge close to the gutter, a hair's breadth of paper left, so exactly cut that I could have shaved with it.

"Razor," she said.

I'd seen a straight edge like that before. In a pink message binder, in the Atlantic archive, with a carbon where a carbon had been.

"Water gets into everything on a boat," said Dill, who was twenty-six and had been trying for ten minutes to be helpful.

"Yes," said Nell. "It does." She put the book into a bag. "Theo, don't touch the locker."

"I haven't."

"You were about to."

I was. A long cockpit locker, port side, under the seat. She knelt and lifted the lid. There was a coil of anchor rode. A fender, a bailer, a tin of marine varnish with a brush stuck to its rim. And in the corner, in a clear freezer bag with a zip, a spool of braided line in dull green, nearly full, and a loose brass eye hook, unopened, still in its blister pack.

"Hanley's," said Nell. She turned it over. There was a price sticker. "Same stock number. He'd have bought it on the sixth, too." She didn't look at me. "Or on the fifth, or in August, or whenever anyone buys a spool of line for a boat."

"Fifty yards. And a hook."

"A hook and eye is how you hang a hammock. A spool of line is how you do half the things on this boat." She stood up. Her knees cracked. "If I put this in front of a prosecutor, she'll show me a photograph of Hollis Dunn's boat and ask me what in it is unusual. And I'll say, *the pages are missing*, and she'll say, *water*, and I'll say, *the book from five years ago*, and she'll say, *an old man's attic*. And she'll be right. You can't convict a man for owning a boat."

"I know."

"I know you know." She put the bag down on the chart table and looked at me. "Say it."

"I can't."

"Why?"

"Because he was in a hospital bed when Voss was shot."

"Yes."

"Because Reyes came to the ward and asked for him by name."

"Yes."

"Because somebody put him in that hall. Somebody hit him. He has a bruise the size of a plate on his chest, Nell. I watched them cut the cardigan."

"Yes," she said, with her mouth hardly moving. "I watched too."

We stood there, the two of us, in that clean small cabin. Above us, the gull shifted on the pulpit.

"I don't have an explanation," I said. "I have a list. It isn't proof. It's the shape a loss makes when the story is wrong. That's all it is. I could stand in front of Sayer's jury and be torn to pieces."

"Then you need something that isn't a list."

"Yes."

"Something that only he could know."

"Yes."

"And you can't get it by asking, because he's smarter than both of us, and he's been teaching you how to question people since before you could drive."

I looked at the logbook through the plastic. The clean cut edges.

"I can get it," I said, "by lying to him."

Nell looked at me for a long moment.

"We'll need Sayer," she said.

---

I went to the hospital alone at five, with Nell's officer on the door and Sayer's number in my pocket and a paper cup of tea I had bought in the lobby, because I'd decided on the stairs that I would not refuse one.

He was sitting up. The swelling on his eye had come down a little and turned the yellow-green of an old bruise, and the white of his hair, brushed, made him look like a painting of a patriarch who has been wronged. He saw me and put down a paperback. A Patrick O'Brian. He'd read it, I knew, twelve times.

"Theo."

"Hollis."

"Sit, sit. You've been in the wars. Sayer says they opened my old diary." He smiled, and winced, and put a finger to the dressing. "I do hope it helped, dear. I was ashamed to give it to you in that state. My writing's gone to pot."

"It put me in your kitchen," I said. "On the fifth. From seven to ten."

"Where else would you be?"

I sat down and held the tea in both hands.

"Do you remember what we talked about?" he said. He said it lightly, the way he'd say *did you sleep?* "I've turned it over. I've thought, if I could only recall the *words.* But you wouldn't say. You sat there like a lamp with the wick down. I was so afraid you'd start drinking again."

I looked at him.

A man who had known me for twenty years was watching my face with the whole attention of his mild blue eyes, and under it, at a depth I could never have found before that night, something else was watching. Waiting. A fisherman's patience. The stillness of a man who has dropped a line and sits looking at the water, not at the float.

*Have you remembered? Have you?*

"No," I said. "I haven't remembered a word of it."

It was the first lie I had ever told him that I knew was a lie as I said it.

The corner of his mouth went soft. His whole face eased, like a clenched hand letting go.

"That's a mercy," said Hollis. He reached across the blanket and covered my hand. "That's a mercy, dear boy. Some things are better left."

His hand was warm. It lay over mine as it had on a hundred church-basement chairs, and I did not pull away. I sat in that amber light, holding a paper cup of tea I did not drink, and I thought, with great clarity, as though it were a sentence written on the wall: *he is relieved.*

A man who had loved me and been kind and wanted only my good would not have been relieved.

"I'll come tomorrow," I said.

"Do. Bring the paper. And, Theo."

"Yes."

"Eat something. You look like a ghost."

I said I would. At the door I looked back. He had picked up his book again, and found his place with one finger, and for a moment, with his head bent in the lamplight and his bruised face to the page, he looked as old and gentle as anyone I had ever loved.

Then he turned a page without reading it.

In the corridor I found Nell's number with my thumb, and I stood against the wall until I could trust my hands. I didn't call her. Instead I took out my notebook, and under the last line on the right-hand side, *There you are*, I wrote, *He was relieved*, and then, because I did not want to be a man who could stop there, I wrote what I'd have to do tomorrow.

*Give him one thing nobody else has.*

*A name. A place. A time.*

*And then wait to see who comes.*
