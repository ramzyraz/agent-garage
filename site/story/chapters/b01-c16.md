# Chapter 16: Stay With Me

I said his name again. The breathing stopped long enough for me to think the line had gone dead. Then it came back, wet and shallow.

The door to my room stood open. I didn't remember taking the chain off.

I stopped on the landing and put his call on hold. My thumb missed the nine the first time.

The dispatcher asked for the address of the emergency.

"Quarry Lane. Hollis Dunn's cottage. Port Carrow. I don't know the number. He's on the telephone. Something's happened to him."

"Are you at the property?"

"At the Anchor Inn. He called me. Someone knocked. He opened the door. I heard him fall."

"Did you hear anyone else?"

"No."

She asked whether he was breathing. I told her what I had heard, and she asked for my number, then his. For a moment I couldn't find it. I knew the number well enough to dial in the dark. On the screen, among the other numbers, it looked unfamiliar.

"We've located the address," she said. "Police and medical help are going. Stay where you are. Do not enter the house."

I gave her Nell's name. She said she'd pass it on.

When I returned to Hollis's call, I heard something dragging across the floor.

"They're coming," I said. "Hollis. Help's coming."

No answer.

I listened for another voice. Feet. A door. There was only his breath and, farther away, the small hard tick of his hall clock.

Nell rang while I was listening.

"I'm coming to the inn," she said. "Wait outside."

"He can't speak."

"I've spoken to dispatch. Go downstairs."

I left my room open. At the bottom of the stairs the clerk asked whether I needed a taxi.

"An ambulance," I said, and saw her reach for the desk phone.

"They've called it. Not here."

I couldn't finish explaining. I went out into the wind.

---

Nell's car stopped with the passenger door level with me. She reached across and opened it before I could get my hand under the handle.

I got in holding the phone against my ear.

"Still connected?"

I nodded.

"Put it on speaker."

For four seconds we drove with the sound of Hollis trying to breathe between us. Then Nell picked up her radio and told someone the call was still open. He was alive.

Alive. It was the first time anybody had said it.

A woman pushing a pram stopped at the curb while we passed. I wanted her to keep walking. I wanted all the ordinary things in Port Carrow to continue until we had him somewhere safe.

"What did he say before the knock?" Nell asked.

"He'd found his old diary. The one I asked him to look for. He said there was something we ought to— Then he stopped."

"Did he say what?"

"No."

She took the turn onto Quarry Lane without looking at me.

"Did you hear a conversation at the door?"

"He said yes. As a question. Nothing after that."

The cottage appeared between the trees. A police car stood across the gravel entrance, lights turning against the wet trunks. Beyond it, the front door was open.

I reached for the handle before Nell had stopped.

She caught my sleeve.

"Wait."

Officer Dill came out onto the step. He spoke into his shoulder radio, then looked toward us and held up an open hand.

The same officer who had put cuffs on me at Pryor's house. I knew his face before I knew what his gesture meant.

He lowered his hand and waved the ambulance in.

Nell released my sleeve.

"Behind me."

---

Hollis lay in the hall with one sock caught under his heel.

That was the first thing I saw. The sock was navy, worn white at the ball of the foot. He had come downstairs at the inn that morning with his shoes polished. Now one foot was bare, and the other pointed toward the open door at an angle that looked wrong until he moved it.

His head rested beside the telephone table. The receiver lay close to his mouth, its cord stretched across his shoulder. Blood had gone into his white hair and spread onto the floor beneath it.

A volunteer in a green jacket knelt beside him, holding a dressing against his head. She looked up at the ambulance crew.

"He's answering. Confused. No one else here."

Dill stood at the study doorway. Another officer came through from the back of the house.

I stayed against the wall while the paramedics put their bags down. There was barely room for them and Nell. I could see Hollis's chest moving beneath his cardigan, quicker than it should have.

"Hollis," I said.

His eyes shifted toward me.

"Theo."

It came out clearly this time. The relief was so sharp I had to put my hand against the wall.

"I'm here."

He tried to raise his head. The woman holding the dressing told him to stay still.

"Don't get up," I said. "You don't have to do anything."

He looked at my hand on the wallpaper. Then at my face. He seemed to be working out why I was there.

A paramedic asked him his name. He gave it. Asked where he was, he said home. Asked what had happened, he closed his eyes.

"The door," he said.

"Did you fall?"

"Someone."

His right hand moved across the floor. I crouched where there was space and let him find mine.

His fingers were cold, but his grip was strong enough to hurt.

I had held this hand outside meeting rooms, in hospital car parks, at the breakwater on the anniversary of Mara's death. Hollis always knew when to let go. This time he held on while they cut his cardigan open.

There was a bruise high on his chest. Blood had soaked the collar of his shirt. He looked down at the scissors.

"Good cardigan," he said.

Nobody laughed. I wanted to. I wanted that small complaint to make him himself again.

"I'll buy you one."

His eyes came back to mine.

"You called them?"

"Yes."

He shut his eyes and kept hold of my hand.

---

They asked me to move while they got the stretcher into the hall.

I backed into the study doorway. Dill stepped aside but stayed close enough to stop me going farther.

Hollis had been looking through boxes. Two stood open beside the desk, and a third had been pulled halfway from a shelf. Paper folders were stacked on the visitor's chair. The quilted cover was still over the typewriter.

There was a black diary on the blotter. Closed. Its year was stamped in faded gold on the spine.

The year Mara died.

I looked at it long enough for Dill to notice.

"That?" he asked.

"He told me he'd found an old desk diary. That's why he rang. I asked him to check something."

Nell turned from the stretcher.

"Don't touch it."

"I wasn't going to."

I said it too quickly. Dill glanced at my hands.

From where I stood I could see the lamp on the desk, the window above it, the carpet worn thin under the chair. Nothing looked disturbed in the way my office had been disturbed. The boxes might have been there for an hour or a week. I had never paid enough attention to what belonged where in Hollis's house. I came here and waited for him to tell me where to sit.

Nell asked Dill to photograph the room as it was.

In the hall, a paramedic was telling Hollis they would take him across to the hospital. Hollis tried to look past her.

"Theo?"

I went back to the doorway.

"I'm coming."

"The book."

"We can get it later."

He moved his head a fraction, trying to shake it. The paramedic steadied him.

"Mr. Dunn," Nell said, "is that the black diary on your desk?"

His eyes opened.

"Yes."

"May we take it for safekeeping?"

"Take it."

She told Dill to photograph it where it lay first and put it in a separate bag. I watched him go into the study. He didn't open the cover.

They lifted Hollis. His hand searched beside the stretcher, and I caught it for the few steps to the front door.

Outside, two neighbors stood at the edge of the lane. One had come out with a dish towel in her hands. She watched us pass as though we were taking away someone who had been there all her life.

At the ambulance, Hollis let go.

A little blood had dried in the creases of my fingers.

---

Nell followed the ambulance toward the causeway.

I kept watching its rear windows. Once it slowed, and my body went forward against the seat belt before our car had even begun to brake.

"They're taking the bend," Nell said.

I nodded as though I had understood that already.

The tide was out. Brown weed lay flat on either side of the causeway, and the yellow gauge post rose above the water. The place where Quill's Volvo had stood was empty. Traffic passed it without slowing.

Yesterday I had watched men get ready to open his car. This morning I had watched Hollis eat nothing while he waited for me to be released. I couldn't keep the days separate any more. Everything seemed to have happened in the same stretch of bad light.

"Did they find somebody?" I asked.

"No."

"Did anyone see a car?"

"They're asking."

"He wouldn't have opened the door if he thought—"

I stopped. Hollis opened his door to anyone. Lost delivery drivers. Children selling raffle tickets. People who needed to borrow a pump or wanted to know whether a rope was safe. I had once watched him spend forty minutes helping a stranger decide which tide to launch a dinghy on.

Nell kept her eyes on the ambulance.

"Tell me the call again. From when you answered."

I told her. When I reached the knock, she asked whether I was sure it was three blows.

"Yes."

"Any name? Did he sound pleased? Surprised?"

"Just yes."

"And the sound afterward?"

"I don't know. Something hitting the floor. Maybe him. Maybe something he knocked over."

She nodded once.

"We'll write that down while it's fresh. After they've seen him."

"You were going to question him."

"I still need to speak to him."

"About Tarbell and Quill."

"Yes."

I looked through the windscreen at the ambulance.

"Now somebody's done this to him."

Nell's hands stayed in the same place on the wheel.

"Let the doctors help him first."

I wanted more than that. I wanted her to withdraw what she'd said in the interview room. I wanted the blood on my hands to settle every question I'd made myself ask about Hollis.

She didn't offer it. I pressed my palms together and watched the road.

---

At the hospital they took him through doors I couldn't follow.

Nell gave my name to a nurse and told her I had been on the telephone when it happened. Then she went aside to speak to the ambulance crew.

The nurse asked whether I was family.

"No."

It hurt to say it.

"Does he have someone we should call?"

"His wife died. No children. There's a niece in New Hampshire. I don't have her number."

"We can ask him when he's ready."

She showed me where to wash my hands.

The blood came off pink, then clear. I kept the tap running after it was gone. In the mirror I looked like a man who had been awake for two nights, which I nearly had. One end of my shoelace hung lower than the other. I bent to fix it and couldn't remember how I'd threaded it wrong.

Back in the waiting area, Nell had found a quiet corner. She sat beside me and opened her notebook.

I went through the call a third time. She asked me to describe the breathing without imitating it, and the impact without deciding what had made it. Then she asked what I had told Hollis about the diary that morning.

"I asked him where I was on the fifth. If he had records. He said he'd look."

"Did he tell you he'd found anything before today?"

"No."

"And you didn't tell him what was in Alan's letter?"

"I didn't call him after we left Cora's. He rang me."

She wrote that down.

"Did you record his call?"

"No."

"We'll need the phone again. Just to preserve what we can. You can keep it with you for now."

I put it on the chair between us. The call had finally ended at the cottage when Dill lifted the receiver. Its duration was still there, all the minutes in which Hollis had been on the floor.

"Who knows about him?" I asked.

"I'll tell Sayer. I'll let Cora and Voss know enough to keep them careful."

"Voss was staying with her."

"He still is. An officer is going there for her statement."

"Ask him to stay after."

"I will."

She closed the notebook and stood.

"There's an officer coming here. No visitors until we know who they are."

"Including me?"

"I know who you are."

She said it tiredly. For once I didn't try to work out what she meant.

---

I had been sitting there nearly an hour when Sayer rang.

"Castellano told me," she said. "How is he?"

"They haven't said."

"And you?"

"Here."

She let that answer stand.

"You've given them an account of the call?"

"Yes."

"Good. Don't turn it into an account of anything you didn't hear."

"I know."

"I think you do. I also think you're tired enough to agree with anyone who offers you an explanation."

Across the waiting area, a man had fallen asleep with a folded coat behind his head. A little girl stood between his knees, carefully peeling the paper from a drinking straw. I watched her lay the unbroken strip across his wrist.

"He found the diary," I said.

"Have you seen it?"

"Closed. On his desk. The police took it with his permission."

"Then we'll ask about it when they can speak to him."

"It might put me somewhere. That evening."

"It might."

She wouldn't make it a promise. I was beginning to hear how much effort that cost people.

"Ellen. He paid you."

"Yes."

"If he can't—"

"I'm still your lawyer. Get some water. Call me when you know how he is."

After she hung up, I went to the machine and bought a bottle. I opened it but couldn't swallow more than a mouthful.

The doors opened. A nurse came out and called my name.

---

Hollis had stitches under a dressing at his hairline. His left eye was swelling, and there was a narrow strip of dried blood behind his ear. They had put him in a hospital gown. Without the cardigan and his careful collar, his shoulders looked smaller.

The nurse told me he was being kept for observation. He was awake, she said, but he needed rest. A police officer sat beyond the curtain, facing the entrance to the room.

Nell stood at the foot of the bed.

Hollis saw me and lifted one hand off the sheet.

"There he is."

I took the chair beside him. He looked at my coat, then my shoes, as though checking whether I'd brought everything.

"You look worse than I feel," he said.

"You haven't looked in a mirror."

He smiled with one side of his mouth.

Nell asked whether he felt able to answer a few questions. He said he'd try.

"Do you remember opening your front door?"

"Yes."

"Who was there?"

His gaze moved to the curtain.

"I don't know."

"A man? A woman?"

He rubbed his thumb against the sheet.

"I think a man."

"Did he say anything?"

"Might have. It's gone."

Nell waited. Hollis looked at me.

"I was talking to you."

"Yes."

"Then the door."

"Yes."

"Didn't get very far, did I?"

His hand went toward his head. I stopped it before his fingers reached the dressing, and he left his hand in mine.

"We're here," I said.

Nell asked whether anything was missing from the house. Hollis said he couldn't know. She told him about the diary, that it had been secured unopened and they would speak to him about it when he was rested.

"All right," he said.

"Is there anyone you're afraid of, Mr. Dunn? Anyone who's threatened you?"

He turned his head toward her. The movement made him wince.

"Should have been more careful."

"About what?"

He shut his eyes.

The nurse came through the curtain and looked at Nell. Nell put her notebook away.

"We'll stop there. If something comes back, tell the officer. Don't try to force it."

Hollis nodded without opening his eyes.

At the curtain Nell paused beside me.

"I'll be outside."

---

For a while we listened to the cart moving along the corridor.

Hollis's breathing had become ordinary again. I kept watching the hollow above his collarbone, the small lift and fall. Each time he took a breath, I stopped holding mine.

"You don't have to sit," he said.

"I know."

"Go and sleep."

"In a bit."

His eyes opened.

"You used to do that. Say in a bit."

"I probably still do."

"At the hospital. First time. Couldn't get you to leave her room."

I looked at the sheet beneath our hands.

I didn't remember Mara's room. I remembered a corridor, a trooper's boots, someone asking whether there was a person they could telephone. For years I had thought there was nothing before that except drink. Hollis had told me little things when I could bear them, giving me back a day in pieces: I'd sat, I'd slept, I'd let him drive.

Now I was in his room, waiting for him to remember.

"Don't talk," I said. "Just rest."

He closed his eyes. His grip loosened. I stayed because there was no one else to stay, and because an hour earlier I had heard him trying to say my name.

When his fingers moved again, I thought he was asleep.

"Theo."

I leaned toward him.

"Gray," he whispered.

I looked at the curtain. Nell was beyond it, speaking quietly to the officer.

"What was gray?"

Hollis's eyes opened. They stayed on the doorway.

"In the door. A shape."

"His coat?"

His fingers tightened around mine.

"Couldn't see. Here."

He lifted his free hand toward his mouth, then let it fall.

I reached for the call button. His grip held me.

"And the voice," he said.

I bent close enough to hear his breath.

"What about it?"

"He said my name."

His eyes came to mine, clear for a moment and frightened.

"Could hardly hear him. He was standing right in front of me. And I could hardly hear him at all."
