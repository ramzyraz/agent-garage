# Chapter 18: Behind the Garden Center

"Where are you?"

The line scraped. I thought he had hung up. Then the whisper came back, so close to the microphone that I could hear the wet in it.

*"You're standing in front of the liquor store with your hands in your pockets."*

I turned around.

Across the lot, past the parking structure and the dark hulk of the hospital laundry, a four-lane road ran east and west. On the far side was the liquor store with its red neon bottle. Beside it was a garden center, closed, its yard of potted evergreens lit by a single sodium lamp. Nothing moved there.

*"Don't look,"* said Rouse. *"Please. Don't look."*

"I'm looking at a tree."

*"I've been here an hour. I watched you come out. I wanted to see if you'd go in."* A pause, a breath that caught on something scarred. *"I thought, if he goes in, I'll leave. I don't know why. A man who goes in there isn't going to hold on to anything I tell him."*

"I didn't go in."

*"No. You stood. I know how that is."*

"Hal, I'm going to call Nell Castellano."

*"No."*

"I have to. A man's been shot. Barrett Voss, he's in surgery upstairs, and the last voice on his phone is yours."

The silence went on so long I heard the hospital's air handlers behind me, the dull, even breath of the building.

*"I know,"* he said. *"It isn't mine."*

"Then tell her."

*"Tell me what you'll say to her first. Tell me the words. I want to hear them."*

I thought about the corridor and her face in the dome light of the car. *I'd have to be an idiot not to knock.* I thought about how long a man could sit on a bench behind a garden center before he decided that a stranger in a stairwell was better than a police station.

"I'll say you called me. I'll say you're alive, you're frightened, and you want to talk. I won't say you're guilty. I don't know that you are."

*"And Reyes?"*

"Nobody tells Reyes anything."

A long breath.

*"Ten minutes,"* Rouse said. *"Before she comes. You and me. Then you can do what you like."*

I was already moving. I crossed four lanes against a red light, with the phone still at my ear and a horn going somewhere behind me, and while I crossed I dialed Nell on the second phone, the one in my other hand, which I had taken out without thinking.

She answered on the first ring.

"Yes."

"I've got Rouse."

I heard her stop walking.

"Where?"

I told her. Garden center, east of the hospital, back fence by the dumpsters. I told her he'd called me, that he said it wasn't his voice on the recording, that he wanted a few minutes. I said it fast, the way you make a confession so that it will be over.

"Don't let him out of your sight. Don't get in a car with him. Don't let him give you anything." A door slammed on her end. "Eleven minutes. I'm on Route 1 and I have to turn around."

"Nell."

"Ten more words and I'll have to hang up."

"He's scared of Reyes."

"Aren't we all," she said, and the line closed.

---

He was sitting on a plastic pallet behind a stack of peat bags with his knees pulled up to his chest, and for a moment I took him for a pile of the same thing.

Hal Rouse had been a heavy man on Thursday. In a day he had lost some of it, or the light had. He had no cardigan. He wore a green barn coat that was too short in the arms and smelled of diesel, and his trousers were black to the thigh. One shoe was wet through. His hands, held between his knees, didn't stop moving.

"The girl at the Irving let me use her phone," he whispered. "I told her it was my son. I gave her forty dollars. She looked at me as though I'd been in a river."

"Have you?"

"Sort of." His laugh was a puff of air. "The cove behind the camp. You go in to your waist. You hold on to a mooring line and you wait for the man to go away."

I sat on a peat bag two yards from him, close enough to hear. It was colder out of the wind and the sodium light made us the same color.

"Start at eleven forty," I said.

He shut his eyes.

"I was in my office. Jean had just brought me the file you wanted. I mean the one you didn't want, the Atlantic one, with the list of what we'd signed. The phone rang on my private line. I answered it myself, because Jean was at lunch." He swallowed. "It was Desmond."

"Reyes."

"He said, 'Hal. Don't go home. Don't go anywhere you're known. Brandt's told the state police about the fifth, and they're coming for you today.' He said I should go to the camp and sit tight, that he'd send someone, or come himself, tonight. He said not to use my phone."

"And you went."

"I took my cardigan and my keys. Forty-one years I've worked for that company. When Desmond Reyes tells me something in that voice I do what he says." His eyes opened. "There was something wrong with it."

"With what?"

"With the voice. It was Desmond's. It had his rhythm, the little hitch before a name. But it was as though he had a cold. Or was talking from inside a cupboard. I thought: he's in a car. I thought: he's at a pay phone. I didn't think what I think now."

"What do you think now?"

"That I've been listening to myself on a telephone for five years, and nobody's said so."

It took me a moment.

"You mean your whisper. Someone who sounds like a man with a cold, imitating Reyes, so you'd sit in a camp until—"

"Until a car came up the drive at a quarter to six." He turned his hands over and looked at the palms. "I was at the kitchen window. A man got out of the car, an older man. Hat. He stood by the open door as if he was thinking about it. He had a telephone in his hand. I saw him put it to his ear. I couldn't hear him from there, only the shape of the sentence. And then, on the road behind him, up past the second bend, I saw someone else."

"Who?"

"Standing in the trees. Not hiding. Just standing, with his hands in the pockets of a rain jacket, gray, with the hood up. Not tall. I never saw his face. I saw him lift one hand to his mouth." Rouse did it, the back of the fingers held a little way off the lips, and I felt the corridor tilt. "The way you do before you speak to someone you don't want to wake."

"Hollis did that. In the bed. This afternoon."

"Did he?" He looked at me, very steadily. "Then I'd say it's a common gesture."

"What happened?"

"I went out the back. Through the pantry, down the bank, into the water. I heard Mr. Voss say my name. 'Hal.' As though we were friends." His mouth worked. "And then I heard somebody answer him in my voice."

Wind moved the evergreens in their pots.

"Do you know what that's like?" Rouse whispered. "I do not have a good voice. I haven't had a good voice since 1994. And there was this clean small perfect thing coming across the water, with my breath in it, my own breath, saying *put it on the ground, please*. Like a recording of a man who'd never been hurt."

I didn't say anything.

"Then two shots. I stayed in the water until I couldn't feel my feet. When I came out the road was empty and there was a man lying in the weeds. I didn't go to him." His voice broke into nothing for a moment and came back. "Tell her that. Tell her I didn't go to him. I walked into the woods, and I left a man bleeding by his car. I didn't have a phone. I told myself I'd send someone."

"Did you?"

"I told the girl at the Irving. I said there'd been a shooting on Route 220. She said, 'Sir?'" He rubbed his face. "She'll have written it down. Maybe."

"Hal. Who knew about the camp?"

"Jean. Desmond. My wife." He counted on his fingers, then stopped. "Everyone. I show the slides. The Christmas party, every year, for twenty years. The old claims department used to drive up for chowder in August. Half of them have slept on that porch. Hollis Dunn once fell off the dock and had to be dried out by the stove."

I nodded. I heard myself say, "He's in a bed with eleven stitches," and Rouse looked at me and said nothing at all.

---

A car went by on the four-lane. Its lights crossed the peat bags and left us.

"Monday," said Rouse. "You asked what I remembered."

"You said I was calm."

"You were *terrifyingly* calm. I've seen men come into that office after a fire. They shake. They want you to tell them it isn't their fault. You sat down in the low chair and said, 'I need to know how to report something inside the company, and I need to know who to be afraid of.' As if you were ordering lunch." He put his chin on his knees. "Then you said, 'She told me last night.'"

"Last night."

"Sunday. You said she'd told you the shape of it. Not the names. She'd begun, and then she'd stopped, and said she'd show you the files in the morning, because she wanted you to see the pages with your own eyes before you decided what to be angry at."

*Sunday night.*

I didn't mean to look at it. It was the sodium light, I think, its hum, the cold coming off the concrete into the soles of my shoes. Or his whisper, close and dry, saying *last night*.

Mara had her back to me.

A kitchen. Our kitchen, the one with the radiator that knocked. Late, with the overhead off and only the light over the stove. She was standing at the counter with a mug going cold between her hands, and she hadn't turned around, and she said, "I need you not to say anything until I've finished." I hadn't. I'd stood in the doorway with my hands hanging, sober as a stone that night, whatever I told myself afterward, and listened to my wife tell me, in the careful voice she used on files, that there were four closings with the same sentence in them, and that three of them had my name.

She told me about two of them. I remember the cold of the kitchen window against my shoulder, left open a hand's width in October. Then she turned around and her face was the color of paper and she said, "I can't. Not tonight. I'll show you tomorrow. Please just let me show you."

And I said I would.

Then the rest, in pieces. I remembered the microwave clock, green numerals, 1:12. The cordless phone, heavier than it should have been. The porch, with its one bulb. I was standing on the back porch in my socks, in the cold, the screen door shut behind me so she wouldn't hear. I had a number in my thumb that I'd dialed in my sleep for three years. It rang twice.

Somebody picked up awake. Not woken. Awake, the way a man is who has been waiting up for something else.

*Theo. What's happened?*

I could hear that voice. That was the horror. It was entirely there, the warmth in it, the little downward settle at the end of my name, like a man lowering a hand onto a shoulder. I could have drawn the room it was in.

*Slow down. Tell me from the beginning.*

And I told it. I stood on that porch with my feet freezing and I told the voice everything my wife had told me in the kitchen, in the wrong order, in whispers, and when I was done there was a pause, and the voice said, very gently:

*Let me talk to her.*

---

"Theo."

I was on my knees in the dirt. I don't know how. Rouse had a hand on my coat sleeve, with his fingers shaking, and he was saying my name in his scraped voice, over and over, like a man trying to wake a child.

"I'm all right."

"You're gray."

"I'm all right." I sat back on my heels. I could taste copper. The hum of the lamp had gone back to being a lamp. "It's coming in pieces. It's—I remembered Sunday. The kitchen. I called someone."

"Who?"

"I don't know." It came out too fast, and I heard it, and went on anyway. "I had a list. There were people. A man at the meetings who kept a number on a card. The state hotline. A priest I used to see. At one in the morning you call a thing that picks up. It doesn't have to be a person. It could have been any of them."

"You said *him*," Rouse whispered. "Just now. 'I called him.'"

"Did I?"

"You did."

"It was a voice. A voice says *him* if it's a man. That's all. It's a man's voice, like half the voices in the world."

He took his hand away from my sleeve.

I got up. My knees were wet. I brushed the dirt off with both hands, slowly, the way you do when you want to be doing something that has an end.

*Let me talk to her.*

Rouse had told me on Thursday: *be careful who you tell.* What else had I heard that day? Only that I'd already told someone. But a man who has told someone on Sunday night and sat down in the low chair on Monday afternoon has not yet decided what to be afraid of. I had gone to Rouse to ask who to be afraid of. I would not have done that if I'd known.

I held that thought like a rail.

It lasted until headlights swung into the yard of the garden center.

---

Nell came in on foot, with her coat unbuttoned and her badge on a lanyard. Two uniformed troopers waited at the fence. She stopped ten feet from the pallet and looked at Rouse the way you look at an animal that has crawled out of a drain.

"Harold Rouse."

"Hal," he whispered.

"Hal, I'm Detective Castellano. You have the right to a lawyer, and I'd like you to have one. I'm not arresting you. I'm going to put you in a car with two troopers and a heater and a blanket, and I'm going to keep you somewhere that's not Port Carrow and not Atlantic Mutual. If you tell me where Desmond Reyes is I'll be pleased. If you tell me nothing, I'll still be pleased you're alive."

Rouse looked at her for a long moment.

"I told Mr. Brandt," he said. "I'll tell you."

"In the car."

"And—" He moved on the pallet. "If Desmond Reyes asks you where I am—"

"I won't know," said Nell. "I'll make sure of it."

She took his arm. He stood up all at once like a man unfolding. In the lamplight I saw how old he was, a worn gray man in a stranger's coat who had held a mooring line in the dark. A trooper put a blanket round him. He went to the fence without looking back.

Nell turned to me.

"Your phone is in your hand," she said.

"Yes."

"Was it in your hand at the liquor store?"

I had known she would ask. I had promised myself the truth, and still it took a moment to get it out.

"I stood there. A long time. I counted steps. I didn't go in."

"I know," she said. "I looked at the garage camera on my way out. Eight minutes." She didn't smile. "If you had, I'd have let you. I wouldn't have enjoyed it."

"That isn't what I—"

"You said it so I'd know. That's what I mean." She looked at the road. "Don't do it again. Tell me *before*. You've got my number. It's the only one I've ever given anybody who wasn't in a squad."

I didn't trust my voice, so I nodded.

"Something happened to you just now."

"A memory. The kitchen on Sunday. I don't have the rest."

"Do you want to tell me?"

"Not yet."

She stood there, and I thought she was going to push. She didn't. Her phone had gone, a short double buzz, and she turned her shoulder to read.

I watched her face.

"What?"

"Reyes's assistant," she said. "I called her back after Boston. He wasn't at the meeting. It was canceled at ten this morning. His car's registered at a Marriott in Portland as of last night, and nobody's seen it since." She put the phone away. "And the officer outside Hollis's door says a man in a gray suit came to the floor at twenty past seven. Didn't ask for you. Asked for Hollis. The officer sent him back down because he wasn't on the list."

"Reyes."

"He left a card. At the desk. He didn't say anything else." She was already walking. "He was in this building an hour ago, Theo, while you were in the waiting room with Cora."

---

She had two men drive me back across the road. At the door of the ward I told the night officer, a woman I hadn't met, that I wanted to look in on Mr. Dunn only for a moment. She checked a clipboard and said I was on the list.

The corridor was quiet. The mop man had gone. Somewhere a monitor beeped to itself.

Hollis's door stood a hand's width open, and a light on the wall behind the bed made the room amber and soft. I put my fingers against the wood.

I was going to say *I'm back* in the dark, softly, the way you do.

Before I could, the figure in the bed turned his head on the pillow. The dressing was white and the swollen eye was shut, but the other one found me instantly, clear and wet and entirely awake.

"Ah," said Hollis, in the voice I had known for twenty years. His mouth moved into something that was almost a smile. "There you are."

I stood in the doorway.

Everybody says it. It's what you say to someone who's late.

I told myself that all the way across the room, and I was still telling myself when I sat down beside him and took the hand he held out to me, and it was warm.
