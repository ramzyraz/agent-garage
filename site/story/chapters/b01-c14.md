# Chapter 14: The Person to Call

By four in the morning I had worn a pale place in the skin between my thumb and forefinger.

It was where a pen rested. There was no pen in the cell, but my hand kept making room for one. I wanted to write the fifth of October down: four o'clock, Hal Rouse; evening, Ansel Pryor. I wanted columns, a line between what somebody said and what anybody could prove. Without paper, the two men kept moving closer together until there was no space left for me between them.

In Rouse's office I had been trying to protect my wife. At Pryor's house, according to a dead man's handwriting, I had been trying to stop her.

Both accounts described me as sober.

That was the part I couldn't leave alone. I'd always put a bottle at the beginning of those missing days. A bottle was a thing you could understand. You could knock it over, pour it out, take yourself to a church basement and count the days since you'd last touched it. But two men had now put me in rooms before the drinking, and I couldn't remember either room.

The door opened at twenty past four.

A woman in a charcoal suit stood beside the deputy. Her hair was short and silver, and she carried a leather bag with one broken handle repaired in red thread. She looked at the steel toilet, then at me.

"Theodore Brandt?"

"Yes."

"Ellen Sayer. I'm your lawyer."

"I didn't call a lawyer."

"Somebody did. Get up."

---

They gave us the interview room with the camera off. Sayer checked the light herself before she sat down. She put a legal pad on the table and uncapped a fountain pen.

"Who hired you?" I asked.

"Hollis Dunn. My office number is on the wall beside his telephone. He called at three seventeen."

There was a place inside my chest that had been held tight all night. It loosened before I could stop it.

"How did he know?"

"He rang the inn because you hadn't answered him. The clerk told him the police had been asking about you. He made another call. Then he called me."

"You've come from Portland?"

"I was in Camden. My sister has a guest room and a very loud clock." She wrote my name at the top of the pad. "Mr. Dunn pays the bill. You are the client. He gets no account of what you tell me unless you ask me to give him one. Do we understand each other?"

I nodded.

"Now. Did you ask to leave?"

"No."

"Have you told them you'd stay voluntarily?"

"I don't think so."

"Have they charged you?"

"They said they hadn't."

She set the pen down.

"Then we'll start there."

I tried to tell her about the note before she went out. She held up a hand.

"I will read it. First I want the room unlocked."

She was gone for twelve minutes. I counted them by the second hand on the clock above the wired glass. Through the wall I could hear Ouellette's voice and hers, both too low to make out. Once his rose enough for me to hear *circumstances*. Hers stayed where it was.

When she came back she had copies of both notes.

"They can't keep holding you just because you spoke with Pryor," she said. "The prosecutor is reviewing the death. In the meantime they intend to book you for criminal trespass."

"He asked me to come."

"That's what you say. Their complaint describes you entering a private house without permission. The telephone records establish a call, not its contents."

"So I'll stay here."

"No. I'll challenge the complaint. Mr. Dunn is arranging the money for bail. You are going to leave this building, and then we're going to address the considerably larger problem." She put the photocopies flat between us. "Tell me everything you did last night. Actions first. Explanations after."

---

She didn't blink when I told her about the brass cat.

She didn't blink at the lie on Nell's voicemail either. She wrote *9 p.m.* in the margin and drew a box round it. Then she asked about Pryor's call, where I had parked, which door I had used at the inn, whether anybody had seen me between ten and eleven.

"I didn't park. I walked."

"And nobody saw you?"

"Somebody called me in as a prowler."

"Somebody called in a man in a dark coat. We don't yet know what they saw."

She made me go through it again. By the second time I could see the evening the way the police would see it: a false message about going to bed, an unobserved exit, an unlocked door, a dead man who had written down my name.

I told her about Rouse. Then Mara's card. *Told T. He'll know what to do.* I said those words with my hands flat on the table, as though holding something down.

Sayer read Pryor's handwritten statement twice.

"Could you have gone to his house that evening?"

"Yes."

"Could you have said these things?"

"I don't know."

She waited.

"I could have told him Mara had proof. I could have asked him to go to the police. If I'd just been to see Rouse, that would make sense."

"And this?" Her pen touched *better for everyone if she stopped*.

"No."

"You remember not saying it?"

I looked at her.

"That's why I'm asking," she said. "I need to know which sort of no it is."

"I can't remember. But I wouldn't have wanted her stopped."

She wrote that down without improving it.

"A frightened man can record a conversation badly," she said. "A dishonest man can record it to suit himself. Either way, the writing is his account. We have to find something outside it."

"He's dead."

"The rest of the world isn't. Where were you living? Did you use a card to buy petrol? Did you have an appointment the next morning? Do not try to manufacture a memory to answer me. Find a record."

For the first time since Ouellette had slid the paper across, the room stopped tilting.

There might be nothing left after five years. But nothing left was different from what Pryor had said being true.

---

At six they took my fingerprints again, this time onto a form with a charge number. Sayer stood where I could see her. Nobody put the cuffs back on.

The deputy returned my shoelaces in a plastic bag. I threaded them with fingers that wouldn't work, and one came out a hole short. I couldn't bear to undo it. I left it that way.

There was a window high in the corridor. The glass was gray now. Somewhere upstairs a telephone kept ringing, stopped, started again. I imagined people arriving at work and being told what they had missed, putting mugs down, leaning over desks. By breakfast the town would have a version of the night. By lunch it would have had one all along.

Nell came down the stairs carrying my coat.

She didn't offer it to me. She looked at Sayer.

"Five minutes. With you present."

Sayer took her pad out again.

We went back into the interview room. Nell set my coat over the empty chair, where I could see the dark stain from Gannet Road's grass at the hem.

"I'm not asking about last night," she said. "Your lawyer has told me not to. I'm telling you what happens next. The death is being treated as suspicious. Augusta hasn't called it suicide."

"The pills—"

"Tell us what was on the desk. They don't tell us how it got there. We haven't got the toxicology. We haven't established which tablets were in the bottle before it was emptied."

She spoke carefully, each sentence smaller than what I wanted it to mean.

"The typed note goes to documents. So does the handwritten one. We have his ordinary handwriting from county records. A match would mean he wrote it. It wouldn't establish that the account is true."

"Can they tell when he wrote it?"

"Not give us a date off a photocopy. Maybe not off the original. Don't start expecting that."

"It says five years ago."

"Yes. It wasn't written on the fifth. Whatever he remembered, or wanted somebody to think he remembered, he put it on paper later."

I had read the page three times in the night. Each time I had imagined Pryor writing it with my footsteps still outside his door.

Nell watched me realize it.

"It could still be an accurate account," she said. "I won't pretend otherwise. But we need to know why he wrote it now, and why a man who called to offer you information left something saying you already knew."

Sayer made a note.

"And the prowler call?" she asked.

"We're preserving the dispatch recording. Caller didn't give a name. They said they were passing in a car. We're checking what can be checked."

"Time?"

"Eleven sixteen."

Six minutes after I arrived. Somewhere near the house there had been a person with a telephone, waiting long enough to be sure I went in.

Or a passing driver had seen a man at a lit doorway. That was possible too. I hated that it was possible.

Nell put a clear property bag on the table. My wallet, my phone, the cutter key.

"Your lawyer and I will arrange access to the calls and messages," she said. "Keep everything. Don't delete anything."

Then she looked at me instead of Sayer.

"Is there something you can tell me that will help keep the next person alive?"

---

Sayer's pen stopped.

I could have waited. I could have had my lawyer explain which questions I ought to answer, while Nell carried my coat back into the corridor and Hollis stood somewhere upstairs with a receipt for my freedom.

"I told Hollis about Quill," I said.

Nell didn't move.

"His appointment. Eleven on Thursday. I told him Wednesday night. I showed him Mara's letter. I told him about the unit and the missing box and the ledger."

Sayer looked at me once, then wrote.

"All of it?" Nell asked.

"Not Cora's recording. Not the boat she saw."

"You told him about Tarbell coming to see me too."

"Monday night. At supper."

The ventilation made a dry rattle over our heads. Nell waited until it stopped.

"And Pryor?"

"I told nobody. I mean it this time. Hollis texted asking me to come over. I didn't answer. I went to the inn."

"We'll need that text."

"It's there."

"Why did you tell me nobody knew about Quill?"

I put my hand on the edge of the table. The pale place between finger and thumb stung.

"Because you would ask me about Hollis."

"Yes," she said. "I would."

Sayer closed her pad halfway, then opened it again. She was giving me time to stop.

"He's kept me alive," I said. "For five years. He's the person I call when I don't know what to do."

"I know."

"That isn't evidence against him."

"It isn't evidence for him either."

She said it without cruelty. I wished she'd been cruel. I could have argued with that.

---

Hollis was at the top of the stairs.

He sat in a molded plastic chair beside a drinks machine, his gray coat folded over his knees, holding a paper cup between both hands. On the chair next to him was a brown bag. He stood when he saw me, slowly, the way he always stood when he'd been sitting too long.

"There you are," he said.

I stopped on the last step.

He set the cup down and came to me. He rested a hand on my shoulder, not a hug, just its weight, and I discovered I was shaking. I hadn't been shaking in the cell. I hadn't shaken with Sayer or Nell. It began under his hand, and I couldn't make it stop.

"Easy," he said. "You've been up all night."

He took the brown bag off the chair. There was a bacon roll in it, still warm, wrapped in white paper.

"You used to say these were the only things in town the seagulls wouldn't steal."

I didn't remember saying it. It sounded like me.

Behind us, Ouellette came through a door with a folder under his arm. Hollis turned without taking his hand off my shoulder.

"I've given Mrs. Sayer the number for my solicitor," he said. "Anything else you need goes through her. Theo will be at the Anchor, where you know perfectly well how to find him."

"Mr. Dunn, we're conducting—"

"A man called you because he'd found somebody dead. I'd like the next man to feel able to do the same."

Ouellette's mouth narrowed. Sayer took a step toward him, and they went aside to discuss something with no raised voices. Nell stood by the stairs and watched me holding the bag.

Bail had been posted. There were conditions on a sheet I had to sign: an address, a return date, instructions about witnesses. Sayer went through them with me. Hollis stayed at the other end of the counter. He didn't crowd the pen.

"I'll repay you," I said afterward.

"Eat the roll first."

"The lawyer. The bail."

"I heard you."

He picked up his coat. I saw the cup he had left beside the machine. Water. He'd waited for me for hours without making a ceremony of it.

That seemed a reason to trust him. I was still looking for those.

---

Sayer left us outside the Anchor at half past eight.

She would stay in Camden, she said. I was to call her before answering police questions. She gave me two cards and put one into my wallet herself.

Hollis carried my coat upstairs. The night clerk's replacement looked up from behind the desk, then down again too quickly. A radio in the breakfast room was giving a weather forecast. More wind from the northeast. Another system following the first.

In room 6 Hollis opened the curtains. The window faced a wall with a drainpipe on it. There wasn't much morning to let in.

He filled the kettle at the sink.

"No," I said.

He turned.

"No tea. Please."

"Coffee?"

"Nothing."

He set the kettle back on its base without switching it on.

I sat on the bed. He took the chair beside the little desk, and for a moment it was a room like any of the rooms he'd found me in five years ago: me on a bed, him between me and the door, something warm cooling in a bag.

"Ellen tells me they have a statement," he said. "From Pryor."

"She told you?"

"Only that there's a statement. I asked why they were keeping you. She said she couldn't discuss the rest."

I rubbed my wrists. There was nothing on them now, not even a red mark.

"He says I went to see him the fifth of October. That evening."

Hollis sat very still.

"And did you?"

"I don't know. Rouse says I was in his office at four. I don't remember that either."

"Hal?"

"I asked him how to report fraud. Mara had found it. I was trying to help her."

"Then that's something you know."

"It's something he's told me."

He nodded. He accepted the correction. It made me want to take it back.

"Were you with me that night?" I asked.

His eyes went to the window, then back.

"The fifth? I can't tell you without looking. Five years is a long time. I was still working then. Some nights Portland, some here."

"Would you look? Diaries. Expenses. Anything."

"Of course."

"I need somewhere I was."

"I know."

He leaned forward, his forearms on his knees. The sleeve pulled back above the blue anchor on his wrist.

"Has anything come back? A bit of a conversation? Anything you didn't remember before?"

A kettle. A smell. His voice saying my name in a kitchen that might have been any kitchen we'd shared.

"No," I said.

It was the first lie I'd told him that morning. It was so much easier than the truth I'd told Nell that I was ashamed of the relief.

"Don't force it," he said. "Remember what they told you at the hospital. You fill a hole by guessing, you can end up believing the guess. Let Ellen find the paper."

He put his hand on my knee and stood.

"Sleep. I'll be home. You know the number."

At the door he paused.

"Did Pryor say what you threatened him with?"

I looked up.

"I didn't say threatened."

"No. I'm sorry. Ellen said the statement was being used against you. I assumed—" He shook his head. "Let me read it before I say any more. If you want me to."

I could have given it to him. I had a copy in my coat. All I had to do was reach.

"I'll ask her," I said.

"Good. That's what she's for."

He pulled the door shut. His footsteps went along the carpet, stopped at the stairs, continued down.

I got up and put the chain on.

---

I didn't sleep.

At nine twelve I switched my phone on. The battery had seventeen percent left. A row of messages arrived, most from numbers I knew professionally, men who had heard something and wanted to know whether the hearing was right. One from Gus: *Office secure. Call when you can.*

One from Cora, sent at eight forty-six.

*Voss has something of Dad's. We need you here. Don't tell anyone yet.*

I looked at the last five words for a long time.

Then I rang Nell. I told her where I was going and who would be there. She told me not to touch anything Voss wanted to show me. I said I wouldn't. Sayer answered on her second ring and said she would meet me outside Cora's house.

Only then did I call Cora.

She picked up without saying hello.

"Are you alone?"

"Yes. But I've told Castellano. And my lawyer's coming."

A sharp breath. For a moment I thought she would hang up.

"All right," she said. "All right. Just come."

"What has Voss found?"

"A letter. Dad left it with somebody in case he didn't get to speak to you. They saw the news this morning."

I stood beside the bed with one shoe still laced wrong, looking at the empty chair.

"When did he leave it?"

"The afternoon before he died."

"Cora. What does it say?"

She started to answer, stopped, and I heard paper move against the phone.

"The first line is your name," she said. "Then: 'I knew what they were going to do to Mara. I let them.'"
