# Chapter 11: The Fourth Floor

Nell dropped me at the Anchor at ten past two, and said nothing for the whole of the ride back. At the curb she put the Ford in park and sat looking through the windshield at the Anchor's wet sign.

"Portland," she said.

"Yes."

"Rouse is a frightened man in a nice office. He didn't kill Quill. He can't change a tire."

"Then he won't mind me asking."

"I can't stop you." She turned her head. "I can tell you to call me from the lobby. And again when you come out. And if you don't come out, I'll know what building to start in." When I got out, she added, without looking at me: "Eat something, Theo."

I stood in the road a while after she'd gone. Then I got my car.

---

It is ninety-one miles from Port Carrow to Portland, and the first fourteen of them are the causeway, the low point, the gauge post, the place where the road remembers it was a sea floor. I took it at forty. At the trough, a county truck sat with its amber light turning while a man in a slicker hosed silt off the asphalt in broad, patient sweeps, as if the road could be made to forget. I didn't look at the guardrail. Past it, the world went back to being a state highway: a Dunkin', a tire place, a billboard for a personal-injury lawyer smiling like a man at his own funeral.

Nell called at Wiscasset.

"State's got him," she said. She sounded tired, with the careful flatness of a woman reading from something she didn't want to be true. "Augusta's van got there at three. Their man looked at him in the bay and rang me from the van, so this is a first look and I'm not going to pretend it's more. No water in the airway. No froth. Nothing in the lungs. He didn't drown, Theo."

I took my foot off the gas without deciding to.

"The tide came over a dead man. Lividity's all in the seat, consistent with sitting up. No marks. They ran what they call a rapid screen in the field lab because I leaned on them, and it lit for a benzodiazepine. Not a little. The man said it's a level you'd put a horse down on."

"He took them?"

"Quill? He didn't take aspirin. His doctor told me last winter he wouldn't take a flu shot." A pause, a road noise. "Somebody sat with him in that car. Somebody had a thermos, or a bottle of something, and he's seventy and polite, and somebody said it was cold and they'd drink to Mara."

*It sounded exactly like you.*

"I'll call you from the lobby," I said.

"You do that."

---

The Atlantic Mutual Casualty building on Congress Street is eleven stories of brown glass built in 1979 by men who admired Mies and didn't understand him. I'd spent ten years of my life in it. I knew which of the three elevators stuck, and which stair door set off an alarm no one answered, and that the third-floor coffee machine made a sound like a drowning cat. I knew that if you came in at 3:40 on a Thursday the guard at the desk would be eating a sandwich behind the *Press Herald* and wouldn't look up.

He looked up.

"Mr. Brandt," he said, and went quietly red. He had been a different man five years ago. I couldn't think of his name. "We've had a— I'm to call upstairs."

"To whom?"

"Mr. Reyes's office. Anytime you come." He swallowed. "I'm sorry. It's the standing note."

"How long's it stood?"

"Since Saturday."

Saturday. Before Tarbell. Before Quill. The day after the body in the boathouse. I thanked him and took the elevator that stuck, and it stuck, and I got out on four with the sensation of a man arriving at a party at which his name had been called out ahead of him.

---

Hal Rouse had a corner office with two windows and a view of the harbor, and a secretary named Mrs. Keene who stood up when she saw me as though a policeman had come in. He was alone behind a desk the size of a door. Harold Rouse, 63, regional vice president, a long thin man with a narrow lined face and the hollowed look of someone who had been losing weight at a steady rate for years and had run out of things to lose. He wore the same navy cardigan under the same suit jacket I remembered. He was holding a pen in both hands, upright, like a candle.

"Theo," he said. His voice was a whisper. It had always been a whisper; he'd had throat surgery in the nineties. "I thought you might come. I told Jean to let you up. I'm sorry about Ferris Quill."

"You knew him."

"Wednesday afternoon, yes. He came to see me." He put the pen down. He took it up again. "I understand he's dead. It's on the radio. A car on the causeway."

"He was seen going into this building at 2:30 on Wednesday. In his diary it says H.R."

"I'm H.R." Rouse said it flatly, with a kind of exhausted dignity. "I've always been H.R. I have every initial in this company that begins with an H and an R."

I took the visitor's chair, low and tan and ugly, and looked around. On the credenza behind him stood a brass-armed banker's lamp with a green glass shade, lit in the middle of the afternoon, pooling yellow on a blotter. Above it hung a framed photograph of Rouse with the governor.

It was the lamp. It was the right lamp. Not a trick of the light, not the angle; the green was the green in the ledger photograph, the same tilt in the shade. And behind the desk, the high-backed leather chair, cracked at one arm, the kind you sat in and heard it creak.

I'd sat in the other one. I'd always sat in the other one.

"Mr. Quill asked me about the lamp," said Rouse, who had followed my eyes. "Showed me a photograph. A piece of one. Asked if I knew it. I said every regional director of the old guard has one. The company bought three hundred in 1988 from a man in Providence and told us we were being modernized." The ghost of a smile. "I said, I expect you'd find that lamp in a good many rooms."

"What did he want?"

"He wanted to know who approved the payments." He folded his hands. "Four files. He had the names on a piece of paper, initials only, but I knew them. Doyle. Anselm. The Farrow woman. And a fourth he wouldn't tell me, and I think I know it, and I'd just as soon not say it aloud." He looked up. "He asked who signs. I told him: I signed. I'm the signature. Regional VP. Anything over five hundred thousand goes to me."

"You signed them."

"I signed forty a week. Fifty." The whisper cracked. "Do you know what a regional VP reads? The cover sheet. A one-page summary and the adjuster's recommendation. The file's behind it. I've never in my life read a file where the cover sheet was clean, Theo. If the adjuster was good and the director had initialed it—"

"Which director?"

"Whichever one it was. On those, the cover sheet said *recommend payment, no evidence of third-party involvement*, and under it a pair of initials, and they were good initials. I trusted them."

"Whose?"

A long pause. In the harbor beyond the glass a ferry was working back to the pier.

"It was a long time ago," said Rouse.

"Hal."

"Yours." He said it so gently I thought I'd misheard. "Yours, Theo. The adjuster's recommendation. T.B., T.B., T.B., and I trusted you more than I trusted myself. You were the best I had. I'd sign a file with your name on it blindfold. I *did*."

---

I said nothing for what felt like a minute. I had seen the ledger photograph. But there's knowing, and there is a frightened old man telling you across a desk that you were the reason it worked.

"Someone put them on my desk," I said.

"I assume so."

"Who assigns the field files? Not you."

"The regional director. Assigned, and then countersigned at the close." He rubbed his face. "I saw it, once. The language. I'd had four closing letters on my blotter over three years and I thought, they read as if they were all written by the same man. And I said so, in a meeting, to Desmond. He was assistant then. I said, *the language is very consistent in these.* And Desmond said that consistency was a sign of a well-run shop." Rouse laughed without sound. "And I thought, he's right. And I went home."

"You looked away."

"I *looked away.*" It wasn't a whisper. It was only very quiet. "I have been looking away for nine years, and in all that time I have been the nicest man in this building. I'm known for it. I send flowers. It is not an accomplishment."

"The chair," I said.

"What?"

"Mara said I'd know the chair. And Quill said it on the phone before he died. That he'd be the man who didn't ask what chair." I looked at the leather behind the desk, and I watched him look at it as well, without comprehension, as one looks at a sofa. "I think it's this one."

"It's my chair." He seemed bewildered. "I've had it eleven years. It came with the office. They always do. I expect Hollis's old fellow had it before—"

The door opened without a knock.

---

Desmond Reyes did not come into a room; he deployed into it. He was fifty-two and fit, with the shaved scalp and rimless glasses of a man whose self-image was a surgeon's, and he moved with the tidy economy of someone who expected to find the furniture arranged correctly. He wore a charcoal suit and a tie with a very small knot. Behind him, in the door, Mrs. Keene put her hand to her throat and said, "I'm so sorry, he—"

"Thank you, Jean."

She went.

"Theo," said Reyes. He didn't smile. He gave me my own first name the way you hand a man his hat. "I'd hoped it would be a phone call. I've had a bad week. I imagine you have as well."

"You sent the lobby a standing note on Saturday."

"I send the lobby a note on anyone with a claims history." His nasal voice was carefully pleasant. "Hal, you're not feeling well. Go sit down in the other room."

"I'm perfectly—"

"Hal."

Rouse stood up. His chair rolled back and struck the credenza and the green shade rocked, and for a second the light swung over all of us. Then he went out through the side door, with his cardigan hanging off his narrow shoulders, and shut it behind him with exquisite care.

Reyes took the leather chair.

He didn't ask. He sat. He put his two hands flat on the blotter, and under the green light he looked at me for what felt like a very long time.

"I'm withdrawing your engagement on Farrow," he said. "Effective now. Atlantic thanks you for your work. You'll bill me through Tuesday and you'll return every document in your possession by Monday noon, and I'll have a courier at your office if you'd prefer not to deliver them yourself."

"On what grounds?"

"On the ground that you are no longer a disinterested adjuster. You're a witness. You're a person of interest in the death of Ned Tarbell. The state police have your business card on his body." He said it as a matter of courtesy, in the voice of a man listing the ways a window might leak. "And as of this morning, you appear in the file of a second death. I have to think about the company, Theo. I have to think about what it is if our field investigator is found to have been, in any sense, party to a—"

"Quill."

"Mr. Quill. Yes."

"You knew Quill was dead before I walked in."

"The *Press Herald* has it online, Theo, with a photograph of the car." He tilted his head. "I'd like you to hear me. I'm not warning you off because of what I fear. I'm warning you because of what I know of you. You were a good adjuster and then you were a very sad man, and then you were a sober one, and Atlantic has been grateful, and generous, and patient. I'm talking about *patience.*" A small smile, at last, quick and joyless as a hinge. "If you go on—if you take what you've found, whatever it is, to people outside the company—the company will defend itself. We'll say what we know. That you had a breakdown. That you've been sleeping rough. That you have a gap in your memory that you've never been able to account for. I can *document* it."

It was very quiet. Out in the corridor a phone rang and rang.

"Who told you about the gap?" I said.

"I read your file."

"It isn't in the file."

Reyes looked at me through the rimless lenses, and for the first time his face did not move at all.

"Mr. Dunn mentioned it," he said. "At a dinner. Years ago. He worries about you so. He asked us all to be understanding." He stood. "Go home, Theo. Go back to Port Carrow and sit with your friend. He's the only one of us who's never been anything but good to you."

---

The guard walked me out. He was very apologetic. On the sidewalk on Congress Street the wind had come up, with a smell of rain and diesel, and I stood with my back to the glass and called Nell and told her *I'm out*, and *Reyes pulled me*, and *Quill saw Rouse for the lamp, Rouse signed, nothing else.*

"And?"

"He's frightened. He's compromised. He didn't kill anyone."

"I told you."

"He signed forty files a week on my name, Nell. On my name. They went through because it was me."

She was quiet. Then she said, "I'm sorry," in a voice that didn't soften it, and I loved her a little for that.

I was fumbling for the car key when the lobby door opened behind me and somebody said, "Mr. Brandt?" in a whisper.

Rouse had come down the stairs. He'd put on a gray raincoat over the cardigan and he held the door with a hand that wasn't steady, and he looked back over his shoulder at the empty lobby the way a man looks who has been followed before. He didn't come out. He stood between the glass doors, in the little vestibule where the mats were.

"I didn't want to say it in front of Desmond," he whispered. "I didn't want to say it in that room. I'm a coward, Theo, I told you. But Quill asked me one more thing on Wednesday that I didn't tell you. It was the last thing, and I didn't want to answer, and I did."

"What?"

"He asked if anyone had come to see me. Five years ago. About Mara."

The wind blew a page of newspaper across the road.

"I said no. And then I said, *well. You did.*"

"I what?"

"You came to see me, Theo. You don't remember." He wasn't asking. His eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "It was a Monday. The fifth. Mara had been dead— no. She wasn't. It was nine days before. You came in at four in the afternoon and you sat in the low chair, where you were sitting today, and you were sober. Do you hear me? You were stone sober, and white as paper, and you asked me one question. You wanted to know how an employee of Atlantic reports a fraud inside the company. Who runs the ethics line. Who reads it. Whether it goes to the director of claims."

I couldn't feel my hands.

"I said, *do you have proof?* You said, *my wife does.* And I said—" His voice failed him, and came back. "I said *be careful who you tell.* That's all. I said it as a *kindness.* And you thanked me and went out. And nine days later your wife was dead and I never said a word to anyone, because I thought: *who did he tell, after me?*"

The glass doors hummed in their frames. Across the street a bus sighed to the curb.

"I've been waiting five years for you to ask," said Hal Rouse. "And you never did. And I thought: he doesn't remember. He doesn't *know* he came. So whoever he told next is the one person who's never had to wonder."

"Who did I tell?"

"I don't know," he whispered. "That's what I'm asking you."

He let the door close between us. I saw him through the glass, a thin old man in a gray coat, going back across the lobby like someone returning to a cell. I stood on Congress Street with the key in my hand, and the sky coming down, and ten days of October behind me in the dark.

I had walked into that building looking for a chair, and I had found the chair, and it was the one I'd been sitting in.
