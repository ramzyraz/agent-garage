# Chapter 10: The Low Point

Nell drove the way she did everything, with the whole of her attention and none of her comfort. She took the county building's back lot in a skid, ran the Front Street light, and put the unmarked Ford onto the causeway road with the needle past seventy and her jaw set like a latch.

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that she didn't already know. The tide table on the conference room wall had said high water at 5:48 that evening. The morning's water had been and gone, and the road was dry and shining and empty, and somewhere out on it was a man I had spoken to on Tuesday afternoon, in his office above the antiques, while he made me promise to eat something.

"Don't touch anything," Nell said, to the windshield. "Don't speak to anyone from the county. If Pryor turns up, you stand behind me and you look at your shoes."

"Pryor?"

"It's his causeway. It's his dead." She changed down for the bend. "Which is why I've already called the state lab in Augusta. I'd like somebody in this county to die without being rounded to the nearest accident."

The causeway is a mile and a quarter of rock fill and asphalt laid across the mud flats in 1911, by men who believed the sea could be argued with. For most of its length it sits comfortably above the flat, but at the middle, in the place the locals call the low point, the road dips eight feet into a trough and the sea comes over it first and leaves it last. There's a sign. There's a yellow gauge post painted in feet. Every summer somebody ignores them both and the volunteer fire company winches a rental car out of the grass with the tourists crying on the shoulder.

There was a trooper's cruiser pulled across the westbound lane, lights turning slowly in the flat noon light, and two men in orange slickers smoking by the guardrail. Beyond them, in the trough, mud-brown to the door handles and strung with weed, sat the gray Volvo station wagon that had been old when I met its owner.

I got out of the car, and the smell came up to meet me: salt, rot, wet wool.

---

It was nose-down in the shoulder with its left wheels on the asphalt and its right in the drainage ditch, as if the driver had just been about to turn it around. The tide had been over it. You could see exactly how far: a clean brown line across the doors, the glass, up to the middle of the roof, where the water had stood and drawn its own level and then gone away. Windrows of eelgrass hung from the wipers and the roof rack. A single crab, dead and the color of a stained tooth, sat on the hood.

"One tide," Nell said.

I looked at her.

"Dispatch said twice. It's once." She crouched by the rear wheel and put her finger to the line and looked at the silt on it. "One band, no second wash mark. The high water came over at about twenty past five this morning, stood a couple of hours, and went down. The lobsterman who found it called it in at eleven-forty. He said it looked like it'd been sat in the ocean all night, and the dispatcher turned that into 'twice.'"

"Before twenty past five," I said.

"Before the water came, yes. It was already here." She straightened up. "Which is where the girl in Portland and I have a conversation."

I'd been avoiding the driver's window. I'd been looking at the crab and the gauge post and the gulls, the way you look at everything but the thing, and I could hear that I was doing it. Nell touched my sleeve once. Then she took the cruiser's torch from the trooper and shone it through the glass, and stepped aside, and let me come.

He was in the driver's seat with the belt across him.

Ferris Quill, seventy years old, in his gray overcoat and the Homburg he'd worn since before I was born, with his hands in his lap, one upon the other, like a man waiting for the clerk to call his name. The water had put his hair flat and it had taken the color out of his face, and the glasses he'd worn on a cord for thirty years hung crooked from his neck. His eyes were closed. If somebody had told me he'd dozed off, I'd have wanted to believe them for several seconds.

"Oh, no," I said. "Oh, you old—"

Nell said nothing. She let me stand there.

"He told me to eat something," I said. "On Tuesday. He made me take half his sandwich."

"I know." She said it very quietly. "He told me you'd done it, too."

I looked at the doors. Locked, all four; the little pins were down. The window, rolled up. The key in the ignition, turned off. In the passenger footwell a folded umbrella, black, perfectly dry in a car that had been under the sea. Dry because it had been on the seat, I thought, and had fallen as the water went. His briefcase, empty, lay on the back seat with its clasps open. And that was all.

"The box," I said.

"No box."

"He had a banker's box. Everything he was bringing to you."

"I saw." Nell looked at the empty back seat as though it had insulted her. "The girl said he took it in at seven. If he took it in at seven, he took it out again, and if he took it out again someone has it, and it isn't in this car."

A white county van came over the rise from the island side and pulled up behind the cruiser, and I didn't have to turn to know who got out. I heard the click first. A ballpoint, being pressed and released, pressed and released, a nervous man's metronome.

---

"Detective." Ansel Pryor came down the verge in his oatmeal cardigan with a leather bag, mopping his face with a handkerchief. In the daylight he looked sick, the skin of his cheeks glossy and gray. "I'd heard there was something on the causeway. My God. Is it Quill?"

"It's Quill."

"A terrible thing. An old man." He lifted the handkerchief to his mouth and lowered it. He didn't look at me. He looked at the water line on the door. "He'll have mistaken the tide. It's very easy. We have one a year, Detective. People think they know the table, and then there's a surge, and a car in the dark—"

"In the dark," said Nell.

"At four, five in the morning. The low point is a trap. He was elderly, he'd have been tired—"

"He didn't drive onto the low point at five in the morning, Doctor, because he'd never have been on this road at five in the morning. He lived in Portland, he took the 7:40 train, and he was careful about it." Nell kept her voice quite level. "I'd like him to go to Augusta."

"That's irregular. It's a county death—"

"It's a suspicious death in a county where the last two deaths I put in front of you were called accidents, and one of them is my case and the other was a friend of mine." She let that go by without looking at me. "I've asked the state lab. They're sending a van. If you want to be present, you're welcome. If you want to touch him before they get here—"

"I wouldn't," said Pryor.

He said it too fast. He said it to the car, to the crab on the hood, and I watched his thumb go down on the pen and stay there, and when I looked at his face he had gone to the color of paper.

"I'd never," said Pryor.

Then he went away up the bank to sit in his van with the door open and his hands between his knees, and Nell watched him go.

"He's afraid," she said.

"Of you?"

"Of whatever it is that's coming up the road behind me." She turned back to the car. "Think, Theo. Think like Hollis taught you. A man drives to the low point in the middle of the night, an old man who doesn't drive at night. Why?"

*Because someone asked him to.*

I said, "Nobody makes Quill do anything. He'd go, if it was Mara's father's lawyer asking. If it was a client. If it was an emergency." I heard myself and stopped. "If he thought it was me."

Nell's phone buzzed in her hand. She read it, and she was silent for a time.

"That's Portland," she said. "They've been in his office."

---

The detective on the line was named Ruiz and he talked in Nell's ear for four minutes while I watched the sea come back across the flats in a long bright seam. It was a mile out and turning. By five it would be over the road.

When Nell hung up she told me.

The office door on Exchange Street had been locked, and had been locked with a key, from outside. Inside, the lights were on, and the desk lamp, and the little electric heater under the desk, and every other thing in the room was exactly where Quill kept it. Except the cabinet. The big oak cabinet at the back, with the four deep drawers. The bottom drawer was labeled in Quill's cramped hand, BRANDT, M., and the drawer was empty. Not rifled. Emptied, the file folders gone and the hanging rails wiped down.

"The girl says she saw him go in at seven," Nell said.

"This morning."

"This morning is what the trooper reported. I put it to her again myself on the phone from the lot. She thinks about it. She says, 'Well, it was Wednesday, wasn't it? The day before. He said it'd be a big day tomorrow.'" Nell looked at her phone like it owed her money. "The trooper had asked her what time she saw him go in and she said seven, and he wrote *today.* Nobody asked her what day."

"So she saw him on Wednesday morning with the box."

"And not since. The lights have been on since sometime Wednesday. The man left his office on Wednesday and he took the box with him." She counted it on her fingers, quick. "He rang me Wednesday night. I told you. About nine, from home, gleeful as a boy. He's got a landline. His phone company's pulling the log. Ruiz says his neighbor heard his car start at twenty to two."

"At twenty to two in the morning."

"Which is when he leaves Portland, with a banker's box, and drives two hours to a causeway he's not supposed to be on, and gets here at about quarter to four with the tide coming. Which is, if you work it back, about the time a person could do something to a car and leave it where the water would come." She put the phone away. "Who rings an old man at one in the morning and gets him in the car?"

I'd known it for several minutes. I had the answer ready in my mouth like a stone.

"Somebody who sounds like me," I said.

She didn't say yes. She didn't say anything. She stood with her hands in her jacket pockets and the wind taking her hair, and she let me say the rest.

"The way Tarbell's wife said. The caller who said he was Brandt. I wasn't on the phone, Nell. I was at the Anchor. The clerk—" I stopped. "The clerk saw me at ten past one. I came in. I went up. And then I slept through, and I mean through, I slept eight hours."

"Chain on."

"Chain on."

"Did your phone ring?"

I took it out. I'd muted it to hear Cora's recording, and I had never turned the sound back on. There was one voicemail on the screen, left at 1:41 that morning from a number I knew, because it was a number I'd rung on Tuesday to ask a very old man to eat lunch with me.

I handed it to her. Nell took it. She held it to her ear and I watched her listen, and then, because she was Nell, she said, "Put it on speaker," and held it between us in the wind.

*"Mr. Brandt."*

Quill's voice. Dry, formal, unhurried, with the faint whistle he got around the *s*.

*"It is a quarter to two, and I am in the car, and I have decided to leave you a message, because I do not at all understand why I am doing this. You rang me at one-fifteen. You said that a thing had come up in the matter of Mara, that there was a place you wanted to meet and not the county building, and a reason. You said the low point of the causeway. I said, Why the low point? And you said, Because there's no one to see us."*

A pause. The little tick of the indicator.

*"It sounded like you. I want you to understand that. It sounded exactly like you, a young man's voice, rather tired, a little too polite. But you did not call me* Ferris. *In five years you have never once in your life called me anything but Mr. Quill, and tonight you called me* Ferris, *and I thought: perhaps he is frightened. Perhaps something has happened. So I am coming, and I am bringing the original with me. I wouldn't care to leave it in the office overnight."*

Nell's hand tightened on the phone.

*"If this message finds you, Mr. Brandt, and you know nothing about it, then somebody wished very much to meet me alone, and I have walked into it with my eyes open, which is a thing I was always rather proud of not doing. I shall have the Homburg on. I shall be wearing the good overcoat. If anyone should wish to identify me, I'll be the man who didn't ask what chair."*

Silence. Wind on the line, and the long tire-hiss of a road.

*"She said you'd know. I never did. I only deliver. Goodnight."*

The voicemail ended.

Neither of us moved. A gull came down on the roof of the Volvo and took a tentative step through the weed.

"I told him about the chair," I said at last. My voice sounded very far away. "No. Mara did. In the letter. He said it was on the second envelope, he never read it, he said, *you'll know the chair.*"

"Do you?"

"I don't know."

I did not say what I was seeing, which was a green glass lamp shade at the top edge of a photograph, out of focus, with a pool of yellow light under it. Nell had the photograph. She'd looked at it all night.

I thought, quickly, because I couldn't think slowly: every regional director at Atlantic had been issued the same banker's lamp, brass-armed, green glass. I'd worked under a dozen. They'd been given out in 1988 and nobody had ever taken them back. Hal Rouse had one. I'd seen it. It was on the credenza behind his chair on the fourth floor of the Portland building, the chair with the high leather back, which he'd sat in while a younger me delivered a report he'd tried to talk me out of. *Hal. H.* And on Wednesday afternoon, after Rockland, Quill had had a free afternoon.

"I'm going to Portland," I said.

"Why?"

Because there was a thing I wasn't going to tell her. Because I had put my own wife's letter in the hands of an old man, and an old man had driven to his death in the dark for the sound of my voice, and I could not stand on a causeway with an empty car and tell the detective that the only person I'd told about the eleven o'clock meeting in Port Carrow was the one I'd gone to clear first.

"Because Quill had a diary," I said. "He kept it on his desk. Tell Ruiz to look at Wednesday."

Nell was already on the phone. I watched her turn away and speak, listen, speak again, and I watched her face change.

"What," I said.

She held the phone against her chest.

"Wednesday, 2:30 p.m.," Nell said. "In his own hand. *H.R., Atlantic Mutual, 4th floor.*"

The sea was a hundred yards off now, and coming on across the mud with a sound like a thousand pages being turned.

"Theo," said Nell, in a voice so even it frightened me more than shouting would have. "Who did you tell about the eleven o'clock?"

It was the question I'd been waiting for since twelve-forty. It had come at last, and she asked it plainly, without a trace of accusation. She asked it as someone who had lost a friend, and wanted to be told a true thing.

I thought of a kitchen. A kettle. An old man's hand on the back of my neck. *Put me on the list.*

"Nobody," I said.

She looked at me for a long time. Past her, in the car, the old man with the Homburg sat with his hands in his lap, waiting for the clerk to call his name.

"Okay," said Nell. She said it very softly. "Okay."

And she didn't believe me. I knew it, as surely as I knew the tide was coming. I watched her put the knowledge away in the place where she kept things she meant to use.
