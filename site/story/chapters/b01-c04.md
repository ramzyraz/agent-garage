# Chapter 4: The Seventh Page

Dolores Pike answered on the ninth ring, with a television going behind her and the voice of a woman who had been interrupted at a Sunday roast.

"Theo Brandt," she said. "Well. I'd heard you were back on the Farrow thing. Desmond's had the whole third floor whispering since Friday."

"I need a favor, Dolores."

"You always do. You always say it like that, too, like you're asking to borrow a kidney."

I stood at the window of room six with the curtain open an inch and Cora's phone bill flat on the sill. Down on the harbor road a man in a yellow slicker was hosing the deck of a lobster boat that didn't need it. I asked her what she remembered about the phones in the claims annex six years ago.

"The phones." She turned the television down. "Lord. You mean the Nortel business."

Dolores Pike had run the claims annex front desk for twenty-two years. She knew where every file was and every person who had lied about where it was. When I started at Atlantic she told me which vending machine gave change and which supervisor to never, ever get in an elevator with. I had once watched her talk a man out of a lawsuit using only a stapler.

"The direct lines," I said. "Mine was 0188. Who picked it up when I was in the field?"

"Whoever was nearest. You didn't have an assistant, hon. You had *me,* and the overflow." She paused. "In March? You were out half that month. The Searsport total loss, and the cannery thing in Rockport."

"The cannery thing."

"Don't ask me, it's your cannery." I could hear her thinking. "We forwarded your line to the overflow desk when you were out. Anybody on the floor could take it. And the retirees who still came in for the training program had the overflow desk on Thursdays. March fifth was a Thursday, wasn't it?"

It was. I'd looked it up in the car.

"Who had the desk that Thursday?"

"Theo, I can't tell you that. I couldn't tell you what I had for lunch." A spoon rang against a cup. "There's a log. The phone system kept forwarding records. Or it did until the changeover. They wiped everything older than four years when we went to the new system. Desmond signed off on it himself, I remember, because Legal squawked."

"Reyes signed off."

"Reyes signs off on the weather. He's that kind." Her voice softened. "Is this about the girl? The daughter?"

"It's about a call."

"Then I'll tell you what I'd tell any adjuster who asked. If it was a call that mattered, somebody wrote it down. Look in the overflow binder. Pink sheets. Anyone who took a call for you filled one out, and I filed them by quarter."

"Where's the binder?"

"Archive, in Portland. Or the dumpster. Honestly, either." She let out a long breath. "Don't go in there on a Sunday. I'll look tomorrow when I get in. Nobody watches me. I'm furniture."

"Dolores, don't tell Reyes."

"Hon," she said, "I haven't told Desmond Reyes a thing since 2011."

When I hung up the gull was on the sill again, looking at me with one flat yellow eye. I told it I'd tried.

---

Hollis's study was at the back of the cottage, a narrow room that had once been a pantry and now held about two thousand books, an oak desk, a green-shaded lamp and an old Selectric under a quilted cover, which he used, he'd told me once, for Christmas cards and letters to the editor. A gray waxed coat hung from a hook behind the door, still stiff with last night's weather. The room smelled of tea and paper and the particular dust of a man's lifetime.

He'd said come at noon. When I got there he'd already cleared the desk and set out two mugs.

"You look like something the cat declined to bring in," he said. "Sit. I've read your message four times."

I'd told him on the phone only that I needed a second pair of eyes on the Lydia file. I'd said nothing about the tag in Alan's hand, nothing about Cora or the phone bill. I'd had the odd, closed feeling in my chest that you get at a door you've walked through all your life, and I hadn't examined why, because I didn't want the answer.

I opened the laptop and brought up the scan. Thirty-one pages. I turned the screen toward him and scrolled to the seventh.

He read it the way he read everything, slowly, lips pressed, one finger moving down the margin without touching the glass. The blocky type. The smudged capitals. Three paragraphs summarizing an interview with Alan Farrow, and then the sentence I'd been staring at for two days: *There is no evidence of third-party involvement.*

"I never wrote this," I said. "I remember the interview. I remember what Alan said, and it isn't what this says. He was shaking. He wouldn't look at me. I wrote that down. It's on page six, in my own words. This is a different page, in a different hand, that agrees with a conclusion I hadn't yet reached."

"Hm." He scrolled up, then down. He went back to the signature. "That's your signature."

"It looks like my signature."

"It looks *exactly* like it." He took his glasses off and pinched the bridge of his nose. "Theo, I have to say something that you won't like, and I'd rather say it now than have you hear it from Castellano. You were six months sober then. Six months is a raw age. You did a hundred things that year you don't remember doing. You wrote me a postcard from Bangor I've still got, and you've never been to Bangor."

"This isn't a postcard."

"No." He put the glasses back on. "No, it isn't. I know it isn't. I'm only saying: before we decide somebody forged a page, let's agree what we'd have to believe. That someone had access to your file. Knew your signature. Had a typewriter and a reason." He tapped the page with one finger. "That font, now. Do you know what that is?"

"I thought maybe an old Atlantic template."

"It's Courier. A poor man's Courier, run through a bad ribbon. Atlantic used it on addenda in the eighties and nineties. Any adjuster who'd been there before the redesign would have the form in a drawer." He looked up, and his face was gentle and clear. "I could produce that page myself in ten minutes, if I had to. So could Alan's lawyer. So could Desmond Reyes, who started in the mail room."

I looked at the quilted shape on the stand across the room, and it was only a typewriter. A man with a typewriter in his study is not a clue. A man with a typewriter in his study is a man who writes Christmas cards.

"Who else saw the file?" he asked.

"Reyes had it in the archive. Pryor, the ME, saw the autopsy portion. Tarbell gave the statement."

"Ned Tarbell." Hollis nodded slowly. "The harbormaster. I'd talk to Ned. He's a good man, a bit slow, and he's afraid of the sea, which is a strange thing for a harbormaster. If he signed something he didn't read, he'll know." He closed the laptop gently and slid it back to me. "And Theo, I'd keep this between us for now. Reyes especially. Don't tell him what you've found. A man who sends the file at 9:12 and calls at 9:12 is a man who's thought about it."

I stared at him. "How did you know it was 9:12?"

It came out too fast. I heard it in the room.

"Reyes, I expect," Hollis said, with no pause at all. "He rang me Friday night. Or somebody did, Theo, I've had a houseful of telephone calls since Alan, and half of them were Port Carrow's finest asking me to be quiet." He smiled, a little rueful. "My memory's gone to rags. Forgive me."

I hadn't told him. Nell had told me, in the county building, after I'd left his table. But Reyes had phoned Hollis the night before, and Reyes was proud of the minute, and Reyes was exactly the kind of man to say *I sent it the very second.* It was a small thing. It was nothing. I'd have said it myself.

"It's all right," I said.

"I'm an old fool," said Hollis. He stirred his tea. I never took sugar either.

---

The Cutter's Rest was the one diner in Port Carrow, and on a Sunday afternoon it held a few fishermen, a table of church ladies and one man in a corner booth with his back to the wall.

I knew he was Voss before he stood up. I'd never met him, but you can tell the ex-cops by how they take up a booth. He was in his fifties, thick through the shoulders and thinner everywhere else, in a tweed jacket that had been good during a previous administration. His hair was cut short by someone who did not love him. He had a coffee, a half-eaten slice of pie, and in front of him, face-up on the table, a business card.

"Barrett Voss," he said. "I'd offer a hand, but I'm told you don't trust mine."

"Who told you that?"

"My client. Sit down, Mr. Brandt, before the ladies in the corner decide I'm arresting you."

I sat. The waitress came, and I ordered coffee. When she'd gone, he slid the card an inch across the table. BARRETT VOSS, INVESTIGATIONS. A Portland number and no slogan.

"Cora hired me in June," he said. "Before her father died, which I want you to note, because the Staties will make a meal of it. She wanted someone to look at her mother's death. She'd found a name in her father's papers, and she'd got it into her head that someone was lying about Lydia. I told her what I tell everyone. Eighty percent of the time, nobody's lying. The other twenty, it's the person you least want it to be."

"What did you find?"

"Mostly a closed file, with your name on it." He said it without heat. "It was a good one, I'll give you that. Complete. Clean. Well documented. I read it in a motel in Camden and I thought, this man's careful. Then I got to the harbormaster's statement and I thought, this man is *very* careful, and I didn't like it."

"Why not?"

"Because it's perfect." He put down his fork. "A witness who saw the victim aboard alone, drinking, the afternoon before. Who happens to be the one man in the harbor positioned to see it. Who's never been interviewed on anything else in thirty years. Who signed in blue-black ink, a month after the signature was typed. That kind of statement doesn't happen. It gets made."

I said nothing. I was thinking of page seven.

"I tried Tarbell in August," Voss went on. "He wouldn't see me. Sent his wife to the door. Said the matter was closed and it upset him. Then, in September, I'm in the Rite Aid parking lot in Rockland, and he walks past my car with a bag of pills, and he looks at me like he's seen a dead man in a window. Twenty seconds. Then he's gone." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn. "Something's eating that man. He wants to say it, and he can't, and I'm the wrong person to say it to. You're not."

"Why would he talk to me?"

"Because you signed the file. If he lied, he lied to you, in a way. Men like that get a sense of obligation." He leaned back. "Also, because he's scared of cops and scared of me. He isn't scared of you yet. Use that."

"I've been told to stay out of this."

"By the nice detective? She'll tell you the same thing she told me. She's right, and it won't help." He finished the pie in two bites. "One more thing. Not free advice, but it's on the house. Somebody's been behind me for a week."

"Behind you."

"Gray Buick. Maine plate, rental sticker. Parked across from my motel in Rockland for two days. Followed me up Route 1 on Thursday and back on Saturday. I lost him once, at the Dunkin' in Thomaston, and found him again in the same spot, which tells me he knew where I was going." He wiped his mouth. "He doesn't know who I'm following, but he knows I'm following. I thought you ought to know it's getting crowded."

"Why tell me?"

"Because Cora says you're not the one who killed her father," said Voss. "And Cora has a very good nose for liars. She's had practice."

I thought about that for a moment. "She told me this morning she doesn't care who says I can't talk to her."

"Did she?" He seemed pleased. "Then she's decided you're useful. Same thing, with her."

He stood, dropped a ten on the table and picked up his hat, which was an actual hat. "Tarbell lives on Pier Road, the green house with the dory in the yard. Go before dark. He eats at five, and he goes to bed with the radio. After seven he won't open the door." He looked at me levelly. "And Brandt. Whatever you've got in that jacket, the one you keep touching, I'd put it somewhere safer than your pocket."

I hadn't noticed I was touching it.

---

I went to Pier Road. It was ten past four. The green house stood at the end of the road with its back to the water: a narrow clapboard house, shutters closed, a battered dory propped on blocks in the yard with ferns growing out of the bow. A woman in a cardigan came to the door and told me, through the chain, that Ned was lying down. She didn't ask who I was. She looked at my face, and I saw that she knew.

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "Please. In the morning, when he's himself."

"Mrs. Tarbell, it matters."

"It's mattered for six years." She shut the door. It was very quiet.

I stood in the yard with the wind coming off the harbor. The dory's name was painted on the transom in white: *Second Chance.* The boat in the yard looked as if it hadn't been in the water in a very long time. I wondered what it was to name a boat for a thing you didn't believe in.

My phone rang as I got back to the car. The screen said GUS CHANDLERY.

"Theo." Gus's voice was thick and careful, like a man walking on ice. "You need to come home. Somebody's been in your office."

"When?"

"I don't know. I was out front doing the Sunday tally, and I saw your door was open. Frame's cracked." He swallowed. "They didn't take the computer. They didn't take the cash in the drawer. I've been up there twenty minutes. Whoever it was, they went straight to the closet."

I closed my eyes. "The banker's box."

"Under the buckets. Yes." A pause. "How'd you know?"

"What's missing, Gus?"

"The box is gone," said Gus. "And Theo, I looked around. Nothing else is. Not your files. Not your pens. They left it all. They just took that one box. The one with the stamps in it."

I stood in a stranger's yard with a dead dory at my back. I wasn't thinking *who.* I was thinking about the day. They knew which closet, which box, and that it mattered before I'd said a word about it to anyone.

Somewhere behind the green house, in the dusk, a radio came on.
