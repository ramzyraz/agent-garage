# Chapter 7: Unit 114

The message was from Hollis.

I listened to it on the stairs outside Quill's office, with the old lawyer's door shut behind me and the smell of lemon polish still in my coat. Hollis never texted if he could talk. His voice came out of the phone slow and rough and kind, with a kettle ticking in the background.

"Theo. It's me. I've just heard about poor Ned Tarbell. Eddie Marsh rang me, he thought I'd want to know. I don't know what to say. I'm sorry you had to be the one who talked to him, if you did, I don't know that you did." A breath. "Don't sit in that room at the Anchor with the television. Come and eat. I've got a haddock chowder that'll stand a spoon up. Ring me when you can. Any hour."

I stood there with the phone against my ear after it ended. I'd spent five years ringing that number in every kind of weather. Quill's wall behind me was cold through my coat, and my first instinct, the one that was older than the envelope and older than the ledger and older than the sober man I'd made out of the wreck, was to call it back and tell him everything.

*Not even the people who love you. Especially them.*

I typed: *Portland today. Back tomorrow, will call.* I read it twice. It was true, and it told him nothing. I sent it and put the phone in my pocket and felt, for no reason I could have defended, like a man who has just stepped over a wire.

---

Nell took the photograph the way she'd taken the letter. She looked at it for a long time with no expression at all, and then she looked at me.

We were in the lot behind Quill's building, and she'd driven down from Port Carrow in ninety minutes on a road I'd have taken in two hours. She stood by her unmarked Ford with her hands in her jacket pockets and the print held flat against the roof, shielded from the drizzle by her body. The ledger lay under her thumb. *T.B. T.B. T.B.*

"I should arrest you for this," she said.

"I know."

"Three dead people. Three claims. Your initials down the whole column, in somebody's neat little hand." She tapped the margin. "And thirty. Thirty percent of a claim is a motive any prosecutor could read aloud to a jury with one eyebrow up."

"The adjuster doesn't get a percentage. The adjuster gets a salary and a pension plan and a Christmas ham."

"The adjuster gets whatever the adjuster decides to get. You know that better than I do." She lifted her eyes. "You also came here and handed it to me. A man building his own alibi wouldn't hand me this."

"A man building a *second* alibi might."

That surprised a laugh out of her, small and furious. She put the print into a clear evidence sleeve from her pocket, wrote on it with a marker, and tucked it inside her jacket. "I'm not charging you with anything today. I'm keeping this. I'm keeping the letter copy. I'll have the lab look at the paper, and the pencil, and that lamp." She tilted her head. "That's a green glass shade. Banker's lamp or one of the old library ones. Mean anything?"

I looked at the blur at the top of the photograph. A curve of bottle-colored glass, out of focus, the kind of detail a man's mind wants to fill with a meaning.

It meant something. I could feel the shape of the meaning, the way you can feel a word on the tip of your tongue, and then it was gone, like a fish turning in black water.

"Half the offices in New England have one," I said.

"Half." She didn't believe me, and she let it go. "Quill has paperwork for the unit?"

"A certified death certificate. He was the one who filed her will. He says he can have letters on the estate drawn up by tonight, and an affidavit that I'm her husband and sole heir."

"Good. Then you'll want to be in Rockland at nine tomorrow." She pulled her car door open and stopped. "Tomorrow's the fourteenth."

"I know what day it is."

"I know you do." For a moment she looked at me the way she'd looked at me across the boathouse in the first dusk, a beat too long, like someone reading an old address off an envelope. "I'll bring the manager a letter from the county, so he doesn't give you the runaround, and I'll stand behind you while you open it. I can't search it without a warrant. I can watch a husband go through his wife's things. That's as far as I can bend."

"Why would you bend at all?"

She looked at me.

"Because you didn't run," said Nell Castellano, and got into the car.

---

I didn't sleep. I'd stopped expecting to, the night before the fourteenth. Every year it had been the same: a cheap room somewhere, the radio low, a bottle I didn't buy sitting in my head like a neighbor who never goes home. I'd watched dawn come up over parking lots in four states. This year I sat on the bed at a motel off Route 1 in Rockland, because I wasn't ready to lose the half-hour of the drive, and I read Mara's letter again by the light of the television with the sound off.

*Don't tell anyone. Not even the people who love you. Especially them.*

I'd read it forty times, and I understood now what I had been refusing to understand. A warning like that has a shape. You don't write *especially them* about a stranger. You write it because there is someone, a particular someone, close enough to ask.

I made a list on the motel pad. *Reyes. Hollis. Nell. Quill. Dolores.* I looked at it for a long time and then I tore the sheet off and ate my own handwriting, a habit from the bad years. It tasted of paper and nothing else.

The rain stopped at four. At five the first trucks started down Route 1.

---

Midcoast Secure Storage sat behind a chain-link fence on the Thomaston road, three long buildings with orange doors and a yard of crushed shell. It was a gray morning, a sea-smoke kind of morning, and the sky hung low enough to touch the roof. The same clerk was at the counter, a boy in a fleece with a nametag that said *DAVE*, and a heavier man came out of the back when he saw me, with the face of someone who has spent his life dealing with other people's regrets by the square foot.

"Mr. Brandt." He glanced past me. "And this is the detective."

"Detective Sergeant Castellano. Maine State Police." Nell laid her letter on the counter, the county seal at the bottom, and then Quill's papers on top of it, one after another: the certificate with its raised stamp, the letters, the affidavit with a notary's blue ink. The manager read each one with his lips moving. He was not a man who liked to be rushed and he was not a man who liked to be seen to be rushed.

"Unit's in good standing through the thirty-first," he said finally. "Paid cash, in advance, same as every year. Year after year, I mean. Five years." He raised his eyebrows. "Some of these tenants, I wouldn't have known the owner was dead if you'd not told me."

"Five years of cash," Nell said. "Who brought it?"

"It came in an envelope, by mail. Our own return envelope. No letter." He shrugged. "Postmarked Portland."

I felt Nell not look at me.

"Quill," I said.

"I'd guess a standing order." She turned to the manager. "Mr. Brandt will open the unit. I'll stand in the doorway. You'll stay out here, and you won't touch the door until we're done."

He agreed to all of it. He gave us the gate code on a slip, and I took it. It was six digits, a birthday I didn't recognize and then did: Mara's mother's, a date in April.

We drove in. B row. Orange door. A padlock with a brass tag on the shackle that matched, in a way my eyes couldn't believe, the key in my hand.

I'd carried that key for four days. I'd put it beside my car keys every night. I'd thought about it in the shower. Now I stood in the cold shell-gravel yard with the sea-smoke blowing between the buildings and I put it in the lock, and the lock was oiled, and turned without a sound.

That was the first strange thing. A lock five years old doesn't turn like that.

"Theo," said Nell behind me. It was the first time she'd used my first name.

"I know."

"Don't go in yet. Look at the floor."

I lifted the door. It rolled up on a chain with a long metallic complaint, and the dim light of the yard fell across a ten-by-ten concrete room.

---

She had taken the unit with a good eye for order. Mara had always been the kind of person who made a map before she went anywhere.

Banker's boxes in three tidy stacks, each labeled in blue felt-tip on a strip of masking tape. A folding card table. One chair. A desk lamp with a cord that didn't reach any outlet. A square of green carpet on the concrete. A small oil heater, unplugged. And on the far wall, pinned with thumbtacks, a map of the Maine coast with Port Carrow circled in red.

I stood in the doorway and could not make myself cross the threshold. It smelled of cardboard and cold. It smelled faintly of her: of the lemon-ginger tea she'd drunk by the quart, of the cedar-chip sachets she put in drawers. The smell was an old photograph.

"Take your time," Nell said, quietly. "I'll be here."

Two things were wrong, and I saw both of them at once, because I had been taught to see what's wrong in a room before I let myself see anything else.

The first was the floor. There were prints in the dust. Not the deep dust of five years, though there was some of that, a gray bloom along the base of the walls. The prints were around the table and in front of the stacks, and they were small and smeared by a soft sole, and they were not old.

The second was the shelf of boxes at the left. There were four stacks of boxes along the back wall, with a stencil of a dusty outline in the fourth: a rectangle, a box's width, where the dust ended on the concrete and clean gray began. A box had stood there, and been taken, and not long ago.

I didn't say anything. Nell stepped in behind me and crouched without touching, her flashlight on the clean rectangle. Then she looked at the prints, and then at me.

"Someone's been in here."

"Someone with the code," I said, "or a key."

"The manager said no one's been in through the office, as far as he knows." She stood. "I'm going to ask him for the gate log."

"Nell."

"I know," she said. "You want to look first. Go on. Don't touch anything you don't have to. Photograph everything where it lies."

I went in.

---

Mara's handwriting was on every label, and it was the worst thing I have ever read.

I'd carried her letter for four days and it had been a voice in a quiet room, a single page I could fold and put away. This was a library of her. *DOYLE, P. — CLAIM / FIELD.* *ANSELM, R.* *FARROW, L. — COMPLETE.* Beneath them, shorter labels: *TIPS — SENT.* *FONTS.* *ME REPORTS.* *CLOSING LANGUAGE — 4 OF 4.* *PRYOR.* *TARBELL (DRAFT).*

I lifted the lid on *FARROW, L.* The copy was a photocopy of the claim file with every page numbered in the corner in her small, square hand, and in the margins, little pencil notes, the way she'd annotated paperbacks and recipes and the backs of my business cards. *Same typeface as Doyle p.4. Check. Same ME. Same closing line. "No evidence of third-party involvement." Four files, four times.* She had drawn a little wave beside it, the kind she drew in the margins of everything she cared about.

And on page seven, which I had read on a scan in a hotel parking lot with my heart going like a trapped bird, she had written one line, in red.

*This is not his hand. His capitals lean. Whoever forged this has copied the signature but not the habit.*

I read it, and then I understood the thing I hadn't been able to say to Nell on the phone, or to Quill, or even to myself.

She had known. In October, five years ago, my wife had sat at a card table in a rented concrete room and compared my handwriting to a forgery of it, line by line, with a magnifying glass, and she had concluded that I was innocent. And then she had gone home and tried to tell me, and lost her nerve halfway.

I put the lid back on very carefully, as if it could break.

"Theo." Nell, from the door. She had a printout from the manager's office in her hand, a single sheet. Her voice was flat in a way I had learned to listen to. "Gate log."

"Tell me."

"Entry on the ninth of October. Eleven fifty-two p.m. Mara's code. Out at twelve-oh-six." She held my eyes. "Fourteen minutes. Last Friday night. The night after Alan Farrow died."

Fourteen minutes, to take one box.

I looked at the clean rectangle on the concrete. I looked at the labels, and I read them again, all of them, in order, with the care you give a will. And at the end of the third stack, where the sequence ought to have ended, I saw what was missing, because Mara was a woman who labeled everything in order, and there were boxes for *FONTS* and *ME* and *TARBELL (DRAFT)*, and no box for the final thing.

There was only the dust outline. And there, in her handwriting, on a strip of masking tape that had come off the missing box and curled to the floor, I read the label.

*OCTOBER.*

---

It took me an hour to go through the rest. Nell didn't leave the doorway, and I was grateful. She didn't speak. She wrote nothing down. She held the flashlight steady and said *there* when something caught the light.

There was a spiral notebook in the *TIPS* box. A year's worth of anonymous letters to the state attorney general, to Atlantic's ethics line, to a reporter in Bangor, all unanswered, all copied in her hand with the date and the postage. *Sent 9/22. Sent 10/1. No reply.* The last was dated September 30.

And there was a single index card, thumbtacked to the inside of the lid of a box labeled *H.*, which I hadn't seen at first because the card had faded to the color of tea.

I read it under the flashlight with a feeling I can only describe as vertigo.

*Oct 5. Told T. He'll know what to do.*

*H. — authorizes payment on all four. Has H. ever read a file? Ask who put them on T.'s desk.*

I read it twice. A third time. I turned the card over and there was nothing on the back.

"Told T.," said Nell, behind me. She had read it over my shoulder. Her voice was very careful. "That's you."

"Her letter says she lost her nerve. Sunday. Halfway."

"The card says Monday. The fifth." She was quiet. "She tried Sunday and failed, and then she did it Monday. She *told* you, Theo. Whatever it was. She told you the next day, and she wrote it down, and she believed you knew what to do about it."

"I don't remember."

"I know."

"I don't remember any of it." My voice cracked on it, in the cold, in the smell of cedar and cardboard. "Nell, it was the fifth. It was a Monday. I must have gone to work. I must have eaten dinner. I must have—" I stopped. "I was sober that week. I was sober till the sixth or the seventh. I would remember."

"Would you?" she said, very gently, which was worse than if she'd shouted.

*H.* I looked at the single letter. The H might be Hollis. It might be Hal Rouse, the CFO, whose signature was on every claim payment at Atlantic and whose name nobody ever said aloud without the word *Hal* first, with a kind of nervous fondness. It might be Harbormaster, which was what Tarbell had been called to his face. It might be a hundred things. A single letter, in a woman's careful hand.

*Ask who put them on T.'s desk.*

I held the card against my chest. She had told me, and written it down, and gone on believing I would know what to do. And I had done something, or nothing, and five years later I couldn't say which.

"Theo," said Nell. "What did you do on the fifth of October?"

And standing in my dead wife's rented room, with her handwriting on every wall, I opened my mouth to answer, and found nothing there at all: not a street, not a meal, not a face. Only a white, smooth, blank place, like the surface of the sea after something has gone down into it.
