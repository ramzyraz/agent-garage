# Chapter 2: Slack Water

The Rockland office of Midcoast Secure Storage opened at nine, and I was standing in its parking lot at 8:52 with the wipers going and a key in my pocket that felt heavier every time I touched it.

The place was a gravel yard behind a chain-link fence, four long rows of orange roll-up doors under a sky the color of dishwater. A keypad stood on a post at the gate. I tried 1-1-4, because I'm not above trying. The keypad blinked red and told me nothing.

At nine exactly a woman in a fleece vest unlocked the office door from the inside. She had a lanyard, a space heater, and the expression of someone who has told a great many people no.

"Unit 114," I said, and put the brass key on the counter.

She looked at the key, then at me, then typed. "That's a B row. Tenant of record is Mara Brandt."

"My wife."

"Is she here?"

I'd practiced a few answers in the car. None of them survived the question. "She passed away. Five years ago this month."

The woman's face did something small and decent. She took her hands off the keyboard. "I'm sorry. I really am." Then she put them back. "It's prepaid. The whole term, in one payment, through the end of this month. Cash, it says. That's unusual."

"It was a long time ago."

"I can't open it for you, Mr. Brandt. I'd need your ID, a certified copy of the death certificate, and either letters of administration or a signed release from whoever's handling the estate. Corporate's strict. We had a thing with a divorce." She said it the way people mention a plane crash. "And the gate code's only given to the tenant of record, or the estate. Not to the key."

"I have the key."

"Everybody has a key. Keys are the least secure thing in the world." She slid mine back across the counter. "Bring the paperwork, and I'll walk you down myself. The manager's in Thursday if you want a ruling."

I could have argued. I'm good at arguing with clerks, I do it for a living. But the clock above her head said 9:14, high water was at 11:40, and Port Carrow was an hour and a half south and east, with a storm surge sitting on the bay like a lid.

I said thank you. I took the key. And I looked down row B through the rain, at a hundred orange doors, and picked out the one I thought was 114 and hated it.

Five years she'd had that door. Five years I'd lived two hours from it.

---

I reached the causeway at 10:50. The sea had already made up its mind.

It's a mile and a quarter of single-lane road across the tidal flats, with a concrete rail on the bay side and a line of yellow poles marking where the road is supposed to be when you can't see it. At the mainland end there's a gravel turnout, a sign that says PORT CARROW IS AN ISLAND TWICE A DAY, and a pole with a gauge painted in feet. The gauge said three and a half. The road, forty yards out, had gone under a sheet of brown water that moved sideways, like a river that had lost its way.

A town truck was parked across the lane with its lights going. The man inside lowered his window an inch and shouted through the rain that the surge was running two feet over the table, and that the road would likely open around four, maybe five, if the wind came round.

"Four or five," I said.

"Or six. Or tomorrow. It's the sea, mister."

So I sat in the turnout with the engine off and the rain beating on the roof like a thousand small fingers, and I did what I'd have told anyone else to do. I read the file.

I read all thirty-one pages of it again, slowly, with a pencil, the way Hollis taught me: once for the story, once for the seams. I read the harbormaster's statement, a single typed paragraph over a name I'd forgotten and a signature I hadn't. *Observed the decedent alone aboard the* Salt Ledger *at approximately 1930 hours, with what appeared to be a bottle.* I read the ME's finding: drowning, with a blood alcohol of .18. I read my own summary, my own words, a young man's careful, sober, confident words.

And I read page seven, again and again, until it stopped looking like a page and started looking like a hole.

I couldn't have said when the rain turned to something thinner. I only knew that the gauge dropped a foot, and then another, and that around three o'clock the town truck pulled out of the lane and a man in a slicker walked along the yellow poles with a stick, testing the road the way you'd test a frozen pond.

The causeway opened at 4:20.

By then the light was going. I crossed in the last gray hour with the water still lying in sheets across the flats on both sides of me, flat and bright and dead still. Low tide, the turn between ebb and flood, when the sea stops pretending to make up its mind. Slack water. There's a stillness to it, like a held breath. The old fishermen say it's when the bay will tell you the truth, if you're stupid enough to ask it.

I wasn't asking. I was thinking about a dead woman's handwriting, and a hundred orange doors, and a man called Alan Farrow who'd been found face down in his own boathouse.

Port Carrow came up out of the dusk piece by piece. A white church. A gas pump. A harbor with the lights coming on one by one, and the long gray line of the breakwater running out to the bell buoy.

I didn't look at the breakwater.

I looked at it anyway.

---

The Farrow place stood on the north shore, at the end of a private road lined with rhododendrons that had been planted by someone who expected them to be admired. The house was big and shingled and dark. The boathouse stood below it at the water's edge, a long barn of a building on pilings with its big doors toward the cove, and a yellow ribbon snapping across the path.

A woman was waiting under the boathouse eave with her hands in the pockets of a black rain jacket. She watched me park, get out, and cross the wet lawn, and she did not appear to be in any hurry about any of it.

"You're Brandt," she said.

"Theo."

"Detective Nell Castellano, Maine State Police." She was perhaps thirty-seven, hard-faced in the way of people who sleep badly, with dark hair scraped back and the kind of eyes that make a list. She looked at me a half second longer than strangers do. Then it was gone, filed. "Atlantic Mutual said they'd sent somebody. They didn't say it was the same somebody who signed off on Lydia Farrow."

"They didn't mention it to me either, at first."

"No," she said. "I'll bet they didn't." She lifted the tape. "Don't touch anything. If you do, I'll know, and I'll enjoy it."

"Noted."

"You're allowed one question per minute. I'm in a mood."

I ducked under the ribbon. "Why are you here at all? I thought it was a misadventure."

"It is. Pending." She got out a flashlight and clicked it on. "That's what it says on the paperwork, and the paperwork is what the county medical examiner told us it should say. I'm here because I'm a nosy person who didn't like the door."

She pushed it open.

The boathouse was one big room, perhaps forty feet long, with a slip down the middle where a boat would sit in a normal week. The boat was gone, hauled for the season. What remained was black water, lapping at the pilings, level now with the walkway on either side, and a smell of salt, diesel, damp wood and something sweeter under it all, whisky. A workbench ran along the left-hand wall with a vise, a coil of line, a shelf of paint tins, and a green glass bottle, three-quarters empty, standing next to a heavy tumbler.

A second tumbler sat upside down on the drainboard of the little sink. Dry. Nell's light slid over it and kept going.

"This was the water line last night." She traced the wall with the beam, a stain a foot above her head. "Surge came over the sill about eleven. By midnight it was four feet in the slip and a little over the walkway. By two it was going out again. He went in, or he fell in, or somebody put him in, somewhere in there. The ME says between nine and midnight. He'll say more tomorrow."

"Who found him?"

"His housekeeper. Seven thirty, came to see why the house was dark. Door was shut. She couldn't open it, so she called the neighbor, the neighbor kicked at it for ten minutes, and in the end they got a pry bar. The door was locked from inside." She turned the light to the door we'd come through. "Look."

I looked. It was a heavy plank door, hung on iron strap hinges. On the inside, at about chest height, was a cast-iron hook that dropped into an eye bolted to the frame. A simple old gravity latch. The pry bar had torn the wood around the eye and left the hook hanging, a little bent.

"That's the only way in or out," she said. "The big doors on the cove are bolted from inside with a bar, and they're swollen shut. We checked. The windows are painted shut. He'd have had to walk in here, drop that hook behind him, pour himself a drink and fall in the water. Alone. Which he might've done. He's sixty-one, he had a bottle, he had a bad few months."

"But you don't like the door."

"I don't like any door that closes that neatly." She clicked the light off. In the dark the water made small sounds. "Tell me about the policy."

"Increased from two million to four-two a month ago. Cora Farrow's the beneficiary."

"The daughter. The one who can't stand him." Nell said it without inflection. "You'll want to meet her. Everyone does, sooner or later. She's made it clear she'd like to meet you first." A pause. "Where were you last night, Mr. Brandt?"

"Portland. In my office, under a bucket."

"Anyone with you?"

"A bucket."

"I'll put that in the report." But the corner of her mouth moved, and I thought, for the first time, that she might be funny when she wasn't working. "I'm not accusing you. Yet. I just like to know where everyone was. Where were you before that? The week Lydia Farrow died. Six years ago."

"Portland. Sober, as it happens. I'd interviewed Alan, interviewed the harbormaster, got the ME's report. I wrote it up. It closed."

"It closed." She nodded slowly. "A lot of things in this county close, Mr. Brandt. They close very smoothly."

Something in her voice made me look at her. She met my eyes and gave me nothing.

"Go have dinner," she said. "I know you're going to; Hollis Dunn's been calling every motel on the island since noon. He heard you were coming. He's put a pot on."

"You know Hollis?"

"Everybody knows Hollis." She walked me to the tape. "He's the nicest man in Port Carrow. Mention the boathouse and he'll cry."

---

Hollis Dunn lived in a gray shingled cottage on Quarry Lane, up the hill from the harbor, with a garden he never weeded and a mooring he never skipped. His cutter, *Second Opinion*, rode below the house on the end of a pennant, a small tidy shape in the dusk. He had the porch light on. He was standing in the doorway when my headlights found him, wearing the gray waxed coat that he'd worn for as long as I'd known him, with a dish towel over one shoulder, a pair of reading glasses on his forehead, and the smile that had been getting me through bad nights for a decade.

"Theodore," he said. "You look like a drowned cat."

"I feel like one."

"Good. It builds character. Come in, come in. There's stew, and God help you, there's tea."

It was the kitchen I knew, low-ceilinged, warm, smelling of woodsmoke and thyme, with the same chipped blue kettle on the stove and the same shelf of sailing paperbacks gone soft from the damp. He took my jacket and hung it by the fire, and for a few minutes, I'll admit it, I let myself be looked after. He ladled stew into a bowl and put it in front of me. He stirred his own cup, no sugar, never any sugar, and watched me eat.

He was sixty-eight and looked sixty. White hair that stood up in a crest, big gentle hands, a face made for being kind. He'd taught me everything I knew. He'd driven to Rockport the morning I surfaced, sat with me for six hours in a plastic chair, and never once said *I told you so.*

"It's the anniversary," he said, at last. "Next week."

"Five days."

"I know. I've been thinking about her all week." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "She was the best of us, you know. The rest of us just looked at the numbers. Mara looked at what was wrong with them."

I put down my spoon. I thought of a cream envelope in the inside pocket of my jacket, hanging a yard from the fire. I thought of *Not even the people who love you.* It's an odd thing to find out how much you'd like to confess something. I'd have told him everything in a breath. I wanted to. I could feel it sitting behind my teeth.

"They've sent me on the Farrow thing," I said.

"I heard." He wasn't surprised. "Reyes called me last night. Told me he'd put you on it, said it was about time somebody who'd seen Lydia's file looked at it. I told him I thought it was a terrible idea." He gave me a rueful look. "Not because you wouldn't be good at it. Because you'd be good at it, and it's the week it is, and nobody's looking after you."

"I'm fine, Hollis."

"You're a man who's counted eighteen hundred and twenty days of fine. Let me worry." He got up, poured more tea. "Is it true? The boathouse, the locked door?"

"Locked from inside. Nell Castellano thinks it's too tidy."

"Nell's a good cop." He shook his head. "God, poor Alan. That boathouse. That latch sticks in damp, always did. I told him a dozen times he'd lock himself in one of these days and drown like a rat."

"It's a terrible way to go," I said.

"Isn't it? The cold. The dark." Hollis stared into the stove for a moment, and I saw his eyes were wet. "I'm sorry. I knew him, you see. Not well. We sailed the same waters. He was a hard man, harder than he looked, but he was lonely, and there's no accounting for that." He cleared his throat and gave me the wry half-smile I'd loved for years. "I'm an old fool. Eat your stew."

I ate my stew.

He asked me about the work, and I told him some of it. He asked whether I'd found a way to sleep and I said there was a bucket in the office that helped, and he laughed until he had to take his glasses off. He asked whether I'd been to a meeting this week and I said yes, which was nearly true. He told me about a family of otters that had moved into the cove. He told me about a woman at the library who'd been trying to marry him for a year. He didn't ask what I'd learned about Lydia, or what I'd found in her file, and I noticed, in a vague way I'd think about much later, that he didn't need to.

At nine he walked me out to the porch. The rain had stopped. A skin of cold had come down over the island, and the sky had broken in the west into long bars of black and silver.

"You'll stay at the Anchor Inn?"

"Yes."

"I'd have given you the spare room."

"I know. I wouldn't sleep."

"Nobody sleeps in the spare room. That's why I keep books in it." He put one big hand on my shoulder, and I felt the weight of it, and the warmth. "Theo. You'd tell me, wouldn't you? If there were something you needed to say."

For one second the porch went very quiet.

"Of course," I said.

"Good." He squeezed my shoulder and let go. "Go to bed. The sea will still be there in the morning."

---

My phone rang as I got to the car. A number I didn't know, with a Maine State Police prefix.

"It's Castellano," said the voice. "Where are you?"

"Quarry Lane. Leaving Hollis's."

"Don't go to the motel. Come to the county building on Mill Street. Around the back, there's a loading door. I'll be there." She didn't say please. "I need you to look at something."

"What?"

"Something that was in Alan Farrow's hand."

"His hand?"

"His right fist." Her voice was low and entirely level, which was how I knew she was frightened. "We didn't see it at the scene. They'd closed his hand around it, and it took the morgue to open it. It's a brass evidence tag, the old kind, with a string. A little stamped number and a name." She waited. "I'm told you'd know the style."

Behind me the porch light was still on. Behind that, in the shape of the window, Hollis stood with his tea, small and gray and gentle, watching me go.

"What does it say?" I asked.

"It says *T. Brandt, oh-four-one-seven.*"

I found myself sitting on the wet hood of the car, cold seeping through my trousers. The voice in the phone was ice-clear. I knew that tag, the stamp of it, the exact fall of the numbers. Years ago, in a different life, I'd stamped that format on every exhibit I'd ever logged.

Tag 0417 had been Lydia Farrow's. The ship's log, in a sealed bag, in my own handwriting.

And the file I'd signed said it had never left my office.
