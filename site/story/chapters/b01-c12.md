# Chapter 12: Retained Sample

The causeway was closed when I got to it.

I should have known. I had a tide table in my head the way other men have a bad knee, and somewhere between Rockland and the Neck road, with Hal Rouse's whisper still going round in the car, I had stopped consulting it. High water was 5:50. It was ten past six. A sawhorse stood across the road at the mainland end with a reflective sign hung on it, ROAD CLOSED — TIDE, and beyond it the causeway ran out flat and dark into a sheet of water that shone like a tray. The low point was gone. The gauge post stood up out of the sea with its yellow feet numbered to nowhere.

Port Carrow sat out there with its lights coming on. Two hours, the sign said, give or take the wind.

I parked in the gravel lot of the Neck Texaco, the last building on the mainland, and turned the engine off, and for a while I did nothing. I'd been told to eat. There was a rack of sandwiches in cellophane through the window. I didn't move.

*Who did he tell, after me?*

I tried it the way you'd try a door in the dark, with the flat of the hand. Monday the fifth, four in the afternoon. A low tan chair. I was sober, Rouse said, white as paper. And then: nothing. Not a wall, not a fog. An absence so smooth that the edges of it didn't hurt, which was the worst of it. I'd been looking for five years for the place where my memory broke, and it turned out to have been cut with a very clean blade.

A knuckle rapped the glass beside my head.

Nell stood in the rain with her hood up and a manila envelope under her jacket. I hadn't heard the Ford.

"Wiscasset to here in forty minutes," she said when I got the window down. "You didn't call from the lobby."

"I called from the street."

"I said the lobby." She went round and got into the passenger seat and shook the water off her hood onto my mat. "Your face looks like somebody read you your own obituary. Don't tell me yet. Eat first."

"Nell."

"Eat." She put a sandwich in my hand. She'd bought it before she knocked. Egg salad. I looked at it and then I ate it, all of it, in the dark car with the windows steaming up and the sea hissing against the end of the road, and she watched me do it with the grim attention of somebody supervising a dose.

---

"Rouse says I went to see him," I said, when I'd finished. "Five years ago. Monday the fifth. I asked him how to report fraud inside Atlantic."

She didn't say anything. Her hands were flat on her knees.

"I told him Mara had proof. He told me to be careful who I told. That's what he said and he thought it was kind." I rubbed my eyes. "I don't remember a minute of it, and I don't remember where I went when I walked out, and he thinks that whoever I told next is the one person who's had five years to look at me and not wonder."

"And who did you tell?"

"I don't know."

It was the truth. That was what made it possible to say it in her face. It was the second thing I had said to her that day that was, in the precise and literal sense, true.

She was quiet a long moment. Out beyond the sawhorse a light on a buoy came round and went out and came round.

"You said *Nobody,*" she said. "About Quill's appointment. When I asked who knew."

"Yes."

"I'm going to ask you once more, because I like you, and because you've just told me something that cost you. Was that true?"

"I told you I didn't remember who I told in October five years ago."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Nell nodded slowly, as though I'd confirmed a measurement. "All right." She took the envelope from under her jacket and set it on the dashboard between us. "Then I'll tell you something, and you can decide what to do with it, and we'll both pretend we're still doing this the clean way."

---

She'd had the idea on Tuesday night, she said, sitting on the floor of her kitchen with the Rockland boxes spread out in photographs. Mara's folder marked ME REPORTS held copies of four death certificates and four autopsy summaries, and every one of them, when you laid them side by side, had the same shape. A short narrative. A cause. A line near the bottom, in the same careful typeface: *Toxicology: negative.*

"Four of them," Nell said. "And Mara's own, which she couldn't have copied, because she was the body. I got that one myself, from the county, in September. Pryor's report. *Drowning, accidental. Toxicology: negative.*" She tapped the envelope. "Except it's got a lab number. Every autopsy from his office gets a lab number, it's how the tox gets matched to the case. I ran that number. It doesn't exist. Nobody in this state ever filed a toxicology request on your wife."

I looked at the envelope and didn't touch it.

"So I asked what else exists," said Nell. "She didn't die in the water. Not on paper. A fisherman pulled her out at the breakwater and a volunteer rescue crew took her to the Port Carrow clinic and the clinic put her in a transport to Pen Bay, because there was a pulse, for about eleven minutes. Did you know that?"

"I knew she'd been alive when they took her out." My voice sounded odd in the car. "I didn't know it was eleven minutes."

"At Pen Bay, the ER draws blood on anything that comes in wet. It's routine. They run alcohol and a basic screen and hold the tube, and they hold it a long time, because of lawyers. Five years, in the cold room, in a rack, by date." She breathed out. "Nobody ever asked them for it. Nobody even remembered there'd been a draw. I got a judge to sign a paper Monday morning and I drove it to Rockport myself. They found it Tuesday. Augusta ran it yesterday. I got the result at four o'clock, in the lot behind the county building, while you were in a brown glass tower being told you had a gap."

She opened the envelope. There was one sheet in it. She held it so I could read it by the glow of the gas-station sign.

I'm not a chemist. I read the line she showed me three times. A drug name, a unit, a number. *Present.*

"Lorazepam," said Nell. "Same class as what was in Quill. Not the horse dose. About what you'd give a man at a dentist's to make him stop caring. In a woman that size, forty minutes before the draw, maybe an hour. Maybe less."

The windshield was fogging again. I put a finger to the glass and drew a line down it, and it ran.

"Mara didn't sleep," I said.

"What?"

"The last month. She wasn't sleeping. She'd get up at three and sit in the kitchen with the files. I'd find the light on." I heard myself and stopped, and made myself go on. "If she took something. If she was frightened and she took one—"

"She had no prescription." Nell said it gently. "I pulled her pharmacy history. Nothing for ten years, not a Tylenol PM. And she never took pills. She didn't like what they did to her head. She told me that on a boat, once, with a seasick patch on her neck, because she'd rather be sick than fuzzy."

"You *knew* her."

I hadn't meant it to come out like that. Nell's face did something I hadn't seen it do. It didn't crumple, it just stopped holding.

"We met on a committee," she said. "Drowning prevention. Lifejacket loaners at the public landings, a stupid worthy thing with bake sales. Six years ago. She used to bring lemon bars and apologize for them." The corner of her mouth moved. "She called me the second week of October. The week she died. She wanted to know whether a state detective could take a complaint about insurance fraud, if it was inside a company and the company wouldn't hear it. I said bring me paper. She said she'd have it in a few days." She stared at the sea. "She never came. And on the sixteenth I was told by my lieutenant, who was kind, that a drowning on the breakwater was closed and I was not to spend my time on a friend's grief. That's how he said it. *Your friend's grief.* Like it was my disease."

"You never said."

"Would you have wanted a cop who was grieving in your hotel room?" She folded the sheet and put it back. "I'm saying it now. I wanted you to know what I'm bringing you, and who it's from, and that I haven't forgotten any of it."

I took a long time with the next question.

"What does it prove?"

"Less than you want." She said it at once, the way she'd have said it to a jury. "It proves your wife had a benzodiazepine in her blood when she went into the water, and nobody prescribed it, and the man who signed her death certificate said he'd tested for it when he hadn't. That's all. It doesn't say who gave it to her. A defense lawyer will say she was terrified and took her sister's pills. It doesn't say she was put in the water. It doesn't even say the drug was the reason she drowned. A woman who's been given a sedative and walks into the sea on her own is still a drowning, and a good lawyer will remind the jury of that."

"But."

"But Pryor lied on the page." She put her finger on the dashboard. "That's the knot. He lied on her report and he lied on Tarbell's and he was sweating through his shirt in front of Quill's car this afternoon. Somebody's got his name on a ledger of their own. And every man who lies like that has one night where it stops being worth it."

---

The causeway sawhorse had a man at it now, a volunteer in a slicker with a flashlight, watching the water the way a groundskeeper watches a game. It had stopped raining. The tide was making its last long slack push at the bottom of the road.

"Tell me about Hollis," Nell said.

I didn't turn my head. "What about him?"

"He rang me. About twenty minutes before you pulled in. I'd turned my phone back on." She sounded careful, as though stepping over something. "He was so worried. He'd heard from somebody at the county that you'd gone to Portland and he wanted to make sure you ate. He asked if I'd told you about Mara's—" She stopped. "I had never said a word to him about a sample. I'd told no one but a judge and a lab tech. I said, *what about Mara?* and he said, oh, only that you'd be thinking of her, with Wednesday just gone. And he laughed at himself and said he was an old woman. And that was all it was."

"That's all it was," I said.

"That's all it was." She looked at me. "It's probably all it was. Port Carrow is six hundred people and a county building with thin walls. I just thought I'd tell somebody I think too much."

*Well. There you are, then.*

*That latch sticks in damp.*

*Nine-twelve.*

Every one of them had a plain reason. I could have lined them up in the glovebox, a row of small excuses, each of them reasonable, each of them mine. I had given them reasons for a week as a man keeps feeding a stove that's going out. I didn't look at Nell. I looked at the water, and I thought of the green cardigan and the kettle, of a man with a blue sailor's anchor on his wrist who'd driven me to my first meeting and sat in the folding chair beside me in the church basement and held my coat, and I said:

"He's worried. That's what he does."

"It is," said Nell, after a moment. She put her hand on the door. She didn't open it. "I'm going to say one thing and then I'll leave you alone. You've got a cutter key in your pocket, haven't you? I saw you take it out at Mara's unit, to look at it, the way people finger a coin."

My hand was in my pocket. It had been in my pocket the whole time, around a brass key on a bit of tarred string.

"Don't use it," she said, "without me." And she got out, and shut the door softly, and went back across the lot to the Ford.

---

The volunteer lifted the sawhorse at twenty to eight.

I'd started the engine and put the car in gear when my phone lit on the passenger seat. Port Carrow exchange. A landline. I let it ring three times, and then I picked it up and said nothing, and there was a breath on the other end, wet and quick, and the click, click, click of a ballpoint pen.

"Mr. Brandt," said Ansel Pryor.

"Doctor."

"Are you on the road?" It was hardly a voice. It sounded as though it were being held up by the person speaking. "Are you alone?"

"Why?"

"Because the detective's been to see me," he said. "At the county building, this afternoon. And she held a piece of paper in her hand and she didn't say anything, she only held it, and I watched her not say anything for about a minute, and I haven't been able to sit down since." The pen stopped. Started. "I've been a coward for nine years. I'd like to stop. I'd like to do it tonight, before I find that I don't."

"Then talk to Nell. She's ten minutes from you."

"No." It came back at once, like a blow. "Not her. Not the county. Not anyone in a uniform. They have— I don't know who they have. I'm saying it plainly, Mr. Brandt, I don't know. I've done things for these people. They're not people who need uniforms." He swallowed. "You were the adjuster. You were the *name.* I'll tell you what I know about your wife, and your file, and the others, and the person who paid me. You first. Then you can take me to whomever you like."

"Who paid you?"

"Not on a phone."

"Where?"

"Tonight." The pen again. "I'm at my house. Gannet Road, the white one, past the gravel pit. I'll leave the porch light on. Come after eleven, when the street's asleep, and come on foot from the church, and for God's sake don't let anyone see you." I heard a door somewhere, or a gate. "And Mr. Brandt. Don't tell anyone you're coming. I mean that. Not a soul."

"I've no one to—"

"Yes, you do," said Pryor, very softly. "That's the point. You always have."

The line went dead.

The sawhorse was gone. The causeway ran out ahead of me, flat and black and glittering, with the lights of the town at the end of it like a thing seen under water. I sat with my hands on the wheel, and the cutter key pressed its small hard shape into my thigh, and for the first time in five years I understood that I had a decision to make about the one telephone number I'd never had to think about at all.

At the middle of the road, where the low point dipped, my phone lit once more on the seat. *Hollis.* A text, as it had always been, in the careful full sentences of a man who never learned to abbreviate.

*Kettle's on. Come and tell me what they've done to you in Portland. I'll leave the light on.*

I read it twice at forty miles an hour with the black water standing on both sides of me, a foot from the wheels, and then I put the phone face down on the seat and did not answer.
