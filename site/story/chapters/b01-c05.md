# Chapter 5: The Statement

I called Nell from the car, before I had time to talk myself out of it.

"Castellano."

"Somebody broke into my office in Portland this afternoon," I said. "They took the kit I use to stamp exhibits. The one that made the tag in Alan Farrow's hand."

The line was quiet long enough that I checked the screen. Then: "You're telling me this now."

"I'm telling you the hour I found out."

"You told me last night your kit was in the closet. That was a statement. Now it's gone, and you'd like me to hear it as bad luck."

"I'd like you to hear it as what it is. Whoever did it wants you to think I made that tag last week, and nobody can say I didn't." I started the engine for the heat. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm asking you to send somebody to look at the door."

"Portland PD has a car on it already. Your landlord called it in." Another pause. "Mr. Brandt, I want you to hear how that sounds from my side. A man who's the only suspect with a reason to say 'someone's framing me' gets framed on cue."

"Yes."

"I also think a man who wanted to hide a kit would have burned it, not left an open door and a cracked frame for the whole street." She exhaled. "Tuesday. Nine a.m. Don't make me come find you."

"Understood."

"And Brandt." Something in her voice shifted, as if she'd put down a heavy thing and picked up a lighter one. "Sleep."

I didn't, much. I ate a bowl of chowder at the Anchor Inn standing up, and went to bed at nine, and lay awake with the radio from Pier Road still going in my head, a thin tune under a man's snoring. At two I got up and checked the chain. It was on.

---

Dolores called at ten past eight on Monday. I was already in the car on Pier Road, a hundred yards short of the green house, with a bag of doughnuts I'd bought so I'd have something in my hands.

"I found it," she said, "and I wish I hadn't."

"Tell me."

"The binder's in the archive, in a banker's box marked ADMIN OVERFLOW, Q1. Right where I said. Fourth cage from the back." She was whispering. I could hear a fan. "I went in at seven, before the day crew. The pink sheets are all there, Theo. January, February. Then March first, March second, March third, March fourth. Then March sixth."

"Not the fifth."

"Cut out. Not torn. Cut, along the perforation, with a blade. You can't tell unless you hold the page up and look at the stub. And the carbon behind it's gone, too." She swallowed. "Whoever did it was careful. They didn't leave a ragged edge. They knew the sheets were in duplicate."

"Who's signed the archive log?"

"Sign-in sheet, front desk. Last person in that cage was somebody with a badge from the third floor. Friday the ninth, three in the afternoon." Another breath. "The log says 'D. Reyes, office.' Not Desmond himself. His office."

"Friday afternoon."

"After he sent you the file. Before anybody could've known you'd ask for the binder." She stopped. "I don't know what that means. I'm furniture. I'm just telling you what the paper says."

Reyes had sent me a thirty-one-page file at 9:12 on Thursday night and, by Friday at three, somebody from his office had gone through a cage to cut out one slip of pink paper. It could mean he'd known exactly what he was sending me. It could mean someone wanted the paper to say so.

"Thank you, Dolores. Leave it where it is. Don't touch the stub."

"Honey," she said, "I've been touching nothing since 1998."

---

Mrs. Tarbell let me in this time.

She stood aside without a word, in the same gray cardigan, with her hands folded in front of her as though she were holding a hymnal. The house was close and warm and smelled of coffee and radiator dust. A kitchen with a yellow table, a calendar from the Co-op, a window over the sink that looked straight out at the dory.

"He's up," she said. "He's been up since four." She seemed about to say more and didn't. "He'll want you to eat something. He won't say so."

I put the doughnuts on the table. She looked at them and, to my surprise, nearly smiled.

Ned Tarbell came in from the back room in a wool shirt buttoned to the collar. He was past sixty, a big soft man who had been bigger once, with a harbormaster's windburned face and hands. He held his shoulders like a man carrying a pail too full of water. His eyes went to me and then to the floor.

"You're Brandt."

"Yes, sir."

"Sit, then."

I sat. He stood a moment longer, then lowered himself into the chair across from me with a sound that wasn't quite a sigh. His wife poured coffee and withdrew as far as the doorway, where she stayed.

I'd planned an opening. I'd planned several. I'd rehearsed them in the car with the heater running and discarded them all. Looking at him, I saw the thing I'd come to say was already sitting on the table between us. He'd been carrying it for six years, and he was tired.

"Mr. Tarbell," I said, "I read your statement about Lydia Farrow."

"I know you did."

"I'd like to hear it from you. Not the paper. What you actually saw."

He picked up his cup and put it down without drinking. "Why? You wrote it."

"I didn't."

It was a very small word to say into a kitchen. I watched it reach him. His head came up, and for the first time he looked at me directly, and I saw that his eyes were a pale, washed gray, red at the rims.

"You came to the harbor office," he said. "April. A Tuesday. It was raining."

"I wasn't there."

"You were. You sat in the chair by the stove." He lifted a hand and put it flat on the table, as if bracing it. "You had a briefcase. You said you were Theo Brandt from Atlantic Mutual and you showed me your card. I looked at the card. It had your name and the little blue anchor. You said Mrs. Farrow's death was a sad business and they only needed to tidy up a few points for the file. You had it typed already. A page and a half. You said, you just sign at the bottom, Ned, and it's done. You said I'd done a kindness, to tell what I'd seen."

"And what had you seen?"

He was quiet. The refrigerator hummed.

"I told you I'd seen the boat on her mooring the evening before," he said. "Salt Ledger. I saw a light in the cabin. I told you I'd seen Mrs. Farrow on deck, once, about six. And you said, and was she alone? And I said, I think so. And you said, and was she drinking? And I said, I don't know. I couldn't tell you. And you smiled and said that's fine, we'll put 'it appeared so,' and you read it back to me. It sounded right. You read it nicely." He lifted his eyes to me. "I signed it."

"You said it was typed already."

"I said you had it typed. Then you wrote some things in the margin. Then you took it away and brought it back clean, in a day or two. And that was the one I signed." He rubbed his thumb along the table's edge. "It wasn't what I said. Not exactly. I didn't say 'bottle.' I never said 'bottle.' But it sounded the way I'd have said it if I'd said it. And you were so kind about it."

"Mr. Tarbell." I kept my voice level. "Look at me. Is that the man you met?"

He looked. I let him. I sat in the thin kitchen light and let a frightened man go over my face as if he were searching a drawer. He looked at my mouth. He looked at my hands. His wife, in the doorway, had stopped breathing.

"I said it was you," Ned Tarbell said slowly. "I've said it for six years. I said it to the police. I said it to my wife."

"But?"

"But I thought you were—" He stopped. His hand moved on the table. "No. It was you. You said your name. I had the card."

"Cards can be handed to anyone."

"The man said he was Brandt." He said it as though pressing on a bruise. "That's what I know. I wrote it down. I know what I know."

"I'm not saying you're lying," I said. "I'm saying someone may have walked into your office with my card and my name and my signature, and been kind to you."

"Why?"

"I don't know yet."

"Did you do it?" he asked.

"No."

He nodded, once. Then he turned his head toward the window, and the dory, and the gray harbor behind it, and said in a different voice, very low, "I never saw her drink."

His wife made a small sound.

"I saw the boat, and the light, and her on the deck at six. She had a cup in her hand. A mug. She was looking at the water, and she waved at me. She waved. I've never told anyone that, because nobody asked, and the man said it didn't matter." He wet his lips. "She wasn't drunk. She was lifting her hand. And I put my name to *it appeared so,* after she was already in the ground. And every night since I lie there and think, if she wasn't drunk, how did she go over? A woman who waves doesn't go over. And somebody's got to say."

"Say it to Detective Castellano."

He stiffened.

"She's honest," I said. "I think she's the best chance you've got. If you tell her what you've told me, there's a record, and you're the man who came forward, not the man who got caught. Tomorrow at nine, the county building. I'll drive you."

"I don't leave the house much," said Tarbell.

"I know."

"I don't like the water." He gave a laugh with no breath in it. "That's a joke on a harbormaster. I was thirty before I told anyone. I'd stand on the wharf and my hands would sweat. But I like the harbor. The lights at night. I just don't like to be in it." He turned back to me. "You'll drive me?"

"I'll be on the step at eight-thirty."

His wife came forward. She put a hand, briefly, on his shoulder, and he reached up and covered it. It was the most private thing I'd seen in a month, and I looked at my coffee.

"There's one more thing," I said. "The man who came that Tuesday. Anything about him that wasn't me? I don't mean his face. Anything. A smell. A habit."

Tarbell frowned at the table. A long minute passed. The refrigerator cycled off, and in the quiet I could hear the wind working at the shutters.

"He took his coat off," he said. "And put it back on. Like it was warm, and then it wasn't. He was a talker. He made you feel you'd done well." He shook his head. "I'm sorry. I thought it was you, so I never looked for anything else."

"That's all right," I said, and found that my voice wasn't entirely steady. "Tomorrow, then."

At the door Mrs. Tarbell gripped my sleeve. She was a small woman and her fingers were strong.

"He hasn't slept in a week," she whispered. "Since the storm. Since they said Mr. Farrow's name on the radio. He's been walking the house." Her eyes were wet. "You'll bring him home?"

"I will."

---

I didn't know what to do with the rest of the day, so I did what I always do, and went to find Hollis.

The cottage on Quarry Lane was warm and smelled of bread. He'd been baking, which he did when he was worried and wouldn't say so, and he put a hot heel of it in my hand at the door before I'd said hello.

"Sit. Eat. You've the look of a man who went into a church and came out of a confessional."

I told him about Tarbell: the card, the page and a half, the wave. I had no reason to hold anything back from the man I'd been confiding in for twenty years. When I got to the part about tomorrow, he put down his knife.

"He's going to Castellano."

"At nine."

"Good," said Hollis. He said it quietly, warmly, with his eyes on the loaf. "Good man. It's a heavy thing to carry that long. I'm glad he's put it down." He glanced up. "And you'll go with him."

"I told him I would."

"Of course." He cut another slice and buttered it, and did not look at it. "Theo, tell me. When he looked at you. Did he know you? I mean, did he *know* you? A man can look at a card for six years and see the wrong face."

"He wasn't sure."

"Poor devil." Hollis shook his head. "I'd tell Castellano to go gently. Frightened witnesses fold if you push. He'll want his wife in the room." He pushed the plate toward me. "Now eat that. It'll be a long day tomorrow, and I'd like you standing up for it."

I ate it. It was good bread. I was grateful for it, and I remember being grateful.

I told him, too, about the pink binder, the cut stub, the log: *D. Reyes, office.* He listened with his head tilted, and said, "Well. That's a man who can't be bothered to lie well." And then, after a moment, "Or one who wants to be seen not bothering."

It was a clever thing to say, and I said so.

"I had a good teacher," said Hollis, and winked, and refilled my tea.

I left at nine with a loaf under my arm. He stood in the lit door in his shirtsleeves until I'd reached the lane. The night was clear and cold, the storm's wind finally gone, the sky above Port Carrow so thickly starred that it looked unreal. In the harbor, the cutters moved gently on their moorings. I stood for a minute at the end of the lane and breathed.

Then I went back to the Anchor Inn, put the chain on, and for the first time in four days slept like a man who had set something down.

---

The phone woke me at ten to six.

I knew it was Nell before I looked. There's a way a call comes through in the dark that feels like a hand on the shoulder.

"Brandt."

"I'm here."

"Where were you last night?" She said it flatly. No hello. Her voice was thick and a little hoarse, and behind it I could hear wind, and gulls, and the slap of water on a hull.

"The Anchor Inn. In bed by ten. The night clerk saw me come in."

"Anyone with you?"

"No. Why?"

"Because I'm standing on Pier Road, at the town landing." She stopped. I heard her take one breath, then another. "A lobsterman found Ned Tarbell's skiff at five-fifteen, drifting off the breakwater with the engine cut and a hand trailing in the water. He was in it."

I sat up. The room tilted. Outside the window the harbor was a flat pewter plate, and on it, a long way out, small and dark against the lightening sky, was a boat with men standing round it.

"His wife says he went out at midnight," said Nell. "She says he never went out. That he was afraid of the water."

"He was afraid of the water," I said.

"Yes," said Nell, very quietly. "That's what I keep thinking. Get dressed, Brandt. And bring that envelope."
