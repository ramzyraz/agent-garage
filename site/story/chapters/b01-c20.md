# Chapter 20: One Thing Nobody Else Has

"It's a trap," Ellen Sayer said, "and I want it understood that I used the word."

We were in the back room of the Rockland barracks on Sunday morning, the one with the cracked linoleum and the coffee machine that dispensed a brown liquid nobody could account for. Nell had pulled three chairs round a table. Sayer sat with her bag in her lap and her hands folded on it, like a woman waiting for a verdict that hadn't been read yet.

"It's a test," said Nell.

"It's a trap with better manners. Let me say what I can live with." She held up a finger. "One. Mr. Brandt tells no untruth about the dead, about the case, or about what he has seen. He may say he has had a recollection that he cannot vouch for. That is true. He has had several this week."

"Fine," I said.

"Two. Whatever he says is said once, to one man, in a room, with an officer at the door who knows only that Mr. Brandt is visiting. Nobody else hears a word of it. Not Mr. Rouse. Not Ms. Farrow. Not your lieutenant."

"Not Ouellette," Nell said. "Agreed."

"Three." The finger came down. "I was never in this room when the content was decided. I will sit in the corridor and drink the coffee. If a judge asks what I knew, I'll say I advised my client to be careful, which I am doing now. *Be careful, Mr. Brandt.*"

"I'm being careful."

"You are being a man who wants to hurt somebody and has found a way to call it procedure." She said it without heat. "I'd rather you knew."

I did know. I'd known it since the stairs on Saturday.

Nell had written the plan on a legal pad, in the square capitals she used for evidence labels. She turned it so I could read.

*ONE DETAIL. ONE LISTENER. WATCH WHO MOVES.*

"Here's the thing about a secret," she said. "If I tell three people three different stories, and a fourth turns up at the third place, I know whose mouth it came out of. It works on drunks and cops and mothers-in-law. Hollis is smarter than all of them. But he can't un-hear it, and he can't act on it without moving."

"He's in a bed."

"He's in a bed with a door and a telephone," said Nell. "And a man in a gray suit left a card at the nurses' desk on Friday." She slid a photocopy across. A plain white business card, a Portland number, and a handwritten line under it: *For Mr. Dunn when he's feeling better. D.*

D.

"The hospital switchboard logs every outgoing call from every room," she said. "I took his cell on Friday. Evidence in the attack. He didn't argue. A man doesn't argue about a thing he's already finished with."

"So the detail has to give him a reason to ring that number."

"It has to give him a reason to want something done before anybody else gets there." She looked at me. "And it has to be something he can't check."

I thought about it for a long time. The linoleum had a crack in it shaped like the Penobscot.

"A tin," I said.

They both looked up.

"A biscuit tin. Blue. Huntley and Palmers. Mara's mother kept her sewing in one. Mara kept hers..." I stopped, because my voice had done something odd. "I don't know that. I've made it up from a thing she said once. It's the sort of thing I'd remember, if I were remembering. It's under the kitchen sink at St. Brendan's, in the church hall. Taped up, behind the pipe. That's where the meeting people make the coffee. I could have put it there. I was in that kitchen every Tuesday for a year."

"Why would you?" said Sayer, very quietly.

"Because it was the last place in Maine anyone would look for a thing of Mara's. Because it was Hollis's church." I heard it as I said it. "Because I trusted that room."

Nell wrote it down.

*BLUE TIN. ST. B'S HALL KITCHEN. UNDER SINK. TAPED.*

"And when?"

"The sexton unlocks at six," I said. "I'd say I'll go at six tomorrow. Before anyone's up. Alone."

"Why alone?"

"Because it's Mara's."

"He'll want to come."

"He'll offer," I said. "He'll say, *let me ring Nell for you.* He'll be kind. He's always kind."

She put down the pencil.

"You're very sure."

"I'm not sure of anything. I'm telling you what he would do."

Nobody answered that. In the front office a phone rang twice and was picked up.

"Weather," said Sayer eventually, because she was the sort of woman who changed a subject when it began to bleed. "They're saying Monday night. A proper northeaster, with the new moon. The causeway's going to be closed longer than it's open."

"We'll be done by then," said Nell.

I have since thought a great deal about that sentence.

---

He was in the chair by the window when I went in, which was new. Somebody had helped him into a robe the color of porridge, and he was looking at the parking structure the way a man looks at a very dull sea. The officer at the door, the woman from Friday, nodded me through without a word. On the tray beside him, a plastic cup of tea, untouched, with the tag hanging over the rim.

Yorkshire Gold. Red box.

I didn't look at it. I made a point of not looking at it.

"Dear boy," he said. "You're late. I'd begun to compose a note."

"I was at the barracks. They wanted my account of the Rockland unit again."

"Of course they did. How was Nell?"

"Tired."

"They always are. It's the job. It ruins the digestion." He patted the arm of the other chair. "Sit."

I sat. We looked at the parking structure together. For the first time in twenty years I found I couldn't tell what I was supposed to say.

"Theo," he said gently. "You look as though you've something on your mind."

I'd rehearsed it in the car. Sayer's rules in my head, one, two, three. *Say only what's true: that you've remembered something and can't vouch for it.*

"Something came up last night," I said. "I was lying in the dark, and I was thinking about that kitchen. Your kitchen. The kettle. And something came loose."

He didn't move. I had known he wouldn't. It was the kind of stillness I'd seen once on a trout in a clear stream, holding exactly where the current wanted it.

"Go on."

"I don't know that it's real." I turned my hands over on my knees. "It's a picture. Mara, in the church hall. At the sink. Putting something in a tin. A blue one, a biscuit tin, and taping it up behind the pipe under the sink where the coffee things are. And she looks at me, and says, *nobody'll look here.*" I swallowed. It was easy. That was the horror of it; it was the easiest thing I had said all week. "I could have made it up. Sayer says I will, if I'm not careful. But I've been in that kitchen a thousand times, and it was never anywhere I've pictured her."

"A blue tin."

"Huntley and Palmers."

"Her mother's," said Hollis softly. "She had a sewing tin like that. I remember it from the funeral. The sister brought it." He had drawn a very slow breath. "Oh, my dear. Under the sink."

"I'm going to look tomorrow. At six, when the sexton opens. I haven't told anyone. I wanted to tell you first, because you were there, you might remember. I didn't want Nell tramping around the hall in boots before I'd seen it. If it's nothing, it's nothing, and I'd like to have been the one to find it."

"Yes," said Hollis. "Yes, of course."

"I haven't told Sayer."

"Wise." He said it almost to himself. Then, lightly, "Shall I ring Nell for you? She'd like to be there. She's very correct."

"No."

"You'll want a witness, dear. If there's paper in it."

"There'll be a witness after. I want to look first. Alone." I made my voice crack, and it didn't need much help. "It's the only thing of hers I've been given that isn't a file."

He looked at me then, truly, and I watched what went across his face and tried to name it. Pity. Under the pity, calculation, rapid and neat as a man counting change, and under that one more thing I hadn't a word for and didn't want.

It was tenderness. That was the worst. He was fond of me. I think he had always been.

"Of course," said Hollis. "Go alone. I understand. And, Theo?"

"Yes."

"Don't stand in the hall for long. It's a draughty room, and you've been unwell." He smiled. "Take a scarf."

I stood up. My legs held.

"Would you drink that?" He nodded at the tray. "I can't bear it. I'm told it's tea."

I picked up the plastic cup. It was warm. I took one swallow, and it was bad, strong and stewed and too sweet, and I thought, *he doesn't take sugar.* A small thing. A man who has lost the use of his kitchen asks for his tea his way, and they bring it his way, and he leaves it.

I put it down.

"I'll come tomorrow after," I said.

"Do."

I got as far as the door. The woman officer took a step to let me by.

"Theo."

I turned.

He was looking at me across the room, an old man in a porridge-colored robe with the light on one side of his face and a yellowing eye.

"I'm so glad you're remembering," he said. "Truly. It's what you've wanted."

---

Nell had the church hall by four.

She told me about it on the phone from the front seat of an unmarked Ford, in the voice she used for logistics. The hall of St. Brendan's Methodist was a low gray box with a gabled roof and an iron fence, sitting on the corner of Church Street a hundred feet below the steeple. A side door into the basement kitchen. A sexton named Mr. Pelkey, seventy, who had been told only that the state police wanted to borrow his keys for a night and had said, to Nell's evident pleasure, that he'd be damned if he'd let a thing like that spoil his Sunday supper.

Nobody had put anything under the sink.

"We took the trap apart to make sure," she said. "It's dry as a bone. Nothing taped. Nothing behind the pipe. There's a mouse. Pelkey says it's a recent mouse."

"Where will you be?"

"Dill in the hall kitchen, in the dark. Two troopers in the sacristy. Me and you in a car across Church Street with a view of the side door. At five-thirty, a trooper called Kessler walks up the path in your coat. He's your height. He wears your scarf. He's got a vest under it that I'd not wish on a dog."

"Nell."

"If somebody wants to be at that sink before you are, they'll want it dark, and they'll want it early, and they will go in at the side. If somebody wants to meet you on the path, they'll do it where your coat is." She breathed. "And if nobody comes, I'll buy you a breakfast and we'll know a hard thing. That he told nobody, that he did nothing, and that you've lied to a hurt old man for the sake of a notebook."

"That's the one I want."

"I know it is."

"What about the switchboard?"

"Switchboard, I'll have by midnight. The hospital's on my side. Nobody goes in or out of that ward unless I'm told."

---

I didn't go back to the Anchor. I didn't trust it. Nell put me in a spare room at the barracks, with a cot and a blanket stamped *Maine State Police*, and at nine o'clock the radio in the corridor said the storm had been upgraded and the causeway would be shut by Monday noon, and I lay in the dark looking at the ceiling, thinking of a church kitchen I had stood in on Tuesday nights for a year.

I thought of the sink. I thought of the pipe. Eleven hours of Tuesday nights, and a drunk man's hands round a mug, and the quiet old man at the urn who said, *Go on, dear boy. Take the first one. It's the hardest.*

At twenty to eleven my phone buzzed.

Nell.

"Switchboard's in."

"And?"

"One outgoing call from room 412 tonight. Nine-forty. Forty seconds." A pause. "He dialed a Portland number. I ran it twice."

I sat up.

"It's the Marriott," she said. "The one on Fore Street. He called the front desk and asked to be put through to a guest."

"Whose?"

"They won't give me the guest's name without paperwork. I'm working on it." Her voice was very even. "The operator remembers the call because the transfer took a while and she could hear his end. He said, *It's me. Quarter to six. The church hall. Under the sink,* and hung up."

I'm not sure what I did with my face. I know I put the blanket over my knees.

"Say that again."

"Under the sink. The night operator remembers it because it struck her as funny. She says it's the sort of thing you say to a plumber."

"A plumber."

"I don't think he was talking to a plumber," Nell said.

---

Monday, four in the morning. A cold that went in at the cuffs.

The unmarked Ford was parked on Church Street with its lights off and its engine ticking, and Nell sat behind the wheel with a thermos she hadn't opened. Across the road the church hall stood in its iron fence. There was a lamp over the side door, orange and small, and a skin of rain on the path that took the orange and broke it into pieces.

I'd put on a spare jacket from Dill's trunk. It didn't fit.

"Tell me again what you saw," she said.

"A man walking up the path. The gray rain jacket, hood. Probably not tall."

"That's what Rouse said."

"That's what the shooter wore."

"We don't know it's the same man."

"No," I said. "We don't know anything."

At four-forty a car came down the hill with its lights off.

It was a dark sedan, nothing special, a rental by the look of it. It slid past the hall and past our Ford and round the turn into Ash Street, and it did not come back. Nell wrote the plate on her knee, said it softly into her collar.

At four-fifty the lamp over the side door went out.

I felt it in my stomach before my eyes understood. A little orange light that had been burning since I arrived, and then it didn't, and the hall sat in the dark like a thing that had never had a face.

"That was the bulb," Nell said. "Or the switch."

"Dill's in there."

"Dill knows. Dill has been told." Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Dill is a nice boy who is very frightened and exactly where I put him."

Nothing moved. Rain ticked on the roof of the car. A long way off, out beyond the roofs, I heard the sea starting to work at the breakwater, a slow dark pull, like a man dragging a chair.

At four fifty-six, a figure came round the corner of the hall.

It came on foot, hugging the iron fence. Gray rain jacket, hood up. Not tall. The face was a patch of shadow in the hood. It stopped at the side door and stood there with its head bowed, in the dark, with the rain coming down on its shoulders. Then it lifted one hand to its mouth.

I'd seen that gesture three times in one week. In a hospital bed, a gray-haired man lifting a finger from a blanket. In the trees on Rouse's camp road. On a voicemail, someone breathing at the edge of a whisper.

*Gray,* Hollis had said. *A shape. In the door.*

"There," said Nell, so softly I felt it in my teeth.

The figure tried the door.

It opened.

It went in.

"Dill," said Nell into her collar. "Dill, you copy. Do not engage. Let them reach the sink. Let them *touch* it."

Nothing. A long, wet, silent nothing. I counted my breaths. I got to eleven, and then I was counting something else, and I realized I was counting the number of times in my life I'd been in that kitchen.

Then I heard the first shot.

It came from the hall, flat and small and enormously final, and Nell said "Go, go, go," and the doors of the Ford were open and rain was hitting my face. There were two more shots, a gap, then a fourth. Somewhere in the sacristy a trooper was shouting. The hall door slammed open and a man in a gray jacket came out of it fast, hood back, one arm clamped across his ribs, and I saw his face for a quarter of a second in the light of the headlamps of a car that had no business being there.

I'll say what I saw and no more, because Sayer has told me to.

It was not a man I knew.

It was a face as plain as a loaf. Fifty. A lot of jaw. Nobody I'd ever seen in a hallway of the fourth floor or a church basement or a funeral. He looked at me across forty feet of wet gravel, and I swear he knew me, and then he was gone round the side of the hall with Nell going after him and Kessler, in my coat, in my scarf, on his hands and knees on the path, saying in a thin cheerful voice, "I'm okay, I'm okay, I'm okay."

He'd taken one in the vest. The other had gone through the scarf.

"Where's Dill?" I said.

Nobody answered. I went in.

The kitchen was dark and smelled of cold coffee and bleach. Dill sat on the floor against the stove with his service pistol across his knees, white to the lips, holding his own left forearm and looking very surprised. The cupboard under the sink hung open. A flashlight lay in it on its side, pointing at a wet wall, throwing a long yellow beam across a blue tin.

A blue biscuit tin. Huntley and Palmers. Somebody had put it there.

It was taped behind the pipe with a strip of silver duct tape, neat as a parcel.

I stood and looked at it. Behind me Dill said, "Sir, I'd not touch that," and I said, "No," and I didn't.

"I didn't put it there," I said. "Nell, I didn't put—"

She had come in behind me, soaked, out of breath, with her gun still out. She looked at the tin. At the tape. At the long clean strip of silver on the pipe, where a hand had been.

"No," she said. "I know you didn't."

"Then who—"

"Somebody who wanted it to be found," Nell said, "by someone he was sure was coming."

I understood. I understood the way you understand a fall when you are already in it.

He hadn't sent anyone to *take* it. He'd sent someone to put it there. To plant it. A tin with something in it, behind a pipe, under a sink, in a hall in Port Carrow, so that when the man who'd claimed to remember it went to find it, there'd be something that the police could say he had hidden. A tin of his own, taped by his own hand, in a place he'd said he'd put it.

"He couldn't check it," I said slowly. "He couldn't check if the tin was real, so he made it real."

"He used your lie," said Nell, "to hang you with."

Her radio crackled.

She pressed the button without taking her eyes off the tin.

"Castellano."

The voice came through in a rush, an officer I knew by sound alone, the woman from room 412, and there was something in it that I had only ever heard in dispatch recordings. It was the voice of a person who has just been told by her own eyes that the thing she was guarding is not there.

"Detective. I'm sorry. I stepped out for one minute. A nurse called me to the desk about a visitor list. I was *gone one minute*, ma'am, and the door was closed and the light was on, and I went back in and—"

"Say it."

"The bed's empty. Mr. Dunn's gone."
