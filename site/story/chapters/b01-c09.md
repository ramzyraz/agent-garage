# Chapter 9: A Spoon Stood Up

I played the recording four times on the walk back to the Anchor Inn, standing under the one working streetlight on Front Street with the phone cupped to my ear like a man listening for a heartbeat. Cora had sent it to me from her porch, her thumb jabbing the screen, her face set. *Don't lose it. Don't let anybody delete it.*

*There you are.*

Three syllables. A man's voice, mild and a little amused, with a door-click behind it. It was not a stranger's voice. That was the thing that made the skin tighten on the back of my neck. I'd had the feeling before, in rooms where I hadn't yet found the reason I was uneasy, and I'd learned to trust it the way you trust a bad tooth.

I knew that voice. I'd heard it over soup, over radios, over the phone at three in the morning. I'd heard it so often that it had stopped being a sound and become weather.

So I did what Hollis taught me. I made a list.

Reyes, whose voice was brisk and nasal and had never in its life said anything kindly. Hal Rouse, whom I had met twice, a whisper of a man. Ouellette. Pryor, wheezing and damp. Quill, thin and dry as a page. Eddie Marsh the lobsterman, who talked like a man gargling gravel. Gus from the chandlery. The night clerk's boy at the Anchor. Hollis.

None of them. I sat on the edge of the bed in room six and ran every voice I'd heard in five years past my inner ear, and none of them fit, and then, because I was tired and because it was the fourteenth and because honesty is cheapest when you're too worn out to pay for anything else, I let the last name on the list in.

I let it in the way you let a dog into a house. Reluctantly, and ashamed.

I listened to the recording once more with that name on it.

It wasn't him. It was muffled. It was a man talking to a man across a room, with a door between them and a wind in the phone. You could hang any voice on it. You could hang mine.

And all the same I got up and put my coat on, because I was a man who had been told by his wife not to tell the people who loved him, and I could not bear to carry that sentence alone one minute more without finding out whether it had a hole in it.

---

Quarry Lane runs inland from the harbor along an old stone-cutters' road, and Hollis Dunn's cottage sits at the end of it where the road gives up. It's a low gray-shingled place with a slate roof and a chimney that smokes in all weather. There was a light in the kitchen and a light in the study, and the gray waxed coat was hanging on the porch rail over the steps with the rain still beading on it.

I stood at the gate for a long time with my hand on the latch.

I wasn't going to tell him. That was what I'd said to myself on the walk up. I would go in, and I would sit at his table, and I would watch him. I would listen to every word with the new ear. And if there was nothing, if there was *nothing*, I'd go back to the inn, and I would have taken one worry off the list. It was the oldest rule in the trade. You clear the people nearest you first, not because they're likely but because they'll hurt the most if you've missed them.

The door opened before I knocked.

"I saw the torch coming up the lane," Hollis said. He was in his shirtsleeves and the green cardigan with the leather elbows. "I'd have put the kettle on for a stranger. For you I've put it on twice." He looked at my face and the humor went out of his own, quietly, like a man lowering a flame. "Come in, Theo. Come in out of it."

The kitchen was warm. It always was. A cast-iron range, a scrubbed table, a bowl of lemons that nobody ever ate, shelves of mismatched mugs. The coat had been on the porch, but its twin smell was in the room anyway, damp wool, drying by the stove in the form of a pair of gray socks over the rail. On the range a kettle had begun its rising note.

I stood there with the sound going through me.

*A kettle.* Nell's voice: *People in the middle of a shock remember the stupidest things.*

It was a kettle. It was Hollis's kettle, in Hollis's kitchen, where I had sat three hundred nights in five years. Of course it was familiar. What did I think, that I'd remember some *other* kettle? I let the thought go by me like a car on a wet road.

"Sit," Hollis said. "There's chowder. I've been keeping it hot since Tuesday out of spite."

It was a very good chowder. Haddock, potato, salt pork, a little cream, a great deal of black pepper. A spoon stood up in it, as promised. I ate the whole bowl without looking at it, and he put another in front of me without a word and sat across the table with his tea and his old man's patience and watched me eat.

I listened to him the whole time. He talked about the town, about Ned Tarbell's funeral, which would be Saturday if the coroner released him, about a new family who'd bought the Pruitt place and put in a *hot tub*, God help them, in a village with a ten-inch frost line. His voice rose and fell. It was warm and gravelly and unhurried and it was the voice I'd heard in my head every day for five years.

It did not sound like the recording. It did not *not* sound like it. I sat there with the spoon in my hand, trying to hold two sounds against each other, and the harder I tried the less either of them was a sound at all.

"You're not eating," Hollis said gently.

"I'm eating."

"You're doing something with a spoon." He turned his cup on its saucer. "You've been to Rockland."

I put the spoon down.

"I didn't say a word," he went on, mildly, before I could speak. "I'm not that clever. But you texted me from Portland on Tuesday and you were at the breakwater yesterday with a face like a man who's been handed a very heavy parcel and told not to put it down. And you have not asked me for one single thing in two days, which in five years is a first." He let that rest. "I'm not going to ask. I told you. I'll be here."

*Especially them.*

I looked at his hands round the cup, the huge scarred knuckles, the faded blue of an old tattoo on the left wrist that he said was a sailor's mistake. I looked at the man who had found me in a parked car at the head of the causeway, three days sober and shaking, and put his coat over my knees.

I thought: *If I'm wrong about this, I'm wrong about everything.*

And then I thought, with the cold clear voice that I'd learned to hate, *that is precisely what a man would think who is about to be right.*

I put my hand inside my jacket. I took out the envelope. I laid it on the table between the bowl of lemons and the sugar I'd never once seen him use.

"Mara left me a letter," I said.

---

He read it with his glasses on the end of his nose and his lips pressed together. He read it twice, the way I'd done, once for the words and once for the person. His hands didn't shake. When he reached the line about the people who love you, I watched his face, because I'd come here to watch it, and what I saw was a man being struck.

He took off the glasses. He put the page down on the table and laid his palm flat on it, as if it might lift.

"Well," he said, very low. "Well, there you are, then."

I'd been braced for denial, or fury, or the kind of smooth wisdom he was famous for. I hadn't been braced for him to look *old.*

"That's why you've been carrying it. That *especially.*" He pressed two fingers against his eyes. "God. She wrote that on a night she was frightened of everyone. She'd have been frightened of me too, I expect. I was the man who put you in this business. The man who'd put you in front of those files." He laughed, a dry, cracked noise. "Theo, if I'd found out that my wife's husband's mentor was possibly the center of a pattern like this, I'd have said *especially* too."

"I didn't tell you because—"

"You didn't tell me because you were told not to. I'd have thought less of you if you had." He pushed the letter back across the table, gently, with one finger. "You did right to wait. And you've done right to come now. Both."

"Hollis—"

"Tell me the rest. Start with the key."

So I told him.

God help me, I told him all of it. It came out the way things came out in those years, in the kitchen, with the tea going cold. Rockland. The unit. The prints, the missing box, the gate log with Mara's own code. The red ink on page seven. The ledger photograph, with its *T.B., T.B., T.B.* and its thirty percent, and Nell with the print against her chest in the lot behind Quill's. The card in the box marked *H.*

I said that last quickly. I watched him while I said it.

He didn't blink. "H."

"It could be anyone. Hal Rouse. The harbormaster. A hundred things."

"Or me," said Hollis.

The kettle shrieked. He got up, took it off the range, and poured it with his back to me. His shoulders were broad and sloped under the cardigan, and the water went into the pot without a splash.

"Put me on the list, old son," he said, not turning. "At the top. I'm an H. I was the regional director who signed off half those files, and my name is on the Doyle review whether I like it or not. If I were Castellano I would have had me in a room on Sunday." He set the pot down. "I'm not offended. I'd be disappointed in you if you hadn't thought it."

I felt my face go hot with a shame that was entirely real.

"I haven't," I lied.

"You came up the lane with a very straight back," Hollis said kindly. "You have the walk of a man going to see a priest. Or a policeman." He sat down again and poured two cups with that steady hand. "It's all right, Theo. It's the right way to do it. You clear the people nearest you first."

*I taught you that.* He didn't say it. He didn't need to.

I drank the tea. It was black and strong and just short of bitter. I could have wept.

---

We talked until after midnight. Hollis had questions, and they were the right ones, which was what I'd always loved in him and what I should have noticed that night. Did Nell have the originals? Where was the ledger photograph kept? Had the lab got the pencil? Did Quill have copies, a file note, the *original* standing instruction in Mara's hand?

"He does," I said. "He told me yesterday. He's bringing it all to Port Carrow tomorrow at eleven. A sworn statement to Nell, at the county building. He's seventy years old, and he said it's the last thing he'll be asked to do for Mara's father." I rubbed my face. "He's the one person in this who hasn't lied to me."

"Tomorrow." Hollis nodded slowly. "At the county building."

"Eleven."

"Good. That's good." He studied the lemons. "Don't tell Reyes. Don't tell anyone at Atlantic. And Theo," he lifted his eyes, "I'd stay near the water. Not in that room at the Anchor with the thin walls. If you want somewhere nobody can find you, the cutter's on her mooring. Second Opinion. She sleeps two, and I've done worse in her than a night in a bunk. Take the key from the nail by the door."

"I'm all right at the Anchor."

"I know you are." He smiled. "I'm an old man. Let me be of use."

I took the key. I'm ashamed to say how glad I was to have it in my pocket.

It was nearly one when he walked me to the door. The rain had thinned to a mist. He stood on the step in his shirtsleeves with the light behind him, and he did what he'd done on the breakwater, he put his hand on the back of my neck.

"Tell me something about her," I said.

I hadn't meant to. It came out of the dark like a boy's request.

He was quiet. "Mara?"

"I can't remember her voice," I said. "Not properly. I've got the *words* she said. Not the sound."

"Ah." His hand stayed where it was. "Well. She laughed a half-second before the joke was over, did you know that? Always. She'd see the end coming, and she'd start. At the Christmas I did the goose, the one that went up like the Hindenburg, you remember, she was already crying with laughter when I opened the oven door. She was the only person in that kitchen who got there ahead of me." His thumb moved once on my neck, an old man's small comfort. "And she said your name as if it were a place. As if you were somewhere she'd been trying to get to. I never heard anyone else do that."

I said, "Thank you."

"Go and sleep."

I went down the lane in the thinning mist with his key in my pocket and my back to his light, and I did not look round. If I had, I think I would have seen him standing there until I turned the corner. He always did.

I didn't see the lamp in the study go on behind me, or the shadow cross it, or the second cup being lifted from the table and rinsed in the sink.

I didn't see any of it. I was thinking of a laugh half a second early, and for the first time in five years I slept the whole night through, with the chain on and the key beside my car keys, and I did not once dream.

---

"She came in," Nell said.

It was nine in the morning, Thursday, the fifteenth, and the diner on Front Street smelled of bacon and wet newspaper. Nell had the corner booth and a plate of eggs she hadn't touched. She looked like she'd slept in her jacket, which, I understood from the line across her cheek, she had.

"Cora?"

"With Voss. Eleven o'clock last night. Sat in my office for two hours and gave me the whole thing: the calls from her father, the hardware-store footage, the knock, the boat." She picked up her fork and put it down again. "I wanted to put her in a cell. I'm going to take her statement to the DA this morning and recommend she isn't charged with anything. I may even be right."

"And the boat?"

"A one-mast boat with no lights in a cove. I've got eleven in this harbor that match, and four more on trailers." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I've got Dolores Pike's archive sign-in, I've got a hardware receipt, a tea bag, a rinsed glass, a forged page seven, and a recording of an old man being interrupted by someone who sounded pleased to see him. I've got more evidence than I've ever had, and not one thing that points at a person."

"The recording," I said.

"You've heard it."

"Forty times."

She looked at me sharply. "Do you know that voice?"

I thought of the kitchen. The kettle. The hands round the cup. *Put me on the list, old son.*

"No," I said.

It was the first lie I'd told her. It was a small one. I told myself it was kindness, because the alternative was handing her the name of the one man in the world who had done nothing but hold me up, on the strength of a *feeling,* three syllables, and a draft in my own head.

She held my eyes a moment longer than she had to.

"Quill's coming at eleven," I said. "Mara's original instruction. A sworn statement. He's bringing the file."

"I know, he rang me last night." For the first time that morning, something in her face eased. "He's a pistol, that man. He told me he'd been sitting on this for five years and he was going to enjoy it. Do you want to be in the room?"

"If you'll have me."

"I'll have you. Eat something."

---

At eleven Nell had cleared a conference room on the second floor of the county building, with a tape recorder and a pitcher of water and a box of tissues that someone had left there as a joke. I sat at the far end with my hands flat. A trooper stood at the door. The tide table on the wall said high water was at 5:48 that evening, and the causeway wouldn't close until after four. The morning's tide had gone out hours ago.

Eleven-oh-five. Eleven-ten.

At eleven-fifteen Nell went out and rang Quill's office. I could hear her through the door, short and level. She came back in, and she said nothing.

At eleven-thirty she rang his cell. It went to voicemail. She left a message with her name in it, and then, after a pause, mine.

"He's seventy years old," I said. "He drives a Volvo that's older than Cora. He'll be stuck behind a truck on Route 1."

"He's never late," said Nell. "Is he."

I thought about it. In thirty years of Exchange Street, the man had never once, to my knowledge, missed a thing he'd said he would do. Not a hearing. Not a deposit. Not a promise to a dead woman's father.

"No."

At noon she sent a trooper down to Portland to knock on his door. At twelve-twenty the girl who ran the nautical antiques shop beneath his office called back to say that the lights were on upstairs and his door was locked, and that she had seen him go in at seven that morning with a banker's box under his arm, and she hadn't seen him come out.

"Which would mean he's still *in* there," said Nell. "Or he went out the back."

"Or he never went in."

Neither of us spoke. The tape recorder sat between us with its little red light off. Down in the street I could hear the first of the afternoon traffic coming off the causeway, and the long scream of a gull.

At twelve-forty her phone rang.

She looked at the screen and her face did something I hadn't seen it do, not at the boathouse, not in the unit, not on the breakwater. It went completely still.

"Castellano."

I watched her listen. I watched her lips, and I watched her free hand go up to the back of her neck and stay there.

"Where on the causeway?" she said.

A pause.

"How long has it been there?"

Another.

"Don't let anybody touch it," said Nell, already on her feet, already reaching for her coat. "Don't let *anybody* touch it. I'm coming."

She was at the door before she turned. She looked at me across the little room and the pitcher of water and the box of tissues, and there was something in her face I couldn't have said I'd prepared for.

"It's a gray Volvo," she said. "It's on the shoulder at the low point of the causeway, and the tide has been over it twice."
