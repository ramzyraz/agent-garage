# Chapter 13: The White House on Gannet Road

I did not go to Hollis's.

I want that on the record, because of what came after. The causeway took me in at ten to eight, water still sliding off its shoulders in sheets. At the first fork the town offered me two roads. Left was Quarry Lane, where a green-shaded lamp would be burning in a converted pantry and a kettle coming up to the boil in the voice I had known for nine years. Right was the Anchor. I sat at the fork with the engine running and my hands at ten and two, like a boy taking his test.

I turned right.

It was not a decision. I would like to say it was, but it was closer to what a hand does on a stove. Then I was in room 6 with the chain on and my coat still wet, sitting on the end of the bed with the phone face-down on the spread, and I couldn't have told you why.

*You always have.*

I have thought about that sentence more than any I've been given. Pryor had said it so softly. He had not said *Hollis*. He had said *someone*, and it was a man who'd spent nine years learning how to keep from naming things. I told myself it meant Nell. I told myself it meant Mara, who had always been the person I'd told everything. By half past eight I had run out of people it could mean, and I got up and washed my face in the sink and did not look in the mirror.

---

At nine I phoned Nell.

I want that on the record too. I had her number up, and my thumb on it, and I rang it, and it went to voicemail, and I listened to her recorded voice say *Castellano, leave it*, and I opened my mouth.

"It's Theo," I said. "I'm at the inn. I'm going to bed." A pause long enough that I could hear the tape running. "Thank you for the sandwich."

I hung up. *Tell no one. Not a soul. Not the county, not anyone in uniform.* I had been told it by a man whose voice shook, and I had spent my working life listening to men whose voices shook, and I knew precisely what the shake was worth and I did it anyway. A frightened man will give you everything if you come alone. Bring a badge and he gives you a lawyer. I had told myself that in a hundred kitchens. It was true in all of them.

It is also the reasoning of every man who has ever walked into a room by himself.

At ten I went down the back stairs, through the alley the night clerk used for her cigarettes, and out into Water Street. The clerk wasn't at the desk. I took that, in my stupidity, as luck.

---

Port Carrow at night is a town with the lights turned down for company. The tide had gone out, and the harbor smell had come up the lanes behind it, kelp and diesel and wet rope, and everything dripped. I walked. Nobody was out. A television flickered blue in an upper window above the hardware store, and somewhere a dog was being told, patiently, to come in.

The church was at the top of Church Street, white clapboard, a steeple the color of a fish's belly under the one streetlight. St. Brendan's Methodist. I knew the basement better than the nave. I stood under the light and looked at the door I had gone in by on my eleventh day of sobriety, on a man's arm, and I let myself stand there for exactly as long as it took to be sure no one was following me.

No one was. I'd have bet my license. I'd have lost it.

Gannet Road ran behind the church and out along the old quarry, a one-lane ribbon of tar with grass in the center and houses set back from it in the dark. Past the last of them was the gravel pit, a crater of pale stone with a chain across the mouth and a sign about trespassers. Past the pit was a white house.

The porch light was on.

It was a fixture shaped like a lantern, with a moth batting at it, and it lit a square of wet boards and a brown door mat and the front door beyond. I stood at the edge of the yard in the wet grass and looked at the whole house. A one-story ranch, white siding, a carport with a county-issue Chevy in it. Lights on in two windows at the side. Curtains drawn. No sound at all but the quarry water dripping in the pit.

It was ten past eleven.

I went up the steps, and I knocked.

---

The door was not latched. It moved inward under my knuckles with a small dry sound, the sound a door makes when it has been left that way on purpose, and the hall behind it was warm. A lamp on. A coat rack with a county parka on it. A smell, faintly, of lemon, and under it, wool.

"Doctor?"

Nothing. I stood on the mat and breathed through my mouth.

*Come in*, I thought, *he's waiting. He said the porch light on, he's in the kitchen with the kettle, he's afraid.* And I went in, because the alternative was to go back down the steps into the dark, and I had come a very long way to hear a man say what he'd done.

The kitchen was empty. A single mug stood on the counter beside a kettle, and the kettle, when I put the back of my hand to it, was warm. Not hot. A mug, a lemon wedge pale on a saucer, the tag of a tea bag over the rim.

I looked at the tag. It was a perfectly ordinary tag. I noticed it the way you notice a stranger in a crowd and do not see his face.

Light came from the end of the hall. I followed it.

---

His study was the room at the back, with a window onto the pit. A big oak desk, a green blotter, a wall of grey metal filing cabinets with their drawers sitting a half inch proud. A leather chair swung around to face the window. Ansel Pryor sat in it with his head back and his mouth a little open, in the oatmeal cardigan, one hand in his lap and the other hanging.

I knew before I touched him. You know. I had been in rooms with the dead ever since I was twenty-six, and the dead have a way of occupying a chair that the living, even the sleeping living, do not.

Still, I went round and put two fingers to his neck. He was cool and slightly clammy, the way a thing is that has been at room temperature for a while. His eyes were half open. They looked at nothing in the window, and the window looked back at him, and the pit lay under it as pale as a plate.

On the desk, a bottle of Glenlivet, a third gone. A tumbler, on its side, a stain on the blotter below it. An orange pharmacy bottle, uncapped, empty, a white drift of tablets spilled beside it like a thing that had happened to a snowman. And a pen.

It was a ballpoint, a cheap blue Bic with the cap chewed. It lay across a single sheet of paper, as though set there by a man who had finished writing and was about to put his name to it. I knew that pen. I had listened to it click for six days.

I do not remember deciding to read the note. I remember my breath, and the sound of the pit dripping, and the smell of lemon.

*To whom it concerns,*

*I can no longer go on. I have signed what I was handed for nine years and I can't sign any more. Mr. Brandt's reports did not tell the truth and I knew it, and I wrote what he needed. He knows what I did and why. I am sorry. I am sorry for the women.*

*A. Pryor*

It was typed. The signature at the bottom was in blue ink, shaky and good.

---

I didn't take the paper. I'd like that remembered too. I touched the edge of the desk, and the back of the chair, and the doorframe going out, and the kettle going in, and the knob of the front door both ways. I touched the little brass cat on the hall table, God knows why. The sum of my evening, as a forensic document, was a lamp and a coat rack and every surface in the house, and I realized it standing in the hall with my phone in my hand and my thumb already on Nell's name.

She picked up on the first ring.

"Castellano."

"It's me. I'm in a house on Gannet Road. The white one past the gravel pit." My voice was steady and it frightened me. "Pryor's dead. I got here five minutes ago. Please come."

There was a short silence in which I heard her stand up from something. "Don't touch anything."

"I've touched everything."

"Okay," said Nell, and the flatness in it was the sound of a woman doing her arithmetic. "Stay in the hall. Don't sit, don't go back in the room. I'm eight minutes. Theo."

"Yes?"

"How long did you know he was going to be there?"

I looked down the hall at the lit door of the study. "Since a quarter to eight."

She said nothing. The line stayed open for a moment, and then it went dead, and out on the road, faint and getting stronger, I heard a siren I had not called.

---

It was a Port Carrow cruiser, a white Ford Interceptor with a bar of blue lights going round. It came up Gannet Road far too fast for the width of it and slewed onto the grass in front of the house. A big young officer got out with his gun not quite drawn. *Dill*, said the plate over his pocket. He was perhaps twenty-six, with a head like a tin of beans and the cautious white face of a man the town had hired for his mother's sake.

"Sir. Step out onto the porch with your hands where I can see them."

"I called the state police. Detective Castellano's on her way."

"We had a call." He had his flashlight in my face. "A prowler. Somebody walking up Gannet Road in a dark coat who looked to be trying the doors. Hands, sir."

"Who called?"

"Hands."

I put them up. It is one of the stranger humiliations of an honest life, how fast the body knows what to do.

"There's a man dead inside," I said. "Dr. Pryor. In the study."

Dill's face changed. It was not a face made for it. He went past me into the house with his gun now fully out, and I heard him in the hall, and then I heard him stop. And then I heard what he said, which I won't repeat.

By the time Nell's Ford came up the road he had put me in cuffs.

---

I have been arrested twice. The first time was the Rockport hospital corridor, five years ago, when a trooper stood me against a wall and I was too drunk to know what for. The second time was a young man named Dill reading me my rights on a porch in the rain, in a voice trembling at the edges, as though the words might go off.

Nell stood in the yard and watched it. She had her hood down. Her hair was plastered flat and she did not wipe the water from her face, and I could tell that if I looked at her too long I would say something that couldn't be said.

"Officer," she said. "Uncuff him."

"Ma'am, I've got a prowler call, he's on the property, he's got a body—"

"I know. I'm taking him in my own car." She held up her badge. "State police. This is a state-police matter, and you're going to go and stand by that cruiser, and not let anyone in the house but the people I tell you. Do you understand?"

He did, in the way a large dog understands. He took the cuffs off. I rubbed my wrists.

Nell took my arm and turned me, slowly, to face her. She stood close, so that no one else would hear.

"Did you touch the note?"

"No."

"What did it say?"

I told her, word for word. She closed her eyes through the whole of it. When I'd finished she opened them, and what was in them was neither anger nor pity, but the hard clear look of a woman reading the last page of a bill.

"You were warned," she said. "By him. *Tell no one.* And you didn't tell me. Not even me, after the car. After the sandwich."

"If I'd brought you—"

"If you'd brought me, he might be alive." She didn't raise her voice. "Or he might not. Or you might have been in that chair. I don't know, Theo, and now I never will, and that's what you gave me." Her jaw worked. "You got a call at 7:45 from a man who was dead by midnight. It's on the phone company's log. It's on yours. I'm not going to be able to make that go away."

"I know."

"Do you know who else knew you were coming?"

It was there. The word. It was right there, behind my teeth, with its blue anchor and its green cardigan and its kettle. And Nell was looking at me, and the porch light shone on her wet face, and in that moment I could have said *Hollis texted me at 7:40.* I could have said *he said he'd leave the light on.*

Which was a thing he had said in his text, and a thing Pryor had said on the phone. The same words.

"No one," I said. "I told no one."

It is a terrible thing to watch a person decide whether to believe you. I watched her do it. I watched her look at me in the yard of a dead man's house and conclude something, and put it away.

"Get in the car," said Nell.

---

They held me at the county building on Mill Street, in the same basement corridor where I had first been told my wife's death certificate was in order. It was a room with a bench and a steel toilet and a drain in the floor. They took my shoelaces. A deputy I didn't know brought me a Styrofoam cup of coffee and then took it away again, apologetically, because someone in an upstairs office had decided I should have nothing hot.

Detective Ouellette came at ten past two. He was a tall, sallow, courteous man in a cheap blue tie, and he sat on the other side of the table with a manila folder and did not open it.

"You're not under arrest yet," he said. "I want to be clear. You're being held for questioning in an unattended death, on the strength of a prowler complaint and the fact that your fingerprints appear to be on every object in the house." He smiled very slightly. "And I'm to say you may call a lawyer."

"I don't have one."

"No. I expect that's a thing that happens to a man." He opened the folder. "Dr. Pryor's time of death will be decided in Augusta, but the state man who looked at him puts it between nine and ten. You were seen entering the Anchor Inn at ten to eight. The night clerk was off her desk from nine until ten-thirty. She says she went to the bakery for a friend. The back stairs let out on an alley." He turned a page. "You'll see what I'm doing."

"I was in my room."

"Is there someone who can say so?"

I thought of the empty desk, the hotel with its lights out, the long white corridor. "No."

"I'd have thought not." He closed his eyes briefly, like a man at grace. "The detective told me what you said about the note, and I read it myself at the scene. That's one sheet. I want to show you something else."

He took a second sheet from the folder and slid it across.

It was a photocopy of a handwritten page, small, cramped, in what I knew at once to be Ansel Pryor's own hand: a doctor's hand, the letters closed up tight like fists. It had been found, Ouellette said, in the bottom drawer of the desk, under the blotter, in an envelope marked *If anything happens.* It was undated. It had two paragraphs. I read the first, and the room tilted, and I read it again.

*On the evening of October 5, five years ago, Mr. Theodore Brandt came to my home on Gannet Road. He was sober and very calm. He said his wife had been going through Atlantic's files and had found things in four deaths that could not be explained, and that she meant to take them to the ethics line and from there to the police. He said there were four ME reports with my name. He said he would give me one night to decide what I would do. He said it would be better for everyone if she stopped.*

*I did not know what he meant. I know now.*

The second paragraph was a single line.

*Ask him where he was on the night of the fifth. He will not be able to tell you.*

I read it a third time.

I don't remember Ouellette leaving. I remember the drain in the floor, and the light going on and off in the corridor beyond the wired glass, and sitting on the steel bench with my hands open on my knees. Hal Rouse had put me in a brown-glass office at four. Somebody had put me, at night, at a stranger's door on the same Monday, saying the thing a man says when he has already decided.

Nine days I had lost. And somebody had known the exact shape of the hole, and had found the one thing to put in it.
