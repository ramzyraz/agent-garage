# Chapter 8: The Fourteenth

"Think," Nell said. "Not hard. Hard is how you lose it. Just stand there and let it come."

I stood in the doorway of Unit 114 with the card in my hand and the cold coming up through my shoes, and let it come.

What came was a kettle.

Not a scene. Not a face. The thin rising note of a kettle about to boil in a room I couldn't see, and the smell of wet wool. It lasted a second. When I reached for it, the way you reach for the end of a word, it was gone. The white place returned, and my heart beat hard against it.

"Nothing," I said. "A kettle."

"A kettle." Nell almost smiled and thought better of it. "That's something. People in the middle of a shock remember the stupidest things. Write it down. Don't build on it."

It was nearly noon. A trooper had come from Rockland with a van and a clipboard, and for the next hour Mara's library went out into the daylight one box at a time, each one photographed, numbered, signed for. I watched them go like a man watching his house taken apart by movers who were being kind. *H.* went out last, in Nell's own arms. She wouldn't let the trooper take it.

"Copies are yours," she said at her car. "Quill's paperwork gives you the right. Everything else is evidence, and you are not going to argue with me about it."

"I'm not going to argue."

"Good. Today's the fourteenth."

I looked at her.

"I know what day it is," she said. "So do you. Where are you going to be tonight?"

"Port Carrow."

"The breakwater."

"I go every year."

"Then go." She opened her door. "But first, you and I are going to see Ansel Pryor, because I'd like to watch his face when I say your wife's name."

---

The county medical examiner kept his office in the basement of the county building on Mill Street, down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and formaldehyde pretending to be floor wax. Pryor was at his desk with the lamp on, in a cardigan the color of oatmeal. He clicked a ballpoint pen. Click, click. He did it the whole time we were in the room, and I began to think it was the only part of him that was still working.

"Detective. Mr. Brandt." He didn't stand. "I've told you everything I have on Mr. Tarbell. Drowning. Water in the lungs, froth, no injuries inconsistent with a fall from a small boat. Old men do odd things at midnight."

"He was afraid of water," Nell said. "His wife says he hadn't been on the harbor in thirty years."

"Fear isn't a finding."

"Neither is *consistent with*." She sat without being asked. "I want a tox panel. Full. Sedatives, hypnotics, whatever you've got reagents for. And I want it run at the state lab, not here."

Click. "I can request—"

"I'm not asking you to request."

"The county has a process, Detective."

"I've seen the process." She let that lie there. Then, in exactly the same tone, as if she were reading from a menu: "I'd also like to look at the Brandt file. Mara Brandt. Five years ago today, the breakwater. You signed it."

The pen stopped.

It was a very small thing. A man's thumb coming off a button. But the room changed around it, the way a room does when the refrigerator cuts out and you realize you'd been hearing it. Pryor looked at Nell. He did not look at me, and that was the part I noticed. In twenty years I have watched a great many people avoid the eyes of a widower, and there's a way of doing it that is only embarrassment. This was something else. It was a man trying not to see a thing he had already seen.

"That was a clear case," he said. "Accidental drowning. I don't see any reason—"

"Was she tested for sedatives?"

"There was no indication."

"There's a *reason* to test," Nell said, "every time. I'm asking if you did."

"I won't discuss a closed case with the husband in the room." His voice cracked on *husband*. "I'll answer to the state board, if the board asks me. Not to this." He clicked the pen twice, hard, like a man trying to restart something. "Please go."

On the wall behind him, between a diploma and a framed tide chart, was a plaque. *Port Carrow Volunteer Rescue, with thanks.* A row of signatures. Mine was not among them, but I knew two. The chief of police, and in a looping, friendly hand I could have picked out of a thousand, *Hollis Dunn.* In a town of eleven hundred, a man like Hollis signed everything. I thought, that's the way it is in a small place, and I looked away from it as I'd look away from any other fact I couldn't use.

In the corridor Nell kept her face straight until the stairs.

"He knew," she said. "Not what happened. That something had been *done* to that file. Did you see him? Like a man who's been expecting a knock for five years and just heard it."

"He didn't deny it."

"He didn't say anything, which is what scared people do." She stopped on the landing. "I can't force him, Theo. A judge needs more than a flinch. But he's going to lie awake tonight, and a man who lies awake starts to think about who he'd rather talk to. I'm going to make sure it's me."

---

The Port Carrow breakwater is a quarter mile of granite blocks thrown out into the harbor mouth in 1911 by men who didn't expect to be thanked. It ends in a little iron light on a pedestal. At the far end, on the seaward side, there is a flat slab with a drop of about six feet to the water, and weed on the lower blocks, and in October after rain the weed is the color of a bruise.

Five years ago tonight, a little before eight, my wife stood on that slab and went in.

I'd come every year. At first because I couldn't not; later because it was a thing you did, like a day-one coin, like Hollis's thermos. I didn't pray. I stood. I put my hands in my pockets, and I looked at the water, and I said what I had to say, which was always the same four words and never enough.

The light was going. There was a wind from the northeast with a wet edge. As I came down the last stretch of granite I saw there was already someone at the end of it, sitting on the slab with his legs hanging over, a gray waxed coat spread on the stone beside him and a steel thermos between his knees.

Hollis looked over his shoulder and lifted a paper cup.

"I thought you might not make it," he said. "The causeway's behaving. I took a chance."

"You didn't have to."

"I know." He moved the coat to make room. "Sit, old son. It's the only thing I've ever been any good at on this day: sitting."

He'd done it every year. The first year, when I couldn't have found the breakwater with a map, he had found *me*, three days sober and shaking in a parked car at the head of the causeway, and he'd walked me out here with a hand under my elbow. After that he'd simply been here, with the thermos and the cup, no questions. I had never once thanked him properly. You can't. It would be like thanking the tide.

I sat. The tea was black and strong, no sugar, the way he made it. I held the cup in both hands.

"You've got something," Hollis said after a while.

I didn't answer.

"I'm not asking. I know a man holding a hot thing." He watched the light. "You went to Portland yesterday, you said. You look like a man who's been to the bottom of something."

"Paperwork," I said. "Estate stuff. For Mara." That much was true, anyway. I'd meant it to sound like grief, and it did.

"Mmm." The gull noises came and went. "I'd tell you to come to dinner, but I think what you'd like is to be left alone to feel it. I'm right?"

"You usually are."

He laughed quietly. The sound wasn't quite a laugh; it was lower. "Not usually. I've been wrong about a number of things in my life. I've just been careful to be wrong in private." He tipped his cup toward the water. "She'd have been forty-three in March."

"Forty-two," I said.

"Forty-two." He nodded slowly. "So she would."

I looked at him then, in the failing light: the lines either side of the mouth, the white hair stirring in the wind, the huge capable hands around the little cup. I thought: *tell him.* I had told him everything for five years. I had told him about the night I drove to Bangor for no reason and the year I couldn't read the newspaper. The envelope was in my jacket, and I could feel its corner against my ribs.

*Not anyone,* she had written. *Not even the people who love you. Especially them.*

It was the *especially* that stopped me. I didn't understand it, and that was what held. You don't walk across a stretch of ice because the man beside you says it will hold. You walk across because you know why it should.

"I will," I said. "Soon. There's something I can't say yet."

Hollis didn't push. He rarely did. He put his hand on the back of my neck, briefly, the way a father does, and took it away. "Soon," he agreed. "I'll be here." He screwed the cap on the thermos, got up with a small grunt, and shook out the coat. "Eat something. You look like a lamp that's been left on."

I watched his gray back go along the granite until it merged with the dusk.

I stood on the slab, and I said the four words, and it was no better than any other year. Then I turned around, because someone was standing on the stones behind me, thirty feet off, hat in his hands.

---

"I'm sorry," said Barrett Voss. "That was bad manners. I waited till he left."

"You were watching me."

"I was watching *him*, a little. Natural curiosity. He's the type people stop talking around." Voss put on the hat. "That's a joke. I'm not good at them. Mr. Brandt, can I have ten minutes? There's something you ought to see, and I'd rather not hand it over on a rock in the dark."

We went to the diner on Front Street, to the same booth as before. Voss ordered a coffee and didn't drink it. He had a manila folder, and he laid it flat and kept one hand on it as if it might lift off.

"Cora Farrow hired me in June," he said. "You know that. She wanted to know how her mother died. I've been working the problem for four months, and I'll tell you honestly, you were my first guess. You. The man who closed the file. Then the harbormaster started dodging me and the picture went sideways."

"Voss."

"I'm getting there. Here's the thing about being hired by someone: you stop looking at the person who hired you. It's a rule. It's a stupid rule." He opened the folder. "Alan Farrow died the night of the eighth. Cora told Detective Castellano she was in Boston from the seventh through the ninth. She told *me* the same. She's a grad student at BU. Library cards. A seminar."

He turned a photograph around.

It was a still from a hardware-store security camera on the Port Carrow causeway road. Time stamp: 10/08, 10:38 PM. A small dark Subaru, headlights on, rain slanting in them. The plate was legible. I had seen it before. It had been parked outside the Anchor Inn on Saturday.

"I got the footage Monday," Voss said. "I sat on it two days. I told myself I was verifying. Mostly I didn't want to know."

"You think she killed him."

"I think she's lying to me. And I think if I can find this, so can Castellano, and by Friday that girl's in an interview room with a public defender and nobody's asking who else was on that causeway." He rubbed his eyes. "She hired me, Mr. Brandt. I don't have to like it. But I'd rather she tell you first."

"Why me?"

"Because," said Voss, "you're the one she'll shout at. And she needs to shout at someone."

---

Cora Farrow was renting the downstairs of a gray-shingled house on Sparrow Lane, three doors from the harbor. The light was on. When she opened the door she had a mug in one hand and a textbook under her arm, and for a heartbeat she looked fourteen.

Then she saw Voss's face, and the photograph in his hand, and the mug went down on the porch rail with a click.

"You *followed* me?"

"I didn't," Voss said. "A camera did."

"I paid you to find out what happened to my *mother*."

"Yes."

"Not to—" She stopped. She turned on me, the way he'd said she would, and her face was white. "You came for this. That's why you're here. To watch me—"

"I came because it's the fourteenth," I said. "And I'm tired, and I don't think you killed him."

It was an odd thing to say on a porch, and it went into her like a stone into still water. She looked at me for a long time. The wind pulled at her hair. Then she turned and went in and left the door open, which was all the invitation there was.

Inside there was a card table with a laptop and four coffee cups and a corkboard of index cards, color-coded, a rope of red yarn running from one pin to another. It was Mara's unit again, on a smaller scale. I felt it in my stomach.

"I was there," Cora said.

She said it to the corkboard. She sat on the arm of a sofa with her arms folded across herself.

"He called me on the seventh. He was drunk, I could hear it, but he was *clear*. He said, *Come up. I'll tell you everything. Not on the phone.* He said he'd been a coward for six years and he was tired of it. I said I'd come Thursday. Then he rang again Thursday afternoon and said *tonight, if you can, I don't think I should wait.*" Her voice shook. "So I drove up. I reached the causeway just before ten-forty. It was already ankle-deep; I almost didn't make it. I parked on the lane. I went down the steps to the boathouse because there was a light on in the loft window."

"What time?"

"Quarter of eleven. A little before." She rubbed her face. "I knocked. I knocked and knocked. I called his name. Nothing. The door was solid, and it was *locked*, I could hear the water inside, like it was—" She stopped and breathed. "I thought he'd passed out. He did that. I was so angry I could have screamed, so I left. I didn't call anyone. I drove back across before the road closed, and I went home to Boston, and I got a call from the police the next afternoon, and I *lied*, because the only thing I could think was that I had stood at the door while he was inside."

Nobody said anything.

"Did you see anyone?" I asked.

"No."

"Anything. A car. A light."

"There was a boat." She frowned. "In the cove, past the boathouse, sort of tucked in under the rocks. I only noticed because there was no light on it at all. A small boat, one mast. The kind everybody has here." She shook her head. "I didn't think. People sail."

I looked at Voss, and Voss looked at me, and neither of us said what we'd both thought. It was a hundred boats in a town of a thousand. It could have been anyone's.

"Cora." My voice was gentler than I'd expected. "You have to tell Detective Castellano. Tonight. Before she hears it from the hardware store. If you walk in yourself it's a witness. If she comes to you, it's a suspect."

"She'll arrest me."

"She might want to. She won't have a reason."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she got up and went to the corkboard and took down one of the index cards, and held it a moment, and gave it to me.

It was in a block hand, hers. *Dad, 10/7 voicemail. Transcribe.*

"He left me a message the day before," she said. "I've listened to it forty times. I didn't tell you earlier because I didn't think it mattered, and then I thought it would make me look guilty, and now I think it's the only thing I've got." She picked up her phone, thumbed it, and held it out at arm's length, as though it were a thing that might bite. "Don't say anything. Just listen."

There was a hiss, and wind, and a glass touched down on wood. Then a man's voice, thick, tired, a fine voice gone to ruin, and so near the phone I could hear him swallow.

*"Cora. It's me. I'm going to say it to you straight, because I've never said it to anyone. Your mother didn't fall. I knew, I knew the day. I let them... I let it be what it was." A long breath. "And the Brandt woman. She came to see me. Did you know that? Five years ago, a week before. Sat in my kitchen and told me what I was. She was right about all of it, every word. I've thought about her every day. I'll tell you what I—"*

The line cut off. Not a hang-up. A bump, a clatter, as if the phone had been put down on something hard, and the sound of a door and a muffled, ordinary voice in the background that said, quite clearly, in the tone you'd use to someone who had been waiting a long time for a cup of tea:

*"There you are."*

Then nothing.

Cora lowered the phone. Her eyes were wide.

"I've always thought it was the housekeeper," she said. "Mrs. Aldous. But she says she was at her sister's in Camden. She says she's never heard that voice."

I couldn't speak. In that rented room, with its corkboard and rope of red yarn, I listened to the silence the recording had left behind. *Mara went to see Alan Farrow.* She had never told me. She had sat in his kitchen a week before she died and said it to his face.

And I had heard that voice before.

I couldn't say where. It was so ordinary, so kind—the sort of voice I'd heard a thousand times without listening.
