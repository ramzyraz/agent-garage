# Chapter 6: Gray Water

The town landing at six in the morning looked like a place where nothing had ever happened to anyone.

A strip of wet concrete, a hoist, a stack of lobster crates, and the harbor lying flat and colorless beyond it. Two Port Carrow patrol cars sat nose to nose with their lights off. A uniformed officer was stringing yellow tape between a piling and a bait barrel, and the tape kept lifting in the wind, as though it didn't want to be part of this either.

The skiff was already on the hoist. Twelve feet of white fiberglass, an outboard tipped up on its bracket, a green plastic bailer. Ned Tarbell lay in the bottom boards in the wool shirt he'd worn to talk to me, buttoned to the collar. Someone had closed his eyes. His right hand still hung over the gunwale and nobody had moved it yet, the fingers just brushing the air above the water he'd been afraid of for sixty years.

I stopped at the tape. I'd seen a lot of the dead, in my line. Mostly after: in photographs, in reports, in the tidy past tense of a file. I had not seen one I'd drunk coffee with the day before.

"Brandt."

Nell Castellano came up the landing with her hands in the pockets of the black jacket and her hair coming loose from its clip. She looked as though she'd been awake for a week. She took in my face and something in her own gave a little.

"Sit down before you fall down," she said. "There's a bollard."

"I'm all right."

"You're the color of that boat." She didn't wait. She took my elbow, steered me to the bollard, and I sat. "Breathe. Then I'm going to ask you things, and you're going to answer them in order."

I breathed. A gull walked along the lobster crates, looking at us with professional interest.

"The lobsterman is Eddie Marsh," she said. "He was hauling at five, saw a skiff turning on the current with no one at the tiller. Thought somebody'd been drinking. He came alongside, found Ned. Cold, wet, face up. His head was against the thwart and his hand was in the water." She lifted her chin at the hoist. "No marks I can see. No blood. The engine was cut, not stalled. The kill-cord was in his pocket."

"He didn't go out on the water."

"So I'm told. By everyone who knew him." She crouched in front of me so our eyes were level. "Where's the envelope?"

I took it out of my jacket. I'd been carrying it in the inside pocket for three days, against the ribs, and it had warmed to the shape of my body. I gave it to her without a word.

She opened it standing, reading with her back to the wind. She read the first lines, and I watched her go still in a way that wasn't the stillness of an investigator. It was the stillness of a person who has been handed the voice of someone they thought they'd never hear again.

"Your wife's hand," she said.

"Yes."

"I know that handwriting."

I looked at her.

"From the file." It came out half a beat late. "I read her death report when I took this job. The one the state police closed. Her signature was on the incident form as a witness to nothing. It's very distinctive." She folded the page along its creases, carefully, as if it could still be hurt. "The wave."

"The wave."

"May I keep this?"

"Photograph it. I'll keep the original."

She looked at me a long moment, then nodded and took out her phone. While she did, I told her about the key, the Rockland storage unit, the clerk's refusal, and Quill's instruction on his client's behalf: *He'll want to call someone. Tell him not to.* I kept the other line to myself. *Not even the people who love you. Especially them.* I wasn't sure why. It seemed like private property.

"I can get a paper for Rockland," she said, when I'd finished. "Death certificate, estate letters. I'll have it by tomorrow if I lean on a judge, which I'm going to do anyway." She handed the envelope back. "I'm not going to say you should have shown me this Saturday."

"Thank you."

"I'm going to say you should have shown me this Saturday." She stood. "Now. Mrs. Tarbell."

---

The green house had every light on.

She sat at the yellow table in her gray cardigan with a state trooper beside her and a mug of tea going cold under her hands. She didn't look up when we came in. The calendar from the Co-op still hung on its nail. Through the window over the sink the dory sat on its blocks with the sun coming up behind it, fern-filled and absurd.

"Mrs. Tarbell," Nell said gently. "Tell it again for Mr. Brandt. As you told me."

She raised her eyes. They went past me and came back, like someone checking a stair in the dark. "The phone rang at twenty to twelve," she said. "The kitchen phone. Ned was still up. He hadn't been to bed. He was sitting where you were yesterday, with the radio on low."

"Who called?"

"He said, 'Hello.' Then he said, 'Oh. Oh, it's you.' And he listened for a while." Her mouth worked. "And he said, 'No, I can't sleep either.' And then, 'All right. I'll come down. Yes. The landing. I know where.' And he hung up and put on his coat."

"Did he say a name?"

"He said it was Mr. Brandt." She said it plainly. She looked at me, and in her face there was grief and a terrible reluctance to be unfair. "He said, 'It's Mr. Brandt. He's nervous too. He wants to go over it once, away from the house, so I don't frighten you.' And I said, Ned, it's midnight. And he said, 'He's been very good to us.' He wanted to be good back."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"It wasn't me," I said.

"I know you think so."

"Mrs. Tarbell, I was in a bed at the Anchor Inn."

"I'm not saying it was." Her hands tightened on the mug. "I'm saying he said your name. He said it twice. He wouldn't have gone for anybody else."

"No," I said. "He wouldn't."

Nell touched my shoulder. It was a very small movement, and it did not feel like comfort. It felt like a hand being placed on something to hold it still.

"Landline," she said to me, outside, in the thin cold. "Incoming number blocked. I've asked the phone company, and they'll tell me it was a prepaid in a drawer somewhere, and the drawer will be empty." She stood with her back to the house. "Do you know what I've got, Brandt? I've got a man who was terrified of water, found in a boat, after a call from a man he'd said he trusted. I've got a witness who says that man was you. I've got a tag in a dead man's fist with your name stamped on it, and a kit that went missing in the nick of time. I've got your wife's letter."

"Which says someone used me."

"Which says that to the one person who'd want it saying." She said it without cruelty. "If I were reading this file, I'd have me do exactly what I'm doing. I'd have me say come in."

"Am I under arrest?"

"I'd like it if you came in for a statement." She didn't smile. "I'd like it very much."

---

They kept me at the county building on Mill Street until half past one.

Nothing happened that I could point to. Just a room with a bolted table, a pitcher of water, and a clock above the door that ran two minutes slow. Nell had a notepad. Beside her sat a second detective, Ouellette, with the face of a man who had never been talked out of anything. I told it in order. The kitchen, the wave, the coat that went on and off. Tarbell's hand on the table and his wife in the doorway.

I told them where I'd been. The Anchor Inn, nine o'clock, chain on the door. The night clerk was a girl of twenty with a ring in her nose and a thesis on Hardy, who said she'd seen me come in at nine-thirty and go up, and had not seen me after that, because she'd been, in her words, "reading, mostly, with my headphones in." The back stairs opened onto the alley. The chain went on from the inside. I could see both detectives work out how it could have been dropped back behind a man by his own hand. I could have done it myself.

Dr. Ansel Pryor, the county medical examiner, put his head in around eleven. He was a long, pale man in his late fifties with a damp forehead and a pen he couldn't stop clicking, and he looked at me once and then did not look at me again. He said a few words to Ouellette at the door. I caught *drowning* and *consistent*. I caught *old men do odd things in the night.* I caught Nell, out in the corridor, say quite clearly, "He was afraid of the water, Doctor," and Pryor answering in the voice of someone trying to put a coat on a chair: "People surprise us."

He left. The clicking went with him down the hall.

"Preliminary time of death is between midnight and two," Nell told me afterward. She had the grace to look at the table. "Your clerk can't cover it. Nothing physical ties you to the skiff. The card—"

"What card?"

She slid a photograph across the table.

It was a business card, in an evidence bag, creased across one corner. Theo Brandt, Forensic Loss Adjuster. The little blue anchor. On the back, in blue-black ink, in a crowded hand I had been looking at on the page of my own life for forty years: *12:00 — landing.*

"They found it in his coat," she said. "Inside pocket. Dry."

I looked at the capitals. The *T*, the *L*.

It was my handwriting. Even I couldn't have told it from my own.

"I didn't write that."

"I know you say so."

"It's the same hand as page seven. It's the same person. Someone's been practicing me for six years."

"Maybe." Nell held my eyes. "Or maybe that's what a man says who's been practicing himself." She picked up the photograph, and put it away. "I'm not charging you today. I can't. You've got a story, and some of it has legs. I'd rather you were somewhere I can find you." She paused. "But I'm not stupid. I know what you are to the next person who reads this. You're going to want to go somewhere."

"Portland."

"Quill."

"He's got more. She'd have left more than a key."

"I'd have said the same." She stood and put her hands flat on the table. "You're not under arrest, and I can't stop you leaving. I can tell you the causeway closes at 11:20 tonight, and if you're on the wrong side of it I will assume you drove into the sea. And I can tell you, Brandt, that Wednesday is the fourteenth."

"I know what Wednesday is."

"Then be careful with it." She straightened. Her voice, when it came, was nearly gentle, and she didn't look at me. "She'd want you back in one piece."

I thought about that sentence all the way out of the building, and halfway to Portland. I couldn't think what business the detective had to know what Mara would want.

---

Ferris Quill's office stood at the top of a flight of stairs on Exchange Street, above a shop that sold nautical antiques. The brass nameplate had been polished so many times the letters had gone soft. A radiator knocked somewhere inside. When I rang the bell, I heard a chair scrape and a long wait, and the sound of someone looking through a peephole.

The door opened on its chain.

"Mr. Brandt." He was a small, upright, white-haired man in a gray three-piece suit, seventy at least, with the dry voice of a man who had spent his life saying *I'm afraid that's not possible.* He looked past me down the stairs, then at the street through the window on the landing, and slipped the chain. "Come in. Quickly, please. I haven't had a good week."

The office was a room of dark wood and green leather and stacked boxes. A grandfather clock. A framed photograph of a much younger Quill shaking hands with a governor. He took me to the chair opposite his desk and sat down himself without offering tea, which for a lawyer of his generation was the equivalent of a shout.

"I read about Mr. Tarbell on the radio at ten," he said. "I did not know the man. But I knew, when I heard it, that you would come."

"Why?"

"Because she told me you would." He folded his hands. "Mara came to see me in October. I won't give you the date. I've given it to myself too many times. She sat where you are sitting and she gave me two envelopes. One I sent you Thursday. The other I was to keep until you came here in person, on your own, after a second death."

"A second death."

"Her words. She said, 'He will come when it's no longer a coincidence.' She said Alan Farrow would be the first, if she was right about him, and a stranger the second, and that you would walk in here with a face like a dropped plate." He paused. "I confess I thought she was being dramatic. I thought so for five years."

"What's in the second envelope?"

"I haven't opened it." He unlocked a drawer and took out a plain manila envelope, soft with handling, a strip of tape across the flap with *FQ* initialed across the join. "She asked me not to. She asked me to ask you to read it here, while I'm in the room. She said she didn't want it in the car."

I took it. It weighed nothing. I peeled the tape.

There was one sheet inside, a color print on photo paper: a sheet of lined ledger, shot at an angle under poor light, a corner curling in someone's hand. There was a thumbnail of shadow at the top edge. At the far margin, slightly out of focus, I could make out the curve of what might have been a lamp, a glass shade the color of a bottle.

The ledger itself was a column of small, tidy entries in pencil.

Initials. Dates. A dollar sum. A column headed *Adj.*

*P.D.* A date eight years and seven months back. *$640,000.* Adj.: *T.B.*

*R.A.* A date seven years back. *$1,100,000.* Adj.: *T.B.*

*L.F.* A date in March, six years back. *$2,000,000.* Adj.: *T.B.*

Three lines. Beneath them, a short horizontal rule, as though someone had meant to add another and been interrupted. And to the right of every sum, in the margin, the same small figure: *30.*

I read it again. And a third time, because the first two had gone through me and out the other side without leaving anything.

Peter Doyle, the cannery watchman. Ruth Anselm, who fell from a cliff path at Pemaquid and left a husband with a seven-figure claim. Lydia Farrow.

I knew every one of them. I'd opened a file on each. I had closed all three.

Doyle: I'd taken the supplemental review that spring because Hollis was stretched thin and had said the file wanted a fresh eye. Anselm: I'd signed the closing, fifteen minutes before a flight to Denver, a clean one, no questions. And Lydia, of course, was the one with my name on every page.

"Mr. Brandt?" said Quill.

"Three people died," I said. I heard my own voice come from a long way off. "Over three years. Each one an accident. And every claim was paid out because I said it should be."

"Yes," said Quill quietly. "I rather gathered."

"Who photographed this?"

"I don't know. She didn't say. She said only that you would know the chair." He watched me. "I should add that my instructions were to give you nothing else. But I was a friend of your wife's father for thirty years, and I have been sitting in this room five years waiting for someone to ask me what I thought. I think a man who knew the shape of this would have wanted your name to be the only name on it."

"Someone has been building me," I said.

"Someone has been *using* you." He leaned forward. "Which is a different thing, and the difference may save your life." He took off his glasses. His eyes were small and old and very bright. "Mr. Brandt, there's a policeman in Port Carrow who I think would be glad to see that photograph."

"She'll see it. She'll see a ledger where every line says my initials." I stared at the three small letters. *T.B. T.B. T.B.* "If I were her, I'd take that as a signature."

"If you were her, you'd take that as a motive. And you'd be right." He put his glasses back on. "That is the other thing Mara said. She said that when the ledger turned up, it would look as though it were pointing at you. And that you were to keep looking."

"At what?"

"At what it wasn't saying." He glanced at the print. "I'm an old lawyer. I read what's missing from a page. Who got the thirty?"

"What?"

"The margin," said Quill. "Thirty beside every sum. Thirty percent of the claim, I should think. And you, apparently, were the adjuster. But an adjuster takes nothing." His mouth was thin. "Thirty percent went to someone. Not the beneficiary. Not you, I trust. And somebody rules the pencil lines so cleanly they have the habit of a ledger. Someone who has done it for years."

I put the photograph on the desk between us. The grandfather clock ticked and went on ticking. In the street below a bus changed gears, and the whole building shook very slightly, like a ship.

On my phone, in my pocket, a message arrived with its small flat chime. I didn't take it out. I knew without reading who it was from. It was a Port Carrow number.

I sat in Quill's green leather chair with three sets of my own initials in front of me, and thought: *I know Doyle's file. I know Anselm's.* And then, because I'd been trained by the best: *Who put them on my desk?*
