# Chapter 15: What He Owed

I put the phone on its charger and stood beside it until there was enough battery to leave.

Six minutes. I watched the little green bar grow and thought about Alan Farrow deciding which pen to use. Sitting at a desk, addressing an envelope to me, setting down the fact that he had known my wife was going to die.

He had lived another five years after knowing it.

I had spent those years learning to sleep in the middle of a bed.

At the bottom of the stairs the clerk asked whether I wanted breakfast. I was still carrying Hollis's brown paper bag. The bacon grease had made a dark circle in one corner.

"I've had it," I said.

Outside, the wind pushed me sideways against the inn's wall. The rain had stopped, but water came down from the gutters every time a gust shook them. I took the long way to Sparrow Lane, through streets where there were people. A delivery man rolled a handcart down a ramp. Two women waited for the pharmacy to open. I wanted witnesses to the ordinary fact of me walking somewhere.

Sayer was beside her car when I reached Cora's house. She wore the same suit with a raincoat over it. Her hair was damp at the temples.

"I checked your conditions," she said. "You can meet them. If anything turns into an accusation about last night, we stop."

"This is about my wife."

"I know. That's why I wanted to be here."

She looked at the bag in my hand.

"Have you eaten that?"

"No."

"Then bring it in."

---

Voss opened the door before we knocked. He had his hat in his hand and his coat on, as though he had never quite committed to staying. Beyond him Cora stood beside the kitchen table. There were four chairs, three cups of coffee and a transparent folder with a cream-colored envelope inside it.

She looked at Sayer.

"She's my lawyer," I said.

"Ellen Sayer. I'm here for Mr. Brandt."

Cora nodded. Her eyes came back to me and stayed there.

"I didn't know," she said.

"I haven't read it yet."

"I know. I need you to know that first."

Voss drew out a chair without scraping it. "Castellano's coming. She said fifteen minutes. The original stays where it is. I photographed it when we opened it."

He put a printout at the empty place. Then another, the envelope front and back. Ordinary paper for me to hold. The thing Alan had touched lay under plastic a foot away.

I sat down.

*Theodore Brandt*, the envelope said. No street. No stamp. Under my name, in smaller writing: *To be given to him personally.*

Voss waited until I'd looked at both sides.

"Mrs. Aldous," he said. "She left a message at my office at seven this morning. Her nephew drove it down from Camden."

"She found Alan."

"Yes. She'd been looking after him twenty-three years. She went to her sister's after the funeral. I've been trying to speak with her since Monday. This morning she saw your name in the news."

"Why didn't she give it to the police?"

"Ask her. Nell will. What she told me was that he made her promise to put it into your hands, nobody else's. She thought you'd come to the house. He'd told her you might."

"A week."

Voss nodded once. He didn't offer a better explanation.

On the printout of the envelope there was a second hand, rounder than Alan's, at the lower left.

*Given to me Thursday, 8 October, after lunch. M.A.*

"She wrote that this morning," Voss said. "Not the eighth. I asked."

I had been about to believe it was a dated receipt. I looked up at him.

"And she opened it?"

"No. We did. Cora recognized his hand. That isn't an examination. It's just what we have."

Cora pulled out the chair opposite mine. She didn't sit in it. She held its back with both hands.

I put down the envelope photographs and picked up the letter.

---

*Theo,*

*I knew what they were going to do to Mara. I let them.*

*She came here and asked me to tell the truth about Lydia. I told her she had misunderstood. She hadn't. I knew she wasn't safe after that visit. I could have warned her. I could have warned you. Instead I waited for her to stop asking, and when she died I let them call it an accident.*

*I have told myself that knowing isn't the same as doing. It is what has allowed me to get up in the morning. I can't make it do that any more.*

*I need to tell you about Lydia first. What I agreed to and what I paid. Then I need to tell you what happened after your wife came here. I won't put a name into a letter Mrs. Aldous has to carry. She knows nothing about any of this. If I get the courage to say it, I mean to say it sitting opposite you.*

*Please come to the house. Ask Cora to arrange it if you won't take a call from me. There is money for her now which I should have spent keeping her mother alive. It does not settle anything. I understand that.*

*If you take this to the police, I have no right to object. I am asking for the chance to tell you myself before I tell them.*

*Alan Farrow*

*8 October*

There was a space between the last sentence and the signature. He had left himself room to say something else.

I read it again, looking for the misunderstanding. A qualification. Something that would turn *knew* into *suspected*, something I could argue with while I caught my breath.

There wasn't one.

Behind me a pipe knocked in the wall. Upstairs, somebody crossed a room. The kitchen clock made a little scraping sound each time its second hand passed the twelve.

Mara had come home from that house. She had put her keys somewhere. Perhaps she had asked what I wanted for dinner. For five years I had searched the days before her death for things I might have done differently. Alan had known what he ought to do while she was still alive.

"What happened after she came?" I asked.

Cora shook her head.

"Did he tell you?"

"No."

I heard how short the word was. I wanted her to make it longer. I wanted something to get hold of.

Sayer pushed the brown bag into my line of sight.

"Put the page down a moment."

"I don't want food."

"Then put your hands on the table."

I did. I had folded the printout across Alan's signature without knowing it.

Cora sat.

"You can hate him," she said. "I won't ask you not to."

"I don't need your permission."

She looked down. Voss moved his hat from one hand to the other.

It was an ugly thing to have said to her, and it had come out with no effort at all.

"You didn't write it," I said.

"No."

"I know."

She brought her eyes up again. We sat across from each other with Alan's letter between us. Her father had killed her mother. My reports had helped him stay her father.

---

Nell came through the door at ten past ten, carrying an evidence bag and a small notebook. She looked at my hands before she looked at the letter.

"Copies," Voss said. "Original hasn't been handled since we put it in the folder."

She asked him to take her through the morning. He gave her the times, his office message, the nephew's arrival, the opening. She wrote down who had touched what. Then she photographed the letter where it lay and put the folder and envelope into the bag together.

"Mrs. Aldous needs to keep the phone she used," she said. "I'll speak to her today. And the nephew. I need both numbers."

Voss gave them to her.

"Is it enough?" Cora asked.

Nell looked at her. "Enough for what?"

"To stop saying my father fell."

"We aren't saying that."

"Enough to believe him, then."

"I believe he wanted to tell Mr. Brandt something. This helps establish what. We'll check the writing and what Mrs. Aldous says. Then we'll see what we can support outside the letter."

"He wrote that he knew," I said.

"Yes."

"Don't turn that into nothing."

Nell closed her notebook, leaving a finger between the pages.

"I won't. And I won't turn it into something it can't prove. He's dead, Theo. He doesn't get to answer a question. We have to find people who can."

Cora took a phone from her cardigan pocket and set it on the table.

"He rang me on the eighth," she said. "I told you that. I didn't tell you all of it."

Nell's finger came out of the notebook.

"Why?"

"Because I thought he was trying to get me back."

---

The call was on Cora's log: Thursday, 8 October, 4:36 p.m., incoming, eleven minutes.

"I didn't record it," she said. "This only shows the call."

Nell nodded. "Tell me what you remember. Use his words where you're sure of them."

"He asked whether I could come home. I told him I had work. He said it mattered. I said it had mattered in June, when I asked him to speak to Barrett and he told me to get off his property."

Voss's eyes stayed on the floor.

"Then he said he'd tell me everything. About Mum. He'd said it in the voicemail, but I hadn't believed it properly. I thought he'd say he'd had an affair, or he'd been drunk, or he'd taken her out when the weather was bad. Something he could confess to and still be a person I could stand to see."

She put her thumb against the side of her phone. The screen went dark.

"I asked him if she was murdered."

The kitchen held still around her.

"He said yes."

"Did he say by whom?" Nell asked.

"I asked. He said he couldn't do it on the telephone. He wanted me to come."

"Did he say that he killed her?"

"He said, 'I'm responsible.' Then he said he had to speak to Mr. Brandt. He wanted me to arrange it. He thought you wouldn't see him if he called you himself."

I looked at the salutation on the printout. *Theo.* A man's first name, doing work that five years of silence could not.

"What else?" Nell asked.

Cora looked at me instead of her.

"He said your wife had tried to give him a way out. She said he'd have to tell what he'd done, but he could stop anything else happening. He said he sent her away. That's when he started crying."

She gripped the chair seat beneath her thighs.

"I told him to call the police. He said he would. After he'd seen me and you. He said, 'I don't want you hearing what your father is from a stranger.'"

"And you agreed to go?"

"Not then. I was angry. I'd spent four months paying someone to ask him questions, and he'd called me cruel for doing it. I said if he had something to confess he could do it without making me drive three hours to listen."

Voss finally looked up.

"You didn't make him die," he said.

Cora shook her head hard, once, as if he had touched a bruise.

"He said someone was coming that evening. He wanted to speak to them first. I asked whether it was his lawyer. He said no. I remember what he said after that exactly."

Nell opened the notebook again.

"'Someone I trusted is coming for me.'"

I watched Nell write it down.

"Coming *for* him?"

"Those were the words. I asked what he meant. He said, 'If I can't put this right with him, I need you here.'"

"Him," Nell said.

"Yes."

"A name?"

"No. I asked again. He wouldn't give me one. He said he had to get through that conversation first."

She looked at the black phone screen as though another call might still appear on it.

"He said ten thirty. I told him I couldn't promise. Then I sat in my room for an hour, telling myself I wasn't going."

"But you did," I said.

"Yes."

She had driven through the dark to a man who said he was responsible for her mother's murder. She had crossed a road the tide was about to close and knocked on the boathouse door. I thought of her out there with the light in the loft, waiting to hear his steps.

"I said he was apologizing," she told Nell. "When you asked what the call was about. That was true. It wasn't all of it."

Nell let a moment pass.

"If you remember more, call me. Even if it seems to contradict this. Don't rehearse it together."

Cora nodded.

---

Voss took another folder from his coat. This one he handed to Nell.

"The policy increase," he said. "Cora authorized me to speak to the broker about her claim. There's a recording of the instruction. I've given you his number and a copy of the email confirming it."

He had the recording on his phone too. Nell asked him to play it.

Alan's voice came out thin and businesslike. A man confirming a premium, agreeing that he understood the quoted figure. A woman asked whether he wished to retain the existing beneficiary designation.

*My daughter. Yes.*

*And you would like the accidental-death benefit increased to four point two million?*

*That's what I said.*

Then a pause. Paper moved. Alan spoke again, less sharply.

*I'm sorry. Yes, please. Four point two.*

The broker thanked him. He asked when it would take effect.

Voss stopped the recording.

"The broker says he initiated it. Rang her office, asked what cover he could buy at his age. No daughter on the call. No request sent through her. You'll want the original recording from them."

"I will," Nell said.

The money had been the first thing Reyes gave me. Four point two million dollars, followed by the name of the woman who would get it. In my work that was how a person became a suspect before I met them.

"I didn't know he'd changed it," Cora said. "The solicitor told me after he died."

"You thought he was buying forgiveness," I said.

"I think he wanted to leave me something after he went to prison."

She said it without heat. It was a worse thing to have to say about a father than anything I could offer her.

Voss tapped the folder once.

"This doesn't clear her of anything by itself. But the increase wasn't her instruction. That's a fact we can get checked."

Nell put the papers beside the evidence bag.

"All right."

For the first time Cora let go of the chair.

---

Nell stepped into the hall to telephone Camden. Voss went with her, taking his phone and leaving his hat on the table. Sayer stayed where she could see me.

Cora stood at the sink and ran cold water into a glass. She drank half of it, then put it down so carefully I could hear the base meet the enamel.

"He said there was something worse than going to prison," she said.

"On that call?"

"Yes. I didn't understand. I asked if he meant me finding out. He said I'd been living with it since I was a child. Finding out wouldn't be the worst part."

Sayer lifted her head.

"Tell Detective Castellano that when she comes back."

"I will."

Cora turned to me.

"Did your wife know him? Before she went there?"

"Through the file, perhaps. Not socially."

"Was she afraid of him?"

I could have invented a brave Mara for her. I had done it often enough for myself: my wife going alone into offices and houses, making copies, posting letters, leaving instructions against her own death. A woman who knew exactly what she was risking.

But Mara had been afraid of ordinary things too. Skidding on black ice. Missing a call from her mother. A pain she couldn't explain. Fear hadn't kept her from doing anything she needed to do; that didn't mean she hadn't felt it.

"I don't know," I said. "She went anyway."

Cora turned the glass a little with her fingertip.

"I want the money held," she said. "Until we know where it came from."

"It's insurance money."

"So was the money he got when Mum died."

I had no answer to that.

---

Before Nell left, she made Cora repeat the last part of the call. She arranged for another officer to take a fuller statement that afternoon. Voss would stay until then. Cora agreed to tell Nell where she went, and to take no private meetings with anyone who claimed to know her father.

"That includes people who say they're helping," Nell said.

She looked at me when she said it.

Outside, Sayer stopped beside her car.

"Send me what you have on the fifth," she said. "Addresses, employers, old bank details. I'll ask for the records. You need some sleep."

I looked back at the downstairs window. Voss was pulling the blind halfway down. Cora's shape moved behind him, carrying the cups.

"Pryor wrote that I wanted her stopped," I said. "Alan knew someone wanted her stopped."

"That doesn't make them independent accounts of what you did."

"It doesn't put me anywhere else either."

"No," said Sayer. "That's the work."

Nell waited at the pavement for her to finish. The wind lifted a corner of the evidence bag against her coat.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"The inn."

"Stay there. I'll call after I've spoken to Aldous."

"Will you speak to Rouse again?"

"Yes."

"And Hollis?"

"Yes."

I nodded. I couldn't make myself say anything more about him. He had been awake all night for me, and I was sending Nell to his door.

She put the bag into her car and returned to the pavement.

"You did the right thing this morning," she said. "Keep doing it."

---

I walked back with the printouts inside my coat. I still had the breakfast bag. At the inn I put it into the bin beside the stairs, then took it out again and carried it up.

I ate standing by the desk. The bread was damp and the bacon cold. Hollis had gone somewhere before dawn to buy it for me. He had sat holding water outside a room where men were deciding whether I had killed somebody. Whatever I didn't know about him, those things had happened.

I set Alan's letter beside my copy of Pryor's statement.

One man asking me to hear him. Another warning the world against me. Both dead before either could explain.

I wanted to ring Hollis and tell him about the morning. I wanted his voice to take the two pages and put them in an order I could stand to look at.

Instead I photographed my old Portland address and the account details from the papers in my bag, and sent them to Sayer. Then I plugged in the phone.

It rang before I could lie down.

Hollis.

I let it sound twice. I thought of the breakfast wrapper, and his hand on my shoulder, and answered.

"Theo." He sounded out of breath. "Have you got a minute?"

"Yes."

"I've found the book. The old desk diary. There's something here we ought to—"

He stopped. Away from the telephone I heard a knock, three quick blows.

"Hang on," he said. "Someone at the door."

I waited. A chair moved. He set the phone down on a hard surface without ending the call.

His steps went away. A latch lifted.

"Yes?" he said, farther off.

Then a sound that wasn't a word. Something heavy struck the floor.

"Hollis?"

There was a scraping noise, and a breath close to the receiver. I had already pulled the charger loose.

"Hollis. Can you hear me?"

The man who had held me through five years of nights tried to say my name.

He couldn't finish it.
