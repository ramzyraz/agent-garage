# Chapter 3: Tag 0417

The county building on Mill Street was a brick box from the sixties with a flagpole and no ambitions. At ten past ten at night the front was dark. Around the back, under a caged bulb, a steel loading door stood open six inches, and Nell Castellano stood in the gap with her arms crossed, as if she had been holding it against the weather for some time.

"You took your time."

"I ate stew."

"Of course you did." She stepped back to let me in. "Walk where I walk."

We went down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and refrigeration, past a door marked with a county seal and the word MORGUE in letters somebody had meant to make friendly. She didn't take me in there. She took me instead to a windowless room with a steel table and two chairs, and shut the door, and put on the table a clear evidence bag with a flat brass tag inside.

I didn't pick it up. I didn't have to.

It was the old kind, the kind Atlantic Mutual stopped issuing the year before I left, a stamped oval the size of a pocket watch with a hole punched at the top and a loop of waxed cord run through it. The number was cut in with a hand die, the digits a little off the line. *T. BRANDT.* A dot. *0417.* The cord had been cut, not untied, and what remained was stiff and dark with water.

"That's mine," I said.

"Say more."

"The format's mine. I stamped every exhibit that way in my last three years at Atlantic. The claims office had a kit: dies, a block, a mallet. When they went to printed labels I bought the kit off the supply cage for forty dollars." I made myself look at it. "T. Brandt is my name. Zero-four-one-seven is the exhibit number. April, the seventeenth entry. Or the seventeenth exhibit of that year. I never decided which."

"And what was exhibit 0417?"

"The ship's log from the *Salt Ledger.* Lydia Farrow's boat. It was in a sealed bag with this tag tied through the zip. The log went to Atlantic's evidence archive in Portland when I closed the file, and the file says the tag went with it." I heard myself saying it. "The file also says it never left my office, which is a contradiction I noticed about forty minutes ago."

"Forty minutes ago you were at dinner."

"I read the whole file on the road. The exhibit list on page twenty-nine says the log was tagged and bagged and held in my office pending transfer. Page thirty says transferred. Nobody mentions a tag coming off."

Nell sat. She did it slowly and kept her eyes on me while she did. "Was it ever off?"

"Not that I know of."

"Where's your kit now?"

"My office. In a banker's box in the closet, under the bucket." I paused. "Under the second bucket."

"You have two buckets."

"The roof has opinions."

She didn't smile, which told me it was going to be that kind of evening. She took out a notebook and uncapped a pen, and for the next hour I was a suspect in the death of Alan Farrow.

She was good at it. I know, because I've done the same job from the other chair. She never raised her voice. She asked the questions in the wrong order and then in the right one, and she wrote down what I said without looking at the page, and every few minutes she asked something I'd already answered, in different words, to see whether it came back the same.

Where was I on the night of the eighth? In Portland, alone, until about ten, and then still alone. Did anyone see me? The man who runs the chandery downstairs saw my light. Did I speak to anyone on the phone? No. Had I ever been inside the Farrow boathouse? Never. Had I seen Alan Farrow since Lydia died? Once, at the hearing on the estate. He hadn't spoken to me.

"Why did Atlantic pick you?"

"Reyes said I was the only adjuster who'd already read the file."

"Reyes sent you the file at 9:12 and called you at 9:12."

I stopped. I hadn't told her that. "How do you know that?"

"I asked his office for the time the file went out. They had it to the second. He was proud of it." She turned a page. "You didn't think that was strange?"

"I thought it was efficient. Then I thought it was strange."

"And in between?"

"I had other things on my mind."

"Such as?"

I thought about the letter. I thought about the key beside my car keys, and a dead woman's voice saying *especially them.* It was a long way to the surface of that answer.

"My wife's anniversary," I said. "Five days."

"October fourteenth," said Nell.

It was a light thing, said lightly, and I almost didn't catch it. But I hadn't told her the date. I had said *five days,* and she'd put the date on it the way you do with a number you've carried a long time. I looked at her. She was looking at her notebook, and her pen had stopped.

"It's in the paper," I said. "When she died."

"It is." The pen started again. "Five years. I'm sorry."

It sounded like a thing she'd had to say. It sounded, I thought later, like a thing she'd had to *not* say something else instead.

"Let me tell you what I have," she said. "I have a man dead in a locked room who was strong enough, and sober enough on a normal night, to be hard to drown. I have a bottle of whisky with only his prints on it. I have a boathouse door that was latched from the inside, on a night when the tide came over the sill. I have a tag with your name on it in a dead man's hand. And I have you, the man who signed off on his first wife's death, arriving the day after, from a town two hours away, with a face like you've been up since Tuesday."

"It's Saturday."

"It's Sunday," she said. "Past midnight."

I hadn't known that either.

"Here's what I don't have," she went on. "I don't have a reason for you to put your own name in his hand. If you did it, you're a fool, and you don't strike me as a fool. If someone else did, they went to the trouble of getting a thing out of your old files and putting it where I'd find it. That means they wanted me to look at you. Or wanted you to look at yourself." She closed the notebook. "Which is it, Mr. Brandt?"

"I don't know."

"Good. That's the first honest answer you've given me."

She went to a metal cabinet against the wall and took out a manila folder, and from it a sheaf of papers stapled at the corner. She laid it in front of me, face up.

"Scene inventory. Everything we bagged in the boathouse. I'd like you to read it and tell me what's missing from the story you're building."

It was eleven pages of small type. I did what I'd been taught and read it twice, once for the story and once for the seams. Tools. Line. Paint. A pair of reading glasses, a dead flashlight, a cushion from the bench. The bottle, the tumbler, the prints. A brass padlock without a key. A wallet and a set of house keys in Alan's coat, which had been hanging by the door. Two rolls of paper towel, one wet. Under *Trash receptacle (galvanized, beside bench)* a short, boring list: a crushed beer can, a cigar butt, a banana peel, a used tea bag with string and paper tag, a torn receipt from Hanley's Hardware. Under *Sink:* one tumbler, inverted, dry, clean, no prints.

"Clean," I said. "No prints at all."

"Rinsed and wiped. Or never used." She leaned on the table. "Alan Farrow lived alone. A housekeeper comes in at seven. He drinks his own whisky from his own glass, and a second glass, washed and dry, is sitting on the drainboard of a sink in a boathouse. Why?"

"Because he had a guest."

"Or because he was tidy."

"Is he tidy?"

"His housekeeper says he left coffee cups in every room of the house for thirty years." She tapped the line with a fingernail. "So I'll say it again. Why?"

"Someone drank with him. Or was offered a drink and said no."

"And left, and latched the door behind them?" She shook her head. "You can't latch that door from outside. I tried it for an hour this afternoon with a screwdriver, a coat hanger and some very bad language. Then I got a locksmith who said you can't do it, either. I hated that."

"Then he was alone."

"Then he was alone, or a ghost took the other glass." She straightened. "That's two things I can't make fit. A door and a glass. I've learned to be nervous when it's two. One's an accident. Three's a conspiracy. Two is somebody thinking."

I read the line about the glass again, and the tea bag, and the hardware receipt, and none of them told me anything. A man had been in his boathouse with a bottle of whisky and a banana. It was a night in a storm. I pushed the paper back to her.

"It's a good inventory," I said. "I don't see what you don't."

"Neither do I." She looked tired. "I'm going to ask you something, and I'd like you to think before you answer it. Is there anything you aren't telling me?"

I thought about the envelope in the inside pocket of my jacket, which was on the back of my chair. The brass key in my pocket. *Not anyone. Not even the people who love you.*

"Yes," I said.

She waited.

"I can't tell you yet."

"Can't, or won't?"

"I got something last night. It's from someone I trust more than I trust myself. She asked me not to tell anyone until I'd seen what it leads to. I don't know why. I'm going to find out. When I know, I'll tell you." I heard how it sounded. "I realize that's what a guilty man would say."

"It's what a guilty man would say," Nell agreed. "It's also what an honest one would say, if he was scared." She stood, and I stood, and for a moment we were two tired people in a windowless room. "I'll give you until the ME report. Tuesday. After that I come looking, and I bring paper."

"Fair."

"It isn't," she said. "But it's what I've got." She opened the door. "Stay in Port Carrow. Don't leave town. Don't touch the boathouse. Don't talk to Cora Farrow."

"Why not Cora?"

"Because she'll talk to you first, and I want to know what she says."

---

The Anchor Inn had eleven rooms, and by the look of the lot I had the only guest. A night clerk with a paperback gave me a key to number six and a look that said the town had already talked. The room smelled of lemon polish and old radiator. The window faced the harbor and the breakwater. I pulled the curtain across it without looking and lay on the bed in my clothes with the tag still behind my eyes.

I slept for a few hours. I know because I woke at six to a gray light, a gull on the sill, and the sure feeling that someone had been standing at the foot of the bed. The room was empty. The chain was on the door.

At seven I went down to the dining room for coffee and found that the town had indeed talked. The waitress put a cup in front of me without being asked and said, in a low voice, "You'll want the corner table." I didn't know why until I saw who was sitting at the next one.

She was twenty-four and looked like her mother. I'd lived with her photograph for a month, six years ago: the long jaw, the dark brows, a stubbornness that had gone to the bones early. Cora Farrow sat very straight with both hands around a mug and her coat still on, as though she'd refused the invitation to stay. She had the flat, scrubbed look of someone who hadn't slept and didn't intend to apologize for it.

"Mr. Brandt."

"Ms. Farrow. I'm sorry for your loss."

"Which one?" Her voice was low and steady, with no softness in it anywhere. "I'm not asking to be difficult. I've had two in six years, and I've never been sure which of you people to thank for the paperwork."

"I'm sorry about both."

"Don't be. Sit down."

I sat.

"I'm told you can't talk to me," she said. "The police woman told me that at the house. She wanted me to know it. I thought you should hear that I don't care." She took a breath. "My father is dead and I'm going to be rich and everyone in this town thinks I did it. I don't mind. I want to know who did."

"Then we want the same thing."

"I doubt it." She reached into the pocket of her coat and put something on the table between us. A folded sheet of paper, soft at the creases, as though it had been opened and closed many times. "My mother called you."

I didn't touch it.

"Eight days before she died," said Cora. "March fifth. I was eighteen, at school in Vermont. She called me that night from the kitchen and told me she was frightened of my father, and that she'd phoned a man at his insurance company who'd promised to look into it. A man who said he'd take care of it." Her voice didn't waver, and I understood that was the cost of it. "She never said the name. She said, 'He was very kind.' Then she died, and a man named Theo Brandt came to the funeral in a good coat and told me he was sorry, and said there was no sign of anything wrong."

"Ms. Farrow—"

"I've kept her phone bill for six years. It was in a drawer at the house. I took it the week after the funeral, because I thought, someday." She slid the paper an inch toward me. "That's the page. The number's highlighted."

I unfolded it. It was a cell phone statement for March, a single sheet with a column of numbers and times. One line, near the bottom of the page, was marked in yellow.

*03/05  4:52 PM  Outgoing  207-555-0188  9 min.*

I knew the number. I'd had it for eleven years.

It was the direct line to my desk at Atlantic Mutual. It had stayed with me through three office moves and a transfer. And I'd have remembered a call.

"I never spoke to your mother," I said. "I never spoke to her before her death. I met her in a photograph."

"Nine minutes," said Cora. "Somebody answered it. Nobody has that long to leave a message."

"I was in the field on the fifth. I know that because—" I stopped, because I didn't know it. I only knew the file said I'd been in the field, in a hand I'd always trusted. I tried to summon the day and got a gray square, flat and dry, like a photograph of a wall.

*March fifth. Eight days before she died.* Six years ago. Sober. I'd been six months sober and counting every one.

"Mr. Brandt?"

"I didn't take that call," I said. "Somebody did."

"Then who?"

I looked at the page. The yellow line. The number I'd had for eleven years, answered for nine minutes by a man who'd been kind, and who had, a week later, typed a page I'd never seen and signed it with my name.
