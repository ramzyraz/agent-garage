# Chapter 17: The Quiet Voice

I told Nell in the corridor, before the curtain had finished swinging shut behind me.

"Gray. He said gray. A shape in the door. And the man who came spoke so quietly Hollis could hardly hear him, standing face to face."

She didn't take out her notebook. She looked at the officer on the plastic chair, then at the door of Hollis's room, then at me.

"Those words?"

"'Could hardly hear him at all.'"

"Did you ask him anything?"

"I asked if it was a coat. He said he couldn't see. Then he put his hand up to his mouth."

"His mouth."

"Like this."

I showed her. The back of my fingers, held a little way from my lips. It looked like a man hushing a child.

Nell stood very still. I had watched her do it at the boathouse and in the Texaco lot, the way she held a thought until it stopped moving.

"Hal Rouse whispers," she said.

"Since the surgery."

"He wears a cardigan."

"Navy."

"In a doorway with the light behind him, navy is gray." She took out her phone. "I'm not saying it's him. I'm saying I'd have to be an idiot not to knock."

"He told Quill's secretary he was in all week."

"Then it won't take long."

She walked off down the corridor, already speaking. I heard *Congress Street* and *Ruiz* and *this afternoon*. I stood by Hollis's door with the gesture still in my hand.

I should have felt something close to gratitude. A man in a gray coat, a voice you had to lean toward. Someone who frightened Hal Rouse into silence for five years and had finally decided to do something else. It fit the shape I'd drawn at the Atlantic building, the terrified man on the fourth floor who had looked away.

It fitted so well I could have built it myself.

I went back in. Hollis had turned his face to the window and the dressing caught the light. I couldn't tell whether he was asleep.

"Told her," I whispered.

His fingers moved on the sheet. Nothing else.

---

Nell was away forty minutes. When she came back her coat was wet at the shoulders and she had a paper cup in each hand.

She gave me one. It was tea. I held it without drinking.

"Rouse left his office at eleven forty this morning," she said. "Jean Keene says he took a call, put his cardigan on over his arm instead of his shoulders, and told her he'd be back after lunch. He hasn't come back. His car isn't in the garage. His wife's in Florida with her sister; she hasn't spoken to him since Wednesday."

"Eleven forty."

"Hollis rang you at what time?"

"Twenty past one. About."

"Which gives him enough time to get to Port Carrow, if he drove straight." She looked at her cup. "It also gives him a lot of time to be somewhere else."

"The guard at the Atlantic building?"

"Saw him go. Didn't see him come back. Ruiz is at the Falmouth house. Nobody home, two newspapers on the step."

"Two."

"Yes. I noticed that too."

"Reyes?" I said.

"Reyes is supposed to be in a meeting in Boston until six, according to his assistant. She read me his calendar. I'll verify it." She drank some of the tea and grimaced. "It's very bad tea."

I looked at mine and put it on the floor.

"This doesn't clear anybody," Nell said. "Hal ran. That could be guilt. It could be fear. A man whose name Alan Farrow wouldn't write down and whom a dead lawyer went to see forty minutes before he drove his car into a tidal flat is entitled to be frightened."

"You think he's frightened."

"I think everybody in this case is frightened except you, and I'd like to know why."

I didn't tell her I was. I'd stopped feeling it somewhere around the stretcher. What remained was a hum in my forearms, like the first week without a drink.

She sat beside me for a while without speaking. Down the corridor a man was mopping the same square of floor.

"The diary," I said.

"Evidence room in Rockland. Sealed. Dill will carry it to Augusta himself. Sayer's asked to be present when it's opened. I told her yes."

"Hollis said I could—"

"Hollis said take it for safekeeping. Nobody's opening it without his lawyer or yours. That includes me."

For the first time that day I almost smiled. Even her rules had rules.

---

At twenty to four Cora called my phone.

She sounded flat and tired and not quite herself, as though somebody had taken her statement and kept something. I stepped into the stairwell to hear.

"They've finished. Detective Pelletier. He was kind." A breath. "Barrett's gone."

"Gone where?"

"He said he was going to buy a sandwich. He's been gone an hour and a half. He took the car. I rang and it went to voicemail."

I put my back against the cold wall.

"Did he say anything else?"

"He asked me what Rouse looked like. I told him I'd never seen him. He said, fine, I'll go find out." She stopped. "That's a stupid thing for me to say. I didn't take it seriously. He's always saying things like that."

"Did he say where?"

"Something about a camp. Rouse has a camp. Mrs. Keene told him. In Friendship." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Theo, he's fifty-six and he has a bad knee."

"I'll tell Nell."

"Tell her *now*."

I went through the door at a run. It was a stupid way to move through a hospital and a nurse called after me. Nell was standing at the nurses' station with a clipboard I hadn't seen her pick up.

I said *Friendship*, and *camp*, and *Voss*. She was already dialing.

"Rouse has a camp on Muscongus Bay," she said, when she had spoken to someone and put the phone against her shoulder. "Bought it in the nineties. Keene told Voss at ten this morning. That's the first I've heard of it."

"He rang her before he rang anyone."

"He rang her because she's the one person who'd talk to him." She was putting her coat on while she said it, shifting her phone from hand to hand. "Ouellette's three minutes from Route 1. Stay with Hollis."

"Nell."

"Stay with Hollis. I mean it. If someone's coming to finish a job, that's where he'll come."

I stayed. I stood in the corridor with my hands against the wall and watched her go.

---

The call came at ten past six.

It wasn't Nell. It was Ouellette, with the wind in his phone. The detective who had held me at two in the morning told me, in flat sentences, that Barrett Voss had been found on the camp road off Route 220 by a man walking a dog, lying in the weeds beside his car. He had been shot twice. Once through the thigh and once in the left side, under the arm. He was conscious when they put him in the ambulance.

"He asked for you," Ouellette said.

"Where is he?"

"Coming here. Same hospital. Detective Castellano's with the car."

"Is he going to live?"

He didn't answer quickly enough.

They brought him in through the emergency doors at twenty to seven. I watched from the end of the corridor, where a security man held out his arm for me. I saw a gurney, a paramedic walking beside it with a bag held high, and a tweed sleeve trailing off the edge, brown and soaked black.

His hat was on the gurney. Someone had put it on his stomach, upside down, like a bowl.

Cora arrived with an officer fifteen minutes after that. She came through the doors without a coat. She didn't ask me anything. She sat on the chair next to mine, put her hands under her thighs, and looked at the floor.

"I told him to go," she said after a while. "I said, 'Fine, go.'"

"You didn't."

"I thought it."

I said nothing. I'd thought it too. I had asked Nell to ask him to stay with her.

---

At seven-thirty Nell came back.

She had been at the camp road for an hour. She had mud on her boots up to the ankle and she wasn't wearing her rain jacket. She told me this standing up, in a quiet voice that wasn't kind and wasn't cruel.

Voss's car had been pulled off the road at an angle, driver's door open. Two shots. The first through the windscreen, high on the passenger side, glass all across the seat; he'd got out. The second had caught him as he ran. The shooter had been close, close enough that Voss's coat smelled of powder when they cut it off.

"The camp's a cedar-shingle thing at the end of a gravel drive," she said. "Rouse's Buick was in it. Engine cold. Keys on the kitchen table. No Rouse."

"Inside?"

"Not a mark. Table set for one, as though he'd made himself soup and stepped out. Bed made. Cardigan on the chair."

"Gray?"

She looked at me.

"It's navy," she said. "I've held it in my hands. I don't know what that tells us. A man leaves his coat behind to be found. Or he doesn't leave anything and somebody else makes it look like he did."

"Two shots," I said.

"Nine millimeter, I'd say. We'll know."

"Hal Rouse doesn't own a gun."

"No. I'll check." She held my gaze. "You've been in a room with him. Tell me honestly. Would he have shot a man in the back?"

I thought of the credenza and the green lamp. His whisper, and his hands, which had shaken so badly at the Atlantic building that he'd given up on the cardigan buttons.

"No."

"Would he have hired someone?"

"I don't know."

She nodded once. It was her answer to most things when she was too tired to argue with them.

"Voss said something to the paramedic before he went under. 'Tell Brandt. Phone.'" She took a clear evidence bag from her pocket, the kind with a red seal. Inside, a battered black phone with a cracked screen. "It was in his hand. Muddy, but it works. It's evidence, and you can't touch it. But he wanted you to see it, so I called Sayer, and she said I could play you what's on it. Here. Outside."

---

We went down to the parking structure and stood next to her car with the dome light on. She had plugged the phone into a cable and put it on the hood. A rectangle of weak screen light shone up under our chins.

"He called your number at five fifty-one," she said. "You didn't answer. You were in the stairwell with Cora."

"I didn't hear it ring."

"It went to voicemail. Forty-four seconds. The recording's on his phone and yours. I wanted to hear it with you first."

She pressed play.

Wind. Gravel under feet, then a door shutting, the dull *chunk* of a car door. A man breathing hard. Voss's voice, low and conversational, as though he had walked out of the room to take a call:

*"Brandt, it's Voss. I'm at the camp road. There's a gray... hold on, I'll call you back."*

A pause. The sound of something heavy being set down. Then a voice that wasn't Voss's.

It was so soft the phone barely caught it. A dry, scraped whisper, with no vowels in it, only air with the shape of words:

*"Mr. Voss. You have something of mine."*

"Who—" said Voss.

*"Put it on the ground, please."*

"Hal? Hal, is that you? I'm not here to—"

A pause. Then a single sound that might have been a laugh, or might have been someone clearing a ruined throat.

The first shot.

Nell stopped it.

We stood there with the mud drying on our shoes. A truck went down the ramp behind us and its lights swung across the pillars.

"You know that voice," she said.

"It sounds like Hal."

"Does it?"

"It sounds like what I'd call a Hal." I put my hand over my mouth and took it away again. "You can't tell anything from a whisper. That's the point of one. It's the same whether it's a man with a damaged throat or a man who wants you to think so."

"Say it again."

"A person could imitate it."

"Who?"

I had my answer ready. *Anyone.* I had put that word in the air before I noticed how it had changed in my mouth, how it had come out tight, how it left room for somebody I loved in a hospital bed.

"Anyone who'd heard him," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment.

"That's what I thought you'd say."

"It's what the sound says."

"It is." She reached for the phone and unplugged it. "Voss said 'gray.' Hollis said 'gray.' Both of them in the same hour, on the same day, about the same man. I'd call that a pattern if it wasn't also the only color anybody in Maine has ever seen in the dark."

"Is that a joke?"

"I'm too tired for jokes."

She slid the phone into its bag and sealed it.

"I want you to go up and sit with Hollis until the night officer arrives at nine. I want you to sleep, if you can. And I want you to stop calling Hal Rouse a hero for trying to hide, because every time you almost do it I can see your face."

"I don't think he's a hero."

"You think he's a coward who looked away. Which is why you believe he'd run." She paused. "And I think he's the only man in this who's been scared the whole time, and I don't know whether that makes him a victim or a killer."

She got into the car. The window came down.

"Did you tell Hollis about Voss?"

"No."

"Don't. Let him rest."

---

I didn't go up.

I walked out of the garage and across the lot and stood on the pavement in the dark. The wind had turned colder. Beyond the hospital fence, across a four-lane road, a liquor store had its lights on. A red neon bottle, a handwritten sign. SALE ON SCOTCH.

It was a short walk.

I knew how many steps. I had learned that the way you learn the steps up to a church: not on purpose, but by looking too many times from the top. After enough days a man stops thinking about it. At eighteen hundred and twenty he thinks about it exactly as often as he thinks about breathing, and then on a night like this it all comes up at once.

Voss had a hole in his side. Quill was in a drawer in Augusta. Hollis was in a bed with stitches in his scalp. Cora had been right: everyone who touched Alan's letter, everyone who got near Mara's boxes, ended up hurt or dead. And me, standing in front of a liquor store at seven fifty in the evening with my hands in my pockets, perfectly well.

*Nobody is that unlucky.*

My phone buzzed against my hip.

I took it out. An unknown number, a Maine area code. I stood in the cold and let it ring three times, the way I had let Hollis's ring that morning.

Then I answered.

Nothing. Wind, again. A car going by somewhere a long way off. The hum of a line held open by someone who couldn't decide to speak.

"Hello?"

A dry rasp. Air shaped like a name.

*"Theo."*

My whole body went still.

*"Don't tell Reyes where I am."* A breath that caught and scraped. *"They're going to say I did it."*
