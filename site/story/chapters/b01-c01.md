# Chapter 1: The Envelope

The rain had been coming sideways since four in the afternoon, and by nine it had found the one place in my office roof I'd been promising to fix for three years.

I moved the bucket six inches to the left and went back to my desk.

That's the job, mostly. Somebody's roof leaks, somebody's boat sinks, somebody's warehouse fills with smoke at two in the morning with nobody inside. Then somebody asks me to say whose fault it was, and how much it should cost, and whether the person asking for the money is telling the truth. My office is over a chandlery on Commercial Street in Portland, Maine. It smells like rope and wet dog. I'm good at what I do.

I'm good at it because I learned from the best. Hollis Dunn used to tell the new hires at Atlantic Mutual Casualty that every loss is a story somebody is telling you. *Your job isn't to believe it,* he'd say, stirring his tea, no sugar, never any sugar. *Your job is to find where it doesn't fit the water.*

I'd added a rule of my own in the years since. Nobody is that unlucky.

Not the man whose barn burns the same month his creditors call. Not the woman whose husband slips in the shower six weeks after the policy doubles. Bad luck is lumpy and random and it doesn't keep appointments. When a loss keeps appointments, somebody made the appointment.

My phone buzzed against the desk at 9:12. The screen said DESMOND REYES, and I let it ring twice, because Reyes was the vice president of claims at Atlantic Mutual and I'd learned that he valued people more when they didn't pick up on the first ring.

"Theo." He had the voice of a man who'd been born in a good suit. "You're working late."

"I'm working under a bucket."

"Still? I'll send you a roofer." He didn't laugh. He never did. "I need you on something. A death in Port Carrow, last night. Alan Farrow."

I didn't say anything. Outside, a gust leaned on the window and the glass answered.

"You remember the name," Reyes said.

"I remember the name."

"His wife, six years ago. You closed that one."

"Accidental drowning," I said. "She went over the side of their boat in March. Blood alcohol of point one eight. No sign of anyone else aboard."

"That's what the file says." A pause, very small, the kind you leave on purpose. "Now it's Alan. They found him this morning in the boathouse behind his house. The tide came up in last night's storm and the boathouse flooded. He was face down in four feet of water with the door locked from inside. The state police are calling it a misadventure, pending the medical examiner."

"And the policy?"

"Accidental death and dismemberment, four point two million. Increased a month ago from two. Sole beneficiary is a daughter, Cora, twenty-four."

I wrote *4.2* on my legal pad and underlined it, though I'd have remembered. "A month ago."

"I thought you'd like that."

"I like it fine. I like it so much I'm wondering why you're calling me instead of one of the staff adjusters."

"Because you wrote the first report." He let that sit. "If there's something in the first death, Theo, I want the man who looked at it to be the one who looks again. Quietly. I don't want a *pattern* in the Farrow family getting to a newspaper before it gets to my desk."

*Quietly.* Reyes had said *quietly* the way other men say *please*.

"Day rate, plus expenses," I said. "I need access to the original file. All of it."

"Done. It'll be in your inbox by morning. The Port Carrow causeway floods at high tide, so check the table. High water's eleven-forty tomorrow morning, just after midnight tomorrow night. Allow for the storm. Don't get stranded." Then, as if it had just occurred to him: "Hollis Dunn lives out there now, you know. Retired. You might look in on him."

"I know where Hollis lives."

"Of course you do."

He hung up. I sat there with the pen in my hand, listening to the bucket.

---

Lydia Farrow.

I hadn't thought about her in a long time. You don't forget the ones that went wrong. You forget the ones that went exactly the way the paperwork said.

I pulled my own memory open like a drawer and went through it: a gray March, a fifty-one-foot motor yacht named *Salt Ledger* moored off the Port Carrow breakwater, a woman in her late forties found two days later by a lobsterman. A husband in shock. A harbormaster who'd seen her aboard, alone, with a bottle, the evening before. A medical examiner's report. A file that closed neatly, the way a drawer does when everything's in the right place.

Under that was something I'd never given a name to. A pebble in a shoe I'd decided to keep walking on.

I'd been sober then. Six months, for the first time in my life. I'd thought the pebble was just me, a man who couldn't trust his own nerves.

I looked at the clock. 9:41.

It was October ninth. In five days it would be five years since my wife died.

I don't usually say that sentence to myself with the date attached. The date is a fence, and I've spent a long time learning to walk beside it without leaning. Mara went into the water off the Port Carrow breakwater on a night much like this one. The state called it an accident. I, who made a living out of not believing things, believed it.

I believed it because the alternative was a door I couldn't open. Also because I had no memory of that week to argue with.

That's the other thing I don't say. There are ten days missing out of my life, the ten days before Mara died. I know what I was doing in the general sense. Drinking. I'd gone back to it that autumn, hard and stupid, and when I surfaced, it was the fifteenth, I was in a hospital corridor in Rockport, and a state trooper was saying my name carefully, like it might spill.

One thousand eight hundred and twenty days since then. I count them. It's a stupid habit and it keeps me alive.

The buzzer downstairs went at 9:58.

---

Nobody rings the buzzer at ten at night in a storm unless they're delivering something or collecting something. I went down the back stairs with the flashlight, through the chandlery's dark aisles of shackles and coiled line, and opened the street door on the chain.

The man on the step was around seventy and soaked to the knee. He wore a dark overcoat that had been good thirty years ago and held a leather document case against his chest with both arms, the way you'd carry a child out of a fire.

"Mr. Theodore Brandt?"

"Who's asking?"

"Ferris Quill. Attorney." He tried to produce a card, found that the rain had dissolved it, and let it go. "I'm very sorry about the hour. I had instructions about the hour, in fact. I was to deliver this to you within twenty-four hours of a certain event, in person, and on no account to leave it with a third party." He studied my face. "You look the way she described you."

Something cold went through the middle of me that had nothing to do with the rain.

"Who described me?"

"Your wife, Mr. Brandt." He drew the case away from his chest, unbuckled it, and took out a long cream envelope with a worn crease across one corner. "Mara Brandt retained me in October, five years ago. She paid in advance. She gave me this, and a very specific instruction, and I have kept it in my safe since the week she died. This morning I read in the *Press Herald* that a man named Alan Farrow had been found dead, and I understood that my instruction had come due."

I took the envelope. I'd like to say my hand was steady.

My name was on the front. *Theo.* No surname, no address. The pen she'd used was a blue felt-tip she'd kept in the kitchen drawer. I could see the faint crimp at the end of the *o* where she always stopped, then pressed again, because she'd learned cursive from a nun who disapproved of lifted pens.

"What did she tell you?" My voice came out wrong. "When she gave it to you."

"Very little. That you would likely not believe it at first." Quill stepped back from the door. "She said you were the kind of man who needed to see the water before he'd believe it was wet. And she said I was to tell you one thing, if you asked about it." He frowned, as if reading a line off the inside of his own forehead. "'He'll want to call someone. Tell him not to.'"

"Call who?"

"She didn't say." The wind tore at his coat. "I'm sorry. I have done what I was asked. I'll be at my office on Exchange Street if you need me. I'd prefer it if you didn't come to my home."

"Mr. Quill—"

But he'd already turned, hunched over the empty case, and was walking back into the weather. I stood in the doorway until he was gone.

---

I carried it up the stairs and set it on the desk under the lamp, and I looked at it for what might have been two minutes or ten.

I read evidence for a living. Postmarks, signatures, the weight of paper, the age of a stain. I examined the envelope's edges, the flap, the seal. Then I opened it like I was opening a stranger's.

Inside were two things: a single sheet folded in thirds, and a small brass key on a loop of string. The key had a plastic tag on it, stamped with a number, 114, and the name of a self-storage outfit in Rockland.

I unfolded the sheet.

It was her handwriting, all of it. Blue felt-tip. Four paragraphs, the second half of the page cramped where she'd run out of room and refused to start another.

> *Theo,*
>
> *If you're reading this, then Alan Farrow is dead, and I'm either dead too or I've failed. I'm writing it down because I'm a coward about saying it aloud, and because I know you. You'll want to hear it from someone you trust, and I need you not to.*
>
> *Alan didn't kill Lydia alone. Nobody does anything like this alone. There are others, and they've been very, very careful, and they've used you, without your ever knowing, to make the careful look like luck. I'm sorry. I should have told you months ago. I tried on Sunday night and I lost my nerve halfway through.*
>
> *The key opens everything I have. Go to Rockland. Don't go to the police first, not the local ones, not the state, not yet. Read the Doyle file again, the cannery one, the one with no name on the closing page. You'll see it. You always see it, once you stop being polite.*
>
> *And Theo. Whatever you do, don't tell anyone you got this. Not a soul. Not even the people who love you. Especially them. I'm sorry. I love you. I always did, even in October.*

Under it, no signature. Just a small drawing she'd done on every note she ever left me. A wave, three lines, a little crest.

I read it three times. The first time like a husband. The second like an adjuster. The third like I was holding a sheet of glass that someone might drop.

Then I did what Hollis Dunn had taught me, because I didn't know what else to do. I stopped asking what it said and started asking what it was.

The paper was ordinary stationery, creamy, heavy, hers. The ink had the slight bronzing blue felt-tip gets with age. The folds were worn but unbroken. That told me very little about how long it had been inside the envelope. A water stain, small, at the lower right corner. I tilted it toward the lamp. A white crystalline rim marked where it had dried.

Salt, perhaps. Seawater would leave a mark like that. So would other things. I wanted it to tell me where she'd been. It couldn't.

I checked the envelope again. No postmark, of course. No date on the letter, either. Quill had said October, five years ago. The week she died. Everything more precise than that came from me.

*Sunday night,* she'd written. *I tried on Sunday night and I lost my nerve halfway through.*

Sunday. October the fourth. That was ten days before she died.

That was the first day of the ten I couldn't remember.

---

I got up and walked to the window. The street was black glass and rain. Beyond it, the harbor lights vanished and returned as the squalls passed.

*Tried to tell you on Sunday and lost my nerve halfway through.*

Halfway through what? I had no memory of Mara trying to tell me anything. I had no memory of Sunday at all. I'd spent five years assuming those days were blank because I'd drunk them into a blank. I'd been grateful for the mercy of it. I'd pictured bottles, rooms, hours lost. Never Mara trying to tell me something. Never myself answering.

I stood there until my breath fogged the window.

Then I went back to the desk and did the things a man does when his dead wife has asked him a favor.

I looked up the storage company in Rockland. Its website advertised twenty-four-hour access for tenants. I had a key and a unit number. I didn't have a gate code. I wrote down the office hours: nine to five.

Then I pulled the Port Carrow tide tables from the drawer where I keep everything that might kill me. High water was 11:40 the next morning, as Reyes had said. The causeway would close well before that and reopen after it, if the storm let it.

Rockland first. Then Port Carrow when the road was clear. I put the key beside my car keys. A route, two places, things I could do with my hands.

My hand went to the phone.

I knew who I'd call. I always called him. When the file didn't fit, when the room felt wrong, when the old fear came up out of the floor at three in the morning, I called Hollis Dunn, and he picked up on the second ring, every time, and said *Tell me where it doesn't fit the water.* He'd got me through five years of nights. He'd been at the funeral. He'd been at the meetings. He'd been, God help me, the closest thing I had to family.

*Not even the people who love you.*

I took my hand off the phone.

It was a small thing, and it cost me. I stood in that cold office with the bucket going *tink, tink, tink* and I felt the loneliness of it arrive, whole, the way weather arrives. Mara had asked me to carry this alone, and in all our years together she'd never asked me to carry anything alone. She'd always taken the other handle.

*Especially them.*

I told myself it meant nothing. A frightened woman, a bad week. A note written by somebody who might not have been thinking clearly, who had maybe been wrong about everything.

Then I pulled up the file on Lydia Farrow, the one Reyes had said would be in my inbox by morning, and found that it was there already.

It had arrived at 9:12.

The same minute he called.

I sat looking at the timestamp and thought about men who say *quietly,* and a file sent before I'd agreed to take the job.

I opened it.

It was thirty-one pages, my name on every page. The first six were as I remembered them: the boat, the lobsterman, the harbormaster's statement, the blood alcohol. Clean and orderly. I'd been proud of them.

Page seven was a one-page addendum, a supplementary finding, typed in a blocky, slightly smudged font that I'd never once used in my life. It summarized an interview with the victim's husband. It concluded, in language I could have recited in my sleep, *no evidence of third-party involvement.*

At the bottom was my signature. The same crowded capitals, the same drag of blue-black ink below the line. Exactly where I'd have put it.

I'd been sober when I worked that claim. I'd interviewed Alan. I'd signed a report. All that I remembered.

I had never seen that page before in my life.

And I had no way, none, to be sure that was true.
