# Chapter 21: The Neck Road

They don't let you near a tin like that.

I wanted to be near it. I wanted to take the duct tape off with my own thumbnail and see what a man who loved me had thought I'd hidden. Nell stood between me and the sink with her arm out like a crossing guard, and a state police evidence tech named Obert, who had been dragged out of bed in Augusta at half past three and drove two hours without complaint, photographed it from every side before anybody breathed on it.

"Tape first," Obert said. "Then the tin. Then I open it. Detective, you're on camera. Mr. Brandt, you're a civilian who is not in this room."

"I'm standing in it."

"You're standing in it very quietly," said Obert, and went to work.

It took twenty minutes. Nell did not speak. The duct tape went into one bag, the tin into another. The lid came off with a small tired pop, like a jar of jam.

Inside, on a folded dish towel from the church kitchen's own drawer: six brass evidence tags, the old kind, stamped T. BRANDT. A pharmacy bottle with the label scraped off, empty, smelling faintly of nothing. And one sheet of paper, typed, in the stiff dated Courier I'd learned to see in my sleep.

I read it over Obert's shoulder.

*I did not mean for her to drown. She was going to ruin us both. I gave her something to make her calm and I thought the water would do the rest. I am sorry. T.B.*

No date. A signature, blue-black, that looked like mine the way a mask looks like a face.

"The kit," I said. "The tags from my Portland office."

"Six. You said the kit had a dozen." Nell was reading it too. She had the very still look of a woman who is angry in a way she has decided to use later. "Obert. Chain."

"Custody opens here, at 5:21 a.m., church hall kitchen, with me and Detective Castellano, on video." He didn't look up. "Nobody calls it evidence of anything until the lab tells me what it is. A bottle with no label is a bottle. Paper is paper."

"It's a confession," I said.

"It's a typed sheet of paper that says a thing," said Nell. "It isn't a confession until somebody can say it came out of your hand. And that is what it was for, Theo. Not to find. To be found." She straightened up. "They'd have had a trooper find it at six, with you standing there, saying you'd remembered. Mr. Brandt, who didn't know he'd done it, pulling his own sin out of a biscuit tin."

"And Hollis tells the world he was heartbroken," I said. "He'd have said I fooled him."

"He'd have said *I was afraid for him.*"

She turned to the hall door, where Kessler, back on his feet and shaking out a bruised rib, had come to say that the man in the gray jacket had gone down the bank behind Ash Street and into a car with no plates. And that the car was now somewhere between here and the Rockland road, with a head start and a hole in somebody's ribs.

"Dill?"

"Rockland General. He says to tell you he didn't drop the gun."

Nell's radio clicked against her coat. The hospital officer had just called in: Hollis's room had been empty since a nurse sent the guard to check a fire alarm on the floor below. The ward camera showed him leaving through the service stairwell in a borrowed hospital gown. The alarm had been false. By the time anyone checked the stairwell, he was gone.

"Tell him he's getting a medal and a lecture." She took out her phone. "And I want every door out of Port Carrow."

"He's not going out," I said.

She stopped.

"Hollis. He won't try to leave. He hasn't got a car, he hasn't got a wallet, and he's seventy percent bruise. He's gone where he always goes." My mouth was dry. "The breakwater. He's been going every year with a thermos."

"It's the nineteenth."

"He'll go anyway. Because I'd know to look." I heard myself say it. "Because he wants me to."

Nell looked at me for a long moment. The rain was still coming through the open side door and the sky outside it had gone the gray of old pewter.

"Say it properly," she said. "Not as a thing you feel. As a thing you can stand behind."

"I can't prove it."

"I know you can't."

"I think he's waiting for me," I said. "I think he's done everything else he can. He's been *kind* for so long, Nell, I don't think he knows another way to end it."

---

My phone rang at 6:48.

We were in the Ford on the Neck road shoulder, a mile from town, watching the sky bruise from the east. The northeaster had arrived early. It was not yet the storm. It was the front edge of one, a wind that came in flat from the open water and pushed at the car like a hand. In the mirror I could see the long dark spit of the Neck, the single road running out to the breakwater between marsh and sea. The tide was making. On the marsh side the cordgrass had gone silver and flat, and the first water was creeping through it in thin bright threads.

Unknown number. I held it up so Nell could see.

"Let him say what he says," she said. "Speaker."

"Dear boy," said Hollis.

His voice was exactly the same. That was what undid me. A voice you've listened to for twenty years doesn't change because you've learned what it is.

"Where are you?" I said.

"Oh, you know where." A small cough, the sound of wind across a handset. "I borrowed a coat. Somebody's husband left it in a closet on the fourth floor. It's much too large, and it smells of liniment. Theo, I don't want to be unkind to anybody. I'd like to talk to you, and I'd like to do it before the tide turns the road into a rumor. Alone."

"No."

"Then I shall be forced to say rather more over the telephone, and I believe that would distress Detective Castellano, who is certainly sitting next to you with that nice little recorder." He sounded fond. "Come as you are. Leave the car at the gate. I'll wait at the end of the road with my thermos, the same as always. Bring nothing but yourself, and you may tell her to stay where she likes. She'll never reach us in time to hear the part that matters."

"What's the part that matters?"

"Why, what you did," said Hollis. "Don't you want to know? Five years you've wondered. I'm the only one who can tell you."

The line went to wind and then to nothing.

Nell took the phone out of my hand, stopped the recording, and breathed out through her nose.

"You're not going."

"I'm going."

"He's got a gun, Theo. He's got a man with a gun and he's got a seventy-year-old with nothing left to lose standing at the end of a causeway."

"He hasn't got a gun." I did not know how I knew it. "He never has. It's not what he does."

"What does he do?"

"He listens," I said. "He waits until you tell him."

She said something quite short to the windshield. Then she got out of the car and opened the trunk and came back with a vest and a cheap black clip the size of a cuff button.

"Under your collar," she said. "It records, and it also sends. I'll be on the far side of the first culvert with Kessler, and I will hear every word you say, and the second I don't like it, we're coming. I'll cut the grass if I have to."

"Tide's making."

"I can swim."

"It's forty-eight degrees."

"I'm from Millinocket," said Nell. "I don't know what that means."

I laughed. It came out of me like something I'd swallowed wrong. She buttoned my collar over the clip with her own two cold fingers, and then, without being asked, with the same fingers, she held the back of my neck for the space of one breath, which was the only time in nine days anyone had done that.

"You remember what you're there for," she said.

"A thing I can stand behind."

"And if he's lying?"

"He won't be," I said. "That's the part I'm afraid of."

---

The Neck road ran a mile and a quarter from the last house to the breakwater. A single lane of cracked blacktop between a stone wall on the sea side and the marsh on the other, built on a ridge of old rubble, with a hand-lettered sign at the head of it that said TIDES. NO PARKING ON ROAD. Somebody had added, in marker: *THIS MEANS YOU, CHARLIE.* At the full moon the water crossed the road in two places, and on the king tides of autumn, if the wind was right, it crossed in four.

Today the wind was right.

By seven-thirty the first culvert was running a thin brown stream across the blacktop, an inch deep and rising. I walked through it with my shoes in my hand, and I did not feel the cold at all, and that frightened me more than any of it. Behind me the Ford had shrunk to a toy. Ahead the road went out and out, with the light coming up pewter and pink over the sea, and at its very end the long black snout of the breakwater, and on the snout a man.

He was sitting on the capstone, in a coat that was not his, with the hood down.

He had a thermos beside him and a second cup upturned on top of it. He lifted a hand as I came, a small gesture, the one he'd used for twenty years across crowded rooms to say *here, there you are.*

I stopped at fifteen feet.

"That's far enough?" he said. "I'll admit I'm hurt."

"Is the man at the church yours?"

"Dear boy." He smiled with half his face. The swollen eye had gone the color of an eggplant. "I am only a retired claims director with a bad hip. The gentleman at the church belongs to someone who regards me as a leak. You've heard of those. Water finds its way."

"You called him."

"I called him, yes. I told him where to be and when. It seemed the only way to be sure you'd be found." He poured tea into the cup. Yorkshire Gold; I could smell the stewed bite of it even in that wind. "Don't make the face. I was frightened, Theo. For the first time in nine years I was frightened. I saw you across that room with your jaw set, telling me about a tin, and I thought, *he's lying to me.* I had never thought it before. Not once. It was like a stair that wasn't there."

"You said *I'm so glad you're remembering.*"

"I was." The cup trembled slightly. "That was true. That was the most terrible thing in the room. I wanted you to remember so much."

The wind pushed at us both. A wave came up the stone and fell back in a long hiss, throwing spray across my ankles.

"Say it," he said gently. "You've been rehearsing. I can see it on you. Say what you came to say."

I'd said it to myself on the stairs. In the dark in the Ford. In the hospital in the middle of the night, to a ceiling. I had never once said it into a face.

"At one-twelve in the morning on the fifth of October," I said, "I stood on the back porch of my house in my socks, and I rang you."

He didn't move.

"You picked up on the second ring. You were awake. You said, *Theo, what's happened.* And I told you. All of it. Four files. My name on three. Mara, with her copies. The ethics line." My voice had gone very flat and very clear, like a sound heard underwater. "And you listened to every word, and when I'd finished you said, *Let me talk to her.*"

"I did."

"I called you," I said. "I called *you.*"

It was louder than I'd meant. It went out over the water and was taken.

"You did," said Hollis. "You were so brave about it. You wouldn't have called anyone else. You'd been sober a hundred and some days, and you needed to be told what to do, and I was the one who'd always told you."

"She was alive for nine more days."

"Yes."

"You went to the breakwater."

"I did."

"She trusted you."

"She *adored* me," said Hollis, with an odd exactness, as if correcting a minute. "She thought I was your father, in a way. She came with her coat buttoned wrong, and she'd made a flask of coffee, because it was raining and she thought I'd be cold." For a moment I thought he might weep. His face did something I could not read, and then it smoothed itself over like a bed. "It was lorazepam in the flask. Not much. Enough to loosen the hands. She said, *Hollis, I feel funny,* and I said, *Sit down, dear, sit down.* And she said your name."

"Stop."

"She said it as if it were a place," said Hollis. "I told you that once. I told you in my kitchen, three days after you came out of that hospital, when you asked what she said at the end. I said, *She said your name as if it were a place.* You cried so. I thought, well. He'll have that, at least."

I couldn't feel my hands. I looked down at them to be sure I had them.

"Why?"

"Why did I kill her, or why did I keep you?"

"Both."

"The first is arithmetic. Four files, a pattern, an examiner who couldn't let go. I had nine years of work in her copies and a state police detective with a conscience on her phone list." He turned the cup in his fingers. "The second is harder, and I'd like you to believe it, though I know why you won't. I kept you because I was afraid you'd remember, and I couldn't have borne you dying of it. Don't misunderstand. If you'd remembered by the winter, I'd have found a way. But you didn't. You got sober, and you thanked me, and every Tuesday you made me tea in that terrible kitchen. And I found that I'd been waiting five years to be thanked by someone I could stand."

"You used me," I said. "You took my name, you took my reports, you rewrote page seven. You let me close Lydia. You let me close *Alan.*"

"I let you be good at your work." He lifted his chin. "I taught you to be good at your work. It was the finest thing I ever did, Theo, and it was the *only* thing I did that wasn't for money. You think the others were tricks? Alan Farrow wept in that boathouse. He said, *Hollis, I can't carry it.* I said, *Then let me carry it for you.* I only ever listened better than you."

The sea came up the stone. I don't know how long we stood there. The road behind me made a sound I hadn't heard before, a long liquid seethe, as the water crossed the second culvert and kept on coming.

"Alan's whisky," I said. "The line and the eye hook. The latch."

"Fishing line. A turn round the cleat." He shrugged. "A little trick my father taught me, for a boat's cabin door. Ruth Anselm I did myself, on the cliff. Peter Doyle, too. I'm not proud. I'm *thorough.*" He sipped. "You'll say it to Nell. She's listening, isn't she? Good. Detective, I hope you've written it all down."

My collar was wet. The little clip was cold against my throat.

"She hears you," I said.

"Splendid. And you needn't look for the man with the broad jaw, dear. He'll go where he was told to go." Hollis smiled. "The rest is somebody else's problem."

"There's one more thing," he said, "and then I'll let you take me in. It's a thing I owe you."

"I don't want anything you owe."

"Mara's box. October. I took it from the unit myself, with her code, the night you opened it, because I couldn't let you read what she'd put in. I've been carrying it ever since." His hand went into the borrowed coat. "It isn't evidence. It's *hers.* A letter to you, written the morning she died. I've read it four times. I thought you ought to have it before—"

He stopped.

He was looking past me.

I turned. The road was gone.

I mean that the way you mean it about a face in the dark. A hundred yards behind me, where the last culvert had been, a long smooth tongue of brown water was running clean across the blacktop from marsh to sea, carrying a sheet of foam and a plastic bucket. Beyond it, small and bright through the rain, a pair of headlights sat on the far shoulder.

They weren't the Ford's.

They were on high beam, and they were not moving, and as I watched them I saw a figure get out of the car. It stood a moment in the glare, then walked slowly down to the water's edge and raised something to its shoulder, a long thing, a thing that I'd seen in a hundred claim photographs and never in a hand.

"Ah," said Hollis softly. He closed his fingers on whatever was in the coat. "I did mention one mistake, didn't I? I told someone where you'd be."

Then the wind dropped, for a breath, as though the sea were drawing in air.

"Theo," said Hollis. "Get down."
